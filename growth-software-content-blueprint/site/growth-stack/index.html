What is a growth stack, and what does it need to cover?

A growth stack is the connected set of software behind acquisition, conversion, retention and measurement, plus the operational tools that keep the work moving. For a lean team it usually means a small number of paid products that share contact and revenue data, not one tool per task.

The connections matter more than the individual products. A lead that arrives through search, fills in a form, receives a nurture sequence and becomes a customer should be traceable as one record. When each tool keeps its own copy of the contact, reporting breaks and teams argue about which number is right.

This publication covers the stack in 15 categories. Each category hub explains its boundaries, the criteria that matter and every buying guide we publish for it.

Which software categories sit at each stage of the funnel?

Acquisition tools bring people in, conversion tools turn interest into pipeline or orders, retention tools keep customers, measurement tools show what worked, and operations tools run the team. Some categories straddle two stages, and we've placed each where most buyers start the search.

Acquisition: getting found and getting in front of buyers

  • SEO software covers keyword research, technical audits, rank tracking and organic reporting.
  • Paid media software handles running, governing and measuring ad spend across networks.
  • Partnership growth software manages affiliate, influencer, referral and channel programs.
  • Webinar, event and community software supports demand generation through live and owned audiences.
  • Email marketing and outreach software spans permission-based campaigns and responsible B2B outbound, and it reaches into retention too.

Conversion: turning attention into pipeline and revenue

  • CRM and lead generation software takes you from prospect data to a qualified pipeline.
  • Website experience software covers builders, CMS platforms, forms, performance and conversion infrastructure.
  • Ecommerce growth software covers storefront, merchandising, checkout and store-side retention tools.

Retention: keeping and expanding customers

  • Customer retention software covers onboarding, support, feedback and customer success.
  • Product-led growth software covers product analytics, in-app onboarding and experimentation for software companies.

Measurement: knowing what worked

  • Marketing automation and analytics software brings workflows, measurement, CRO and distribution together.
  • Data and RevOps software connects marketing activity to accounts and revenue.

Operations: producing and delivering the work

  • AI content software covers writing, creative and content production tools for marketing teams.
  • Content operations software handles planning, workflow, governance and distribution of content.
  • Agency operations software runs projects, clients, resourcing, proposals and billing.

In what order should you buy growth software?

Buy the system of record first, usually a CRM or an email platform with a contact database, and add acquisition and measurement tools that feed it. Buying acquisition tools before you have somewhere to capture and follow up on leads wastes the traffic they generate.

A sensible order for most small B2B teams looks like this, though your own bottleneck should override it:

  1. Capture: a website with working forms and a CRM or email platform that stores every contact.
  2. Follow-up: basic email automation so new leads get a response without manual effort.
  3. Acquisition: one primary channel, such as SEO, paid media or outbound, chosen by where your buyers already look.
  4. Measurement: analytics that ties channels to pipeline, set up before you spend on a second channel.
  5. Scale: specialist tools, extra channels and operations software once a process is repeatable.

Ecommerce teams follow a similar pattern with the store platform as the system of record, and software companies often add product analytics earlier than other businesses do.

What criteria apply to every category in the stack?

Four criteria apply everywhere: how the price grows with your usage, how cleanly data moves between tools, what you can export if you leave, and who on your team will actually operate the product. Feature lists come after those, because mature tools in a category often share the core features.

  • Pricing unit. Contacts, seats, credits, mailboxes, tracked keywords and sessions each grow at different rates. Price your twelve-month workload, not today's.
  • Integration depth. A listed integration may sync only contacts, not events or revenue. Check which fields move, and in which direction.
  • Exit cost. History, automations and templates are often the hardest things to migrate. Ask before you sign an annual contract.
  • Operator fit. A powerful tool nobody has time to configure does less than a simpler one used every day.

What mistakes cost lean teams the most?

The most expensive mistake is paying twice for the same capability, such as a suite's reporting module and a separate reporting tool, or a CRM's email feature and a full email platform. The second is buying for the team you plan to have rather than the workload you have now.

Three other traps are common in published vendor pricing structures: annual commitments that lock in seats before adoption is proven, contact-based billing that keeps charging for inactive records, and add-ons that turn an affordable entry plan into a much larger invoice. How many tools does a small growth team need?

Usually fewer than you expect. A CRM or email platform, a website with forms, one acquisition tool and analytics cover the essentials, and every addition should solve a measured bottleneck.

Is an all-in-one platform better than separate tools?

An all-in-one platform simplifies data and billing, while separate tools let you pick the strongest option for each job. Choose all-in-one when integration effort is your bottleneck, and separate tools when one job needs depth the suite lacks.

How often should a growth stack be reviewed?

Review it at least once a year and before any annual renewal. Check usage, overlapping features and whether each tool still maps to a job someone does every month.
