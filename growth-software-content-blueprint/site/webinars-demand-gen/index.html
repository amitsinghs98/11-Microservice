Where should you start if webinars are your main demand channel?

Start with our pillar guide to the best webinar platforms for B2B lead generation, which compares eight platforms by buyer type, pricing model and CRM handoff. It also explains when a webinar tool stops being enough and which specialist guide to read next.

Most B2B teams enter this category through webinars because they're the cheapest format to run well: one stage, one audience, one call to action. Everything else here, from conference registration to community analytics, is easier to judge once you know how your webinar data reaches sales.

What jobs does webinar and event software do for demand generation?

It turns live and recorded expertise into registrations, engaged attendees and follow-up that sales can act on. The software matters less than whether registration, attendance and engagement data reach your CRM quickly and in a usable shape.

The cluster covers four formats. Webinars span live hosting, automated and on-demand delivery, engagement add-ons and analytics. Events cover virtual, hybrid and in-person conferences, from registration through networking to lead capture on the show floor. Audio and education covers podcast hosting, production and guest booking, plus course platforms used as lead magnets. Communities cover hosted member spaces and the analytics that show whether they're working.

Each format suits a different buyer stage and a different payback period. A webinar can gather a defined audience in an hour; a community takes months to build and pays back over years, so the content syndication vs webinars comparison is worth reading before you commit budget to any single channel.

How do the subcategories fit together?

Think of the category as three layers: formats that gather an audience, tools that capture and move the data, and channels that keep the audience warm between moments. Most stacks need one tool from the first layer, one integration path from the second, and one always-on channel from the third.

Formats create the moment people register for, capture tools move what they do into your systems, and always-on channels keep earning attention after the live date and feed the next event's audience.

Which criteria matter most when choosing webinar and event tools?

Judge the attendee experience and the data handoff together: a polished event that leaves registrations stuck in a spreadsheet wastes most of its value. Then check staffing and cost, because these tools are commonly priced by registrants, attendees, hosts, organizers, events or audience size, and production effort can cost more than the license itself.

The questions we apply across the cluster:

  1. Does registration, attendance and engagement data sync to your CRM or automation platform, and how fast?
  2. Can attendees join without friction on the devices and networks your audience uses?
  3. Who runs production on the day, and what does the tool expect of them?
  4. Can recordings live on as gated or ungated on-demand content you can measure?
  5. Does interaction data (polls, questions, booth visits, meetings) carry through to follow-up and scoring?
  6. Which pricing meter does the tool use, and is it the number your program will grow fastest?
  7. For communities and podcasts, who owns the audience, the feed and the content if you change platform?

How do you shortlist event software without overbuying?

Buy for the next twelve months of events you'll actually run, not the flagship conference you might run one day. A team running a monthly webinar needs dependable hosting and a clean CRM sync long before it needs sponsor booths or matchmaking.

Map the full funnel first using the webinar funnel guide, which runs from topic and registration to replay and sales handoff, then pick tools to fill the gaps it exposes. Teams planning recurring evergreen sessions should compare webinar automation tools with standard hosting before paying for both; teams that go on to run multi-day events can graduate to virtual event platforms.

A practical sequence for any subcategory:

  1. Write down the formats, frequency and expected audience for the next year.
  2. Name the system every lead must reach and the fields sales needs on arrival.
  3. Cut any tool whose pricing meter scales with the number you expect to grow fastest.
  4. Keep three candidates, run a tagged test registration through each trial, and check what lands in the CRM.
  5. Ask each vendor what counts as an event, an attendee and a host before you sign.

What does a typical webinar and event stack look like by team size?

Small teams need one dependable webinar tool wired into the CRM; the extra layers arrive as event volume and headcount grow. These setups are editorial patterns, not prescriptions, so adjust them to the formats you actually run.

Solo marketer or founder-led team

One webinar platform with native CRM or automation sync, recordings reused as on-demand content, and perhaps a podcast if a founder enjoys interviews. Skip event platforms and community software until you have a steady audience to put in them.

Small demand gen team of two to five people

A webinar platform plus an engagement or analytics layer, an automated or on-demand library for evergreen sessions, and a written handoff rule with sales. Teams that attend trade shows add an exhibitor lead capture app; teams with a content-led brand may add a podcast host and production tool.

Mid-market marketing team

A webinar platform and a virtual or hybrid event platform, often from the same vendor, with registration, networking and lead capture for one or two flagship events a year. Event integrations feed scoring in marketing automation, and a community or course program starts to carry the audience between events.

Enterprise field and digital events team

Separate tools for webinars, conferences, registration and onsite lead capture, joined by a deliberate integration layer and shared reporting on pipeline influence. Community and education programs usually have their own owners, so governance and data ownership matter as much as features.

What mistakes do teams make when building an event and community stack?

The costliest mistake is buying for the format and ignoring the handoff, so registrations pile up where sales never sees them. The next is adding formats faster than the team can staff them, which leaves expensive platforms running half-empty programs.

  • Buying event software for webinar work. Multi-track features add cost and setup that a monthly series doesn't need.
  • Measuring attendance instead of outcomes. Registrants and members are inputs; meetings, opportunities and retention are the outcomes to report.
  • Letting recordings die. A session that isn't republished on demand has done half its job.
  • Launching a community without a reason to return. Members need events, answers or peers they can't get elsewhere.
  • Losing ownership. Check exports for registrant lists, podcast feeds and community content before you commit.
  • Leaving follow-up undefined. Decide who calls whom, on which signal and how fast, before the first event.

What else do demand gen teams ask about webinar and event software?

These are the questions that tend to surface while planning a first event program or rethinking one that isn't producing pipeline. Each answer leads into a linked guide.

Do we need a virtual event platform or is a webinar tool enough?

For single-session talks with Q&A, a webinar tool is usually enough. A virtual event platform earns its cost when you run multiple tracks, sponsors or attendee networking.

How soon should event leads reach sales?

As soon as the data is clean enough to act on, ideally within a day or two while the session is still fresh. Set the routing rules and follow-up owners before the event, not after it.

Are on-demand webinars worth gating?

It depends on what the recording is for. Gating collects contact data but cuts views, so many teams gate deep-dive sessions and leave short or top-of-funnel recordings open.

How do we know if a B2B community is working?

Look at activation, repeat contribution and member retention, then connect them to outcomes such as support deflection, renewals or pipeline influence. Raw post or member counts say little on their own.

Should podcasts and courses sit with the events team?

They often do in smaller companies, because the same people book speakers and produce content. Larger teams usually give them separate owners but share one audience and one reporting view.

How many tools does a first event program need?

Usually one: a webinar platform that syncs cleanly with your CRM or marketing automation. Add engagement, analytics or event tools only when a specific gap in the funnel shows up.
