Which onboarding tool should a SaaS company choose?

Choose by how complex your implementations are and who on the customer side has to do work. Multi-week implementations with data migration need a project-style tool with customer tasks and dependencies, while a self-serve product with a short setup needs a checklist and in-app guidance more than a project plan.

  • Structured implementations with professional services and resourcing: Rocketlane.
  • Implementation teams that want customer task accountability and timeline tracking: GUIDEcx.
  • HubSpot-centered teams that want onboarding plans tied to deals: Arrows.
  • Sales-to-CS handoffs that start in a shared deal room: Dock.
  • Customer-facing checklists with a simple portal: OnRamp.
  • Internal handoffs only, on a tool your team already owns: Asana or a similar project tool.
  • In-product setup steps and checklists: Appcues.

Which part of onboarding are you actually trying to fix?

Onboarding has two halves that often get blurred: the internal handoff from sales to the onboarding team, and the customer-facing journey to first value. Onboardings often stall at the handoff, when context about goals, stakeholders and promised features never reaches the person running kickoff.

Separate the two before you shortlist:

  • Internal handoff: a handoff form, owner assignment, a kickoff date and the account's stated goals, ideally created automatically when a deal closes in the CRM.
  • Customer-facing plan: milestones the customer can see, tasks assigned to their people, reminders, file uploads and a single link instead of a thread of emails.
  • In-product activation: setup checklists, tooltips and tours inside the app. That's a different category; our roundup of product tour and user onboarding software covers it in depth.

If your product is complex enough that users need guidance after go-live, a digital adoption platform may matter more than an implementation tool.

What does each tool do well, and where does it fall short?

The first five tools are built for the customer-facing implementation, Asana covers internal coordination, and Appcues covers in-app activation. Teams with complex onboarding often pair one tool from the first group with one from the last.

Rocketlane: best for structured implementations

Rocketlane is built for customer onboarding and professional services, combining project plans, templates, a customer portal, time tracking and resource management. It suits SaaS companies whose onboarding looks like a consulting engagement, with billable hours or dedicated implementation managers.

Limitations: the professional-services depth is overhead if your onboarding is a two-call setup. Skip it if you don't plan resources or track implementation effort. See Rocketlane's pricing page for current tiers.

GUIDEcx: best for customer task accountability

GUIDEcx focuses on the customer's side of the work: assigning tasks to customer stakeholders, sending reminders and showing where a project is blocked and by whom. That visibility helps when timelines slip because a customer's IT team hasn't delivered credentials or data.

Limitations: customers need to adopt yet another interface, so reminders and email-based task completion matter. Skip it if your customers rarely have tasks of their own. See GUIDEcx's official site.

Arrows: best for HubSpot-centered teams

Arrows builds shared onboarding plans that connect to HubSpot deals and records, so a plan can start the moment a deal closes and progress shows up in the CRM. For a HubSpot shop, that closes the handoff gap without another database.

Limitations: its value is tied to HubSpot. Skip it if you run Salesforce or another CRM. See Arrows' official site.

Dock: best for handoffs that start in the sales cycle

Dock offers customer-facing workspaces used for sales deal rooms and onboarding plans, so the page a buyer saw during evaluation can turn into the onboarding hub after signature. That continuity carries goals and stakeholders across the handoff.

Limitations: project management is lighter than in implementation-focused tools. Skip it if your onboarding needs dependencies, resourcing and time tracking. See Dock's official site.

OnRamp: best for customer-facing checklists

OnRamp centers on a customer onboarding portal with checklists, content and progress tracking, aimed at making the customer's next step obvious. It suits teams whose onboarding is a repeatable sequence rather than a bespoke project.

Limitations: less suited to resource planning across many concurrent implementations. Skip it if you need professional-services management. See OnRamp's official site.

Asana: best when the problem is internal coordination

A general project tool such as Asana handles the internal side well: a handoff template, owners, due dates and a CRM-triggered project. If your customers don't need to see the plan, this may be all you need.

Limitations: no purpose-built customer portal, and inviting customers as guests gets messy. Skip it if customer-side tasks are where your onboardings stall. Compare tiers on Asana's pricing page.

Appcues: best for the in-product half of onboarding

Appcues provides no-code checklists, tours, tooltips and modals inside your product, triggered by user attributes and behavior. It handles activation after accounts are set up, which an implementation tool can't reach.

Limitations: it doesn't manage the implementation project or customer stakeholders. Skip it if your onboarding is mostly services-led configuration. See Appcues' pricing page.

What does a 30-day onboarding journey look like for a small B2B customer?

A good 30-day journey has one goal per week and ends with the customer having done the thing they bought the product for, not just having logins. The example below is hypothetical, for a small B2B customer with one admin and a handful of end users; adapt the milestones to your own definition of first value.

  1. Day 0 (contract signed): the CRM creates the onboarding plan from a template, assigns an owner and sends the internal handoff form. Sales records the customer's top goal, success metric, stakeholders and anything promised during the deal.
  2. Days 1–3 (welcome and kickoff): the customer receives one link to their plan. Kickoff confirms the goal, the admin, the go-live date and the customer-side tasks.
  3. Days 4–10 (technical setup): the admin completes account setup, SSO or user import, and the key integration. Reminders fire automatically when a customer task is overdue; the onboarding owner escalates after a second miss.
  4. Days 11–17 (configuration and data): the team configures the product around the customer's first use case and loads real data, not samples.
  5. Days 18–24 (training and launch): admin training, end-user invitations and in-app checklists guide first actions. Short courses from a customer education platform can replace repeated live sessions.
  6. Days 25–30 (first value and handover): confirm the success metric has moved or the first workflow has run, record it, and hand the account to its CSM with notes. The account then appears in the customer success software your CSMs use with an onboarding-complete milestone.

Track two measures for every onboarding: days to first value and the share of customer tasks completed on time. Both reveal where the journey stalls far better than a satisfaction survey at day 30.

Will customers actually use an onboarding portal?

Customers use a portal when it saves them effort, such as one link with their tasks and status, and ignore it when it's another login on top of email. Reminders and lightweight access matter for this reason, so test the customer's experience, not just your team's.

What mistakes do teams make with onboarding software?

The biggest mistake is automating a journey that was never defined, so the tool faithfully reproduces a messy process. The second is treating onboarding software as a replacement for a clear first-value milestone.

  • One template for every customer. Keep two or three templates by segment instead of a single long plan.
  • No handoff fields. If sales can close a deal without recording goals and promises, the best portal can't recover them.
  • Too many customer tasks. Every task you assign to the customer is a place the timeline can slip.
  • Confusing client onboarding with customer onboarding. Agencies onboarding retainer clients have different needs; see client onboarding software for digital agencies.

What else do buyers ask about customer onboarding software?

The remaining questions are mostly about overlap with existing tools and how to measure success. Short answers follow.

Do we need onboarding software if we already have a project management tool?

Not always. If customers never need to see the plan or complete tasks, a project tool with a good template covers the internal handoff; dedicated software earns its cost when customer-side tasks are what delay go-live.

What's the difference between customer onboarding software and a product tour tool?

Customer onboarding software manages the implementation project between your team and the customer's team. A product tour tool guides individual users inside the app once the account is set up.

How should we measure onboarding success?

Measure days from contract to a defined first-value milestone, and the on-time completion rate of tasks on both sides. Review stalled onboardings weekly to find the step that breaks most often.

Who should own onboarding in a small SaaS company?

One named person per account, usually an onboarding specialist or the CSM, with sales accountable for a complete handoff. Shared ownership tends to mean nobody chases overdue customer tasks.
