Best User Research Interview Platforms for Product Teams

User research interview platforms split into three jobs: recruiting and scheduling participants, running and recording sessions, and storing what you learned in a searchable repository. User Interviews and Respondent lead on recruiting, Great Question bundles all three for teams talking to their own customers, Lookback handles sessions, and Dovetail or Condens hold the synthesis.

Which user research interview platform should you choose?

If your hardest problem is finding the right people, start with a recruiting platform (User Interviews, or Respondent for B2B professionals); if you mostly interview your own customers, Great Question covers recruiting, scheduling and a repository in one product. Add Lookback for moderated session recording, and Dovetail or Condens when findings need to live beyond a single study.

  • User Interviews: a good fit for teams that need external participants across consumer and B2B audiences, plus a hub for their own panel.
  • Respondent: a good fit for B2B research with hard-to-reach professionals.
  • Great Question: a good fit for product teams that research their own customer base and want CRM, scheduling and repository together.
  • Lookback: a good fit for live moderated interviews that colleagues observe.
  • Dovetail: a good fit for organizations building a company-wide research repository.
  • Condens: a good fit for smaller research teams that want a repository priced by contributor.

How is a research interview platform different from video meeting software?

Video meeting software records a conversation; a research platform manages everything around it, from screening and consent to tagging and reuse. The distinction matters because most of the cost and risk in interview research sits outside the call itself.

Zoom, Google Meet and Teams are fine places to hold a conversation, and repositories can usually import their recordings. What they don't do is check that a participant matches your screener, stop you from contacting the same customer three times in a month, store consent alongside the recording, or let a colleague search every interview mentioning a competitor next quarter. If a team only runs a few calls a quarter, meeting software plus a shared folder may be enough. Once research becomes continuous, the gaps show.

For in-product feedback rather than conversations, see voice of customer software. For task-based sessions, see usability testing software.

What does each platform cover across recruiting, sessions and synthesis?

Each block notes the job a platform does best and what you'd need to add. Strengths are documented features; fit judgments are our editorial assessment.

User Interviews: best for external recruiting at scale

User Interviews recruits participants with screeners and scheduling, and its Research Hub manages your own participant panel. Recruiting is priced per session, with incentives as a separate cost, according to the User Interviews pricing page.

Limitations: per-session costs add up for continuous research, and it doesn't replace a repository. Skip it if you only interview existing customers you can reach directly.

Respondent: best for B2B professionals

Respondent focuses on recruiting professionals for moderated and unmoderated studies. Its pricing page describes fees per completed session, with incentives as a separate cost.

Limitations: it's a recruiting service, so sessions and synthesis need other tools. Skip it if your participants are consumers or your own users.

Great Question: best for researching your own customers

Great Question positions itself as one product for a research CRM, scheduling, interviews and a repository. Its pricing page describes per-seat plans; check there which features and integrations each plan includes.

Limitations: plan inclusions such as SSO couldn't be confirmed during drafting, so check them before you buy. Skip it if you only need a repository for recordings you already have.

Lookback: best for moderated live interviews

Lookback is built for live moderated sessions that colleagues can observe, and it also supports unmoderated sessions. Plan structure couldn't be confirmed during drafting, so check Lookback's pricing page.

Limitations: synthesis and cross-study search are lighter than in a dedicated repository. Skip it if your interviews already run well on Zoom and the gap is analysis.

Dovetail: best for a company-wide repository

Dovetail is a research repository that imports and transcribes recordings, per its transcription documentation, and supports tagging and highlights. Its help center explains how paid plans are bought; confirm current plans there.

Limitations: it doesn't recruit participants. Skip it if you're a team of one running occasional interviews.

Condens: best for a lean repository

Condens is a research repository with transcription, highlights and tags. Its pricing page describes plans priced per contributor; check current limits and trial terms there.

Limitations: no recruiting or live session tooling. Skip it if you need an all-in-one platform.

Do interview platforms actually improve research quality?

They improve logistics and reuse; they don't improve the questions you ask. Based on the product information reviewed, the gains are in recruiting logistics, consent tracking and findability, not insight itself.

What privacy safeguards should an interview workflow include?

Collect informed consent before recording, store it with the session, limit who can see raw recordings, and delete on a fixed schedule. Check each vendor's security and data-processing documentation before uploading customer recordings.

  • Use a consent form that covers recording, transcription, AI processing and internal sharing.
  • Restrict raw video to the research team, and share redacted clips or highlights more widely.
  • Remove names and account details from tags and highlight titles.
  • Confirm where data is stored and whether the vendor signs a data processing agreement.
  • Set retention periods and actually delete recordings when they expire.
  • Apply contact limits so the same customer isn't over-researched.

What does a working repository workflow look like?

Tag every session against a shared taxonomy within a day or two, write a short summary per session, and roll findings up into insights that link back to evidence. Without a fixed taxonomy and an owner, repositories decay quickly.

  1. Import the recording and transcript straight after the session.
  2. Highlight quotes and tag them using a small, agreed tag set (product area, job, pain, outcome).
  3. Write a three-line session summary.
  4. Group tagged highlights into insights each quarter, with links to the clips.
  5. Feed insights into prioritization; see our roadmap prioritization guide and feedback and voting platforms.

Interviews explain why something happens; usage data shows how often. Before acting on a pattern from a handful of sessions, check how widespread it is in one of the analytics tools startups use to track activation and retention.

What else do product teams ask about interview platforms?

These short answers cover video tools, incentives, AI synthesis and repository ownership. They're based on product documentation and general research practice, not on hands-on testing.

Can we just run interviews on Zoom or Google Meet?

Yes, for a handful of interviews a month. Video meeting software records and sometimes transcribes, but it doesn't screen participants, manage consent, track who you've already contacted, or make findings searchable across studies.

Who pays participant incentives?

Incentives are usually a separate cost from the recruiting fee, so check how each platform's pricing page treats them. Budget for both, and check whether the platform handles payout for you.

Should we trust AI summaries of interviews?

Use them as a first pass to find moments worth reviewing, not as the finding. Always check a summary against the clip or transcript before it reaches a roadmap discussion.

Do we need a separate research repository?

If more than one person runs research or findings need to outlive the study, yes. A repository keeps tagged evidence searchable so the next team doesn't repeat the same interviews.

How long should we keep interview recordings?

Keep them only as long as your consent form and privacy policy allow, and delete or anonymize on schedule. Set the retention rule before the first session, not after.

What's the verdict on user research interview platforms?

Pick a recruiting platform if finding participants is the bottleneck, Great Question if you research your own customers end to end, and a repository such as Dovetail or Condens once findings need to be shared and reused. Lookback earns its place when moderated sessions need observers and notes.
