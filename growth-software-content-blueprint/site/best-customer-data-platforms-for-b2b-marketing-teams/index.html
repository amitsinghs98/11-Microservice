Best Customer Data Platforms for B2B Marketing Teams

The B2B customer data platforms worth shortlisting are HubSpot Data Hub or Salesforce Data 360 (formerly Data Cloud) if your CRM is the center of gravity, Twilio Segment if product events matter, and Hightouch or RudderStack if you already run a warehouse. Which B2B customer data platform should you choose?

Choose by where your customer data already lives: if HubSpot or Salesforce is your system of record, start with that vendor's own data layer; if you have a warehouse and an analyst, a warehouse-native option such as Hightouch or RudderStack usually fits better; if you need to collect product and web events from scratch, Segment is a common starting point. Few B2B teams with fewer than a handful of data sources need a separate CDP at all.

  • HubSpot-centered marketing teams with a few connected apps: HubSpot Data Hub.
  • Salesforce-centered revenue teams that want profiles inside the CRM they already run: Salesforce Data 360.
  • SaaS teams that need product events tied to contacts and accounts: Twilio Segment.
  • Teams with a modeled warehouse who want to push audiences into ad and CRM tools: Hightouch.
  • Engineering-led teams that prefer event collection that writes straight to their warehouse: RudderStack.
  • Teams with a substantial mobile app alongside web: mParticle, though its consumer-app heritage shows.

What does a B2B CDP do that your CRM, warehouse and automation tool don't?

A B2B customer data platform collects behavioral events, resolves them to people and accounts, and sends unified profiles and audiences to other tools. Your CRM stores sales records, your warehouse stores everything for analysis, and your automation tool sends messages; the CDP is the layer that joins them and keeps them in sync.

The boundaries blur in practice. HubSpot and Salesforce now market data layers inside their CRMs, and warehouse-native vendors argue that the warehouse itself should hold the unified profile while a sync tool handles activation. That's why this list mixes three architectures: packaged CDPs that store profiles, CRM-native data layers, and composable tools that sit on your warehouse. If your problem is mainly filling gaps in contact records, you need lead enrichment software for B2B revenue teams rather than a CDP. If it's mainly reporting, start with marketing analytics software.

The B2B twist is the account. Consumer CDPs are built around one person and their purchases. A B2B team needs people rolled up into accounts, accounts linked to opportunities, and product usage attributed to the workspace or company that pays. Check that account objects are first-class in any platform you shortlist, not a custom field bolted onto a person profile.

What does each platform do well, and where does it fall short?

The CRM-native options win on implementation speed, the composable options win on flexibility, and the packaged CDPs sit in between. Each block below covers best fit, the pricing model, a real limitation and when to skip it.

HubSpot Data Hub: best for HubSpot-first teams

Data Hub is HubSpot's data layer, which the vendor describes as the successor to Operations Hub. HubSpot's Data Hub product page positions it around two-way data sync with other apps, data quality tools for duplicates and formatting, and Data Studio for pulling in spreadsheet and warehouse data. Pricing follows HubSpot's tiered subscriptions, so check which sync and mapping features your tier includes before you plan around them.

Strengths: no separate system to learn, and profiles live where your marketing team already works. Limitation: it unifies data into HubSpot rather than serving profiles to every other tool, and event volume from a product is not its core job. Skip it if HubSpot isn't your CRM, or if product usage data is central to your segmentation.

Salesforce Data 360: best for Salesforce-first revenue teams

Salesforce's data platform, now marketed as Data 360 after previously being called Data Cloud, unifies records from Salesforce clouds and external sources into profiles available inside the CRM, with segmentation and activation into Salesforce's marketing products. Pricing isn't summarized here, so confirm with your account executive how usage is metered under your contract.

Strengths: profiles and segments are usable by sales, service and marketing without another sync. Limitation: implementation typically needs a Salesforce admin or partner, and costs are hard to forecast before you've modeled your data. Skip it if you're a small team on a lighter CRM or don't have Salesforce admin capacity.

Twilio Segment: best for SaaS teams that need product events

Segment collects web, app and server events and forwards them to destinations, with Unify adding identity resolution and profiles. The Unify documentation describes identity resolution into profiles, and Segment's documentation also covers account-level traits for B2B through group calls. The pricing page bases plans on monthly tracked users, so check which plan includes Unify before you budget.

Strengths: a mature tracking spec and a long list of destinations. Limitation: usage counts anonymous visitors, so high-traffic content sites can pay for many people who never convert, and profile features may not be on every plan. Skip it if you only need form fills and CRM data unified.

Hightouch: best for teams with a modeled warehouse

Hightouch reads modeled tables from your warehouse and syncs audiences and fields into ad platforms, CRMs and email tools, which the vendor calls a composable CDP. Its reverse ETL product page describes sync from warehouses to business tools; check the current plan structure on the pricing page.

Strengths: no second copy of your customer data, and your warehouse logic defines the account. Limitation: it depends on someone maintaining clean warehouse models, and it doesn't collect events itself. Skip it if you don't yet have a warehouse; see our guide to reverse ETL tools for activating warehouse data for the trade-offs.

RudderStack: best for engineering-led, warehouse-native teams

RudderStack collects events and loads them into your warehouse, then builds identity and profiles there and syncs them back out. Its documentation describes event streams, warehouse destinations and reverse ETL, and the pricing page bases plans on event volume.

Strengths: event-based pricing avoids paying per anonymous visitor, and data stays in your warehouse. Limitation: it assumes data engineering comfort, and marketers rarely self-serve audiences without an analyst. Skip it if no one on your team writes SQL.

mParticle: consider it for mobile-heavy products

mParticle is a customer data platform built around mobile and web SDKs, data quality controls and audience forwarding. Check current pricing with the vendor. Limitation: its design leans toward consumer apps, so check how it handles accounts and buying groups. Skip it if your product is web-only B2B software.

Will a CDP fix messy B2B data on its own?

No: a CDP unifies whatever you send it, so duplicate contacts, inconsistent company names and missing account IDs flow straight into the profiles. Vendors document identity rules and data quality tools, but none of them can decide for you which record is the source of truth.

Most of the effort sits in deciding the identifiers: which email or user ID defines a person, which domain or CRM ID defines an account, and what happens when they conflict. If your CRM already holds many duplicates, budget time for CRM data quality and deduplication tools before or alongside the CDP project.

Which capabilities should you compare first?

Compare identity resolution and account mapping first, because they decide whether profiles are trustworthy. Then check event ingestion, activation destinations, consent controls, implementation effort and how usage is metered.

  • Identity resolution: deterministic matching on email and user IDs is the B2B baseline. Ask how merges are reviewed and reversed.
  • Account mapping: how people are linked to accounts (domain, CRM account ID, workspace ID) and whether accounts carry their own traits.
  • Event ingestion: SDKs for web and product, server-side sources, and whether events land in your warehouse.
  • Profile unification: where the profile lives (vendor, CRM or warehouse) and who can query it.
  • Activation: native destinations for your CRM, ad platforms and email tool, and how often syncs run.
  • Consent: whether consent state travels with the profile and blocks destinations automatically.
  • Implementation: who has to do the work, and whether a partner is expected.
  • Pricing metric: tracked users, events, profiles, rows or negotiated usage, and which one grows fastest for you.

Is your team ready for a CDP?

You're ready when you can name your core identifiers, have at least three systems that disagree about the same customers, and have a person who'll own the data model after launch. If you can't tick most of the list below, a better CRM setup or an integration tool will deliver more for less.

  1. You have one agreed CRM, and its account records are mostly deduplicated.
  2. You can name the field that identifies a person and the one that identifies an account.
  3. Product usage or website behavior would change who you target, not just what you report.
  4. Someone owns the tracking plan and will review new events before they ship.
  5. You know which destinations need audiences: ads, email, CRM, sales engagement.
  6. Consent and region rules are documented, and legal has signed off on what you'll collect.
  7. You have a warehouse, or a clear reason not to need one.

What mistakes do B2B teams make when buying a CDP?

The common mistakes are buying before the identifiers are agreed, choosing on destination count instead of data model, and underestimating who will maintain it. Each one turns a profile project into an expensive pipe.

  • Treating the CDP as a CRM cleanup tool. Fix duplicates at the source, or they multiply.
  • Ignoring the pricing metric. A tracked-user model punishes high anonymous traffic; an event model punishes chatty tracking. Model your own volumes.
  • Buying a packaged CDP when a warehouse already exists. You may end up paying to store the same data twice. Our guide to warehouses for small marketing analytics teams covers that foundation.
  • No owner after launch. Tracking plans decay quickly without review.

What else do buyers ask about B2B customer data platforms?

The questions below cover where a CDP stops and other tools start. Confirm vendor specifics on each official page.

Is HubSpot or Salesforce already a CDP?

Both now market data layers that unify records inside their CRMs, which covers many small B2B teams. They are weaker at serving profiles to tools outside their own ecosystem.

Do I need a data warehouse to use a CDP?

Not for packaged CDPs such as Segment, which store profiles themselves. Composable tools such as Hightouch need a warehouse, because they read your modeled tables.

How do CDPs handle anonymous website visitors?

Most assign an anonymous ID and merge it with a known profile when the visitor identifies, for example by filling in a form. Check whether anonymous visitors count toward your usage bill.

Can a CDP replace a marketing automation platform?

No. A CDP builds profiles and audiences, while marketing automation sends the emails and runs the workflows. Most B2B teams need both or a CRM suite that bundles them.

What's the verdict?

For most small B2B marketing teams, the best CDP is the one closest to the CRM they already run: HubSpot Data Hub or Salesforce Data 360. Move to Segment when product events matter, and to Hightouch or RudderStack when you have a warehouse and someone to model it.
