Which feature flag platform should a SaaS team choose?

Choose LaunchDarkly if releases are business-critical and you need approvals, audit trails and mature SDKs across many services. Choose an open-source option such as Unleash, Flagsmith or GrowthBook if you want to self-host or avoid per-user pricing, and ConfigCat or PostHog if you're a small team that wants flags without a large contract.

  • Enterprise release governance: LaunchDarkly or Harness Feature Management & Experimentation.
  • Flags plus experiments with analytics: Statsig or GrowthBook.
  • Self-hosted, open-source flags: Unleash or Flagsmith.
  • Small team, simple plan model: ConfigCat.
  • Already using PostHog for analytics: PostHog feature flags.

What does a feature flag platform actually do for a release?

It separates deploying code from releasing it, so a change can sit in production behind a flag until you turn it on for chosen users. That gives you percentage rollouts, targeting by user or account, and a kill switch that doesn't need a redeploy.

The trade-off is an operational dependency. Your application now asks the flag service, or a cached copy of its rules, what to show. If the SDK can't reach the service, it falls back to defaults, so you need to decide what those defaults are for every flag. Flags that measure impact overlap with experiments, which our list of product experimentation platforms for growth teams covers in depth.

What is each platform best for, and when should you skip it?

LaunchDarkly is the reference point for governed releases, while the others win on price, openness or a tighter link to experiments. Each block names the fit and the reason to pass.

LaunchDarkly: best for business-critical release governance

LaunchDarkly offers flags, targeting, approvals, release pipelines and experimentation. Its pricing page lists a free plan and usage-based paid plans, and its billing documentation explains how usage is metered (both checked 5 November 2026). Strengths: broad SDKs and mature governance. Limitations: usage meters plus add-ons make forecasting harder. Skip it if you have one app and a few flags.

Harness FME: best for teams standardised on Harness

Harness acquired Split in 2024 and now sells it as Feature Management & Experimentation (checked 5 November 2026). Strengths: flags and experiments next to pipelines. Limitations: check the official site for current pricing, and existing Split users should ask about migration timing. Skip it if you don't use Harness for delivery.

Statsig: best when every flag should measure impact

Statsig ties feature gates to experiment metrics, and its pricing page describes a free developer tier before metered paid plans (checked 5 November 2026). Its ownership has changed, as its acquisition announcement explains. Strengths: analysis built in. Limitations: confirm who owns and supports the product today before you commit. Skip it if you only need on/off release control.

GrowthBook: best for warehouse-native flags and experiments

GrowthBook is open core, can be self-hosted and analyses experiments against your own warehouse. Its pricing page lists a free Starter plan and a per-seat Pro plan (checked 5 November 2026). Strengths: local flag evaluation and open statistics. Limitations: experiment analysis assumes a warehouse is in place. Skip it if you have no data warehouse and no plans for one.

Unleash: best for self-hosted open-source flags

Unleash is open source with paid Enterprise tiers. Its pricing page separates the free open-source edition from paid Enterprise plans (checked 5 November 2026). Strengths: control over hosting and data. Limitations: check which governance features, such as approvals and SSO, need a paid plan. Skip it if nobody wants to run the infrastructure.

Flagsmith: best for open source with a hosted fallback

Flagsmith offers a BSD-licensed core you can self-host and a cloud service. Its pricing page lists a free cloud plan and paid tiers (checked 5 November 2026). Strengths: easy move between hosted and self-hosted. Limitations: check which governance features, such as audit logs and SSO, need a higher tier. Skip it if you need approvals on a small budget.

ConfigCat: best for small teams that want a flat bill

ConfigCat sets out what each plan includes on its plan limits page (checked 5 November 2026), so check the flag and targeting caps against your needs. Strengths: a simple plan model. Limitations: no built-in experiment analysis. Skip it if you want flags and statistics in one tool.

PostHog: best if PostHog already runs your analytics

PostHog bills flags per request with a monthly free allowance (checked 5 November 2026), and flags sit next to analytics and experiments. Strengths: one SDK and one bill. Limitations: lighter approval workflows than LaunchDarkly. Skip it if release governance is the main requirement.

Do feature flags really reduce release risk, or just move it?

Based on the documentation reviewed, flags reduce the blast radius of a bad release but add a runtime dependency and a growing pile of conditional code. The risk moves from deploy day to flag hygiene.

What does a safe release and stale-flag cleanup workflow look like?

Create every flag with an owner, a type (release, experiment, operational or permission) and an expiry date. Roll out in stages, watch guardrail metrics, then remove the flag and the dead code within a set window after full release.

  1. Create: name the flag, set the safe default and record the owner and ticket.
  2. Test in lower environments: confirm both states work in development and staging.
  3. Internal release: target employees or a test account first.
  4. Percentage rollout: increase in steps while you watch error rates, latency and a key product metric.
  5. Full release: serve the new state to everyone and stop changing rules.
  6. Clean up: delete the flag and the old code path, and log the removal.

Review stale flags monthly. Long-lived permission and kill-switch flags are fine, but they should be labelled so they aren't mistaken for forgotten release flags. To see whether released features are actually used, pair this with feature adoption analytics.

What mistakes do teams make with feature flag platforms?

The costliest mistakes are never removing flags and treating every flag as an experiment. Both create code paths nobody understands.

  • Leaving defaults unconsidered, so an outage at the flag service flips users into the wrong experience.
  • Granting production flag changes to everyone without approvals or audit logs.
  • Picking a platform on seat price when the real meter is users, connections or requests.
  • Measuring a rollout without connecting it to product analytics, so nobody knows whether it helped.

What else do SaaS teams ask about feature flag platforms?

These come up after the shortlist is set. The answers draw on vendor documentation reviewed for this draft.

Are feature flags the same as A/B tests?

No. A flag controls who sees a change, while an A/B test randomises users and measures the difference with statistics. Many platforms do both, and our comparison of feature flags vs A/B testing explains when to use each.

Do feature flags slow down an application?

Most modern SDKs evaluate rules locally from a cached copy, so each check doesn't need a network call. Ask each vendor how its SDK caches rules and what happens if it can't reach the service.

Can we build feature flags in-house?

You can build simple on/off flags quickly, but targeting, audit logs, approvals and multi-environment management take ongoing work. Open-source tools are a middle path if you want control without starting from zero.

How many flags is too many?

There's no fixed number, but every release flag without an owner and expiry date is debt. A monthly stale-flag review keeps the count manageable.

What's the verdict on feature flag management platforms?

LaunchDarkly is the safest choice for governed releases at scale, GrowthBook and Statsig suit teams that want every flag to measure impact, and Unleash, Flagsmith and ConfigCat suit teams that value openness or a simple plan model. Pick on governance needs and pricing unit, not feature count.
