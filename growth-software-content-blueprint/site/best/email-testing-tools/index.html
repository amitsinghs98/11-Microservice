Which email testing tool should you choose?

Marketing teams that send designed campaigns should start with Litmus or Email on Acid, because both combine client previews with link, image and accessibility checks. Developers who build templates in code will prefer Parcel, and teams testing product emails in staging or automated tests need Mailtrap or Mailosaur.

  • Marketing team with approvals and many stakeholders: Litmus.
  • Campaign team wanting a pre-send checklist with previews: Email on Acid.
  • Email developer writing HTML by hand: Parcel.
  • Engineers catching app emails in staging: Mailtrap.
  • QA teams asserting on emails in automated tests: Mailosaur.
  • Spot checks on spam filters and inbox placement: GlockApps, as a bridge into deliverability work.

How is pre-send QA different from deliverability monitoring?

Pre-send QA checks the message itself (rendering, links, images, accessibility, content triggers) before you hit send. Deliverability monitoring watches what happens after you send, including authentication, sender reputation, blocklists and inbox placement across your real list.

Most tools on this page focus on the first job, and a few add spam checks or seed tests that overlap with the second. A preview that renders perfectly tells you nothing about whether Gmail will put the message in the inbox, and a clean reputation won't fix a broken button in Outlook. What is each tool good at, and what does it miss?

The campaign suites cover the widest range of checks, while the developer and QA tools go deeper on one part of the workflow. Each block says who should skip it.

Litmus: best for marketing teams with approvals

Litmus offers previews across major email clients and devices, a pre-send checklist covering links, images and accessibility, spam testing, and proofing so reviewers can comment on a draft. It also integrates with common email platforms. See the Litmus pricing page for which checks sit on which plan.

Skip it if you send a simple text-led newsletter; a suite with collaboration features is more than you need.

Email on Acid: best for checklist-driven campaign QA

Email on Acid, part of Sinch, combines client previews with a campaign precheck that flags broken links, missing alt text, accessibility issues and spam risks. It suits teams that want one structured pass before each send. See the Email on Acid pricing page.

Skip it if your bottleneck is review and sign-off rather than technical QA.

Parcel: best for email developers

Parcel is a code editor built for email, with live previews, accessibility checks, reusable components and client previews. It's aimed at people who write HTML email and want feedback as they type. See Parcel's site for current plans.

Skip it if nobody on your team edits email code; a drag-and-drop builder plus a suite like Litmus suits you better.

Mailtrap: best for catching emails in staging

Mailtrap's testing product gives developers a sandbox inbox that captures emails sent from development and staging, so nothing reaches real users. It adds HTML checks and spam analysis, and the company also sells a separate sending service. See the Mailtrap pricing page.

Skip it if you need many real-client renders of a marketing campaign; that's the suites' job. Teams sending app email should pair it with a transactional email service.

Mailosaur: best for automated test suites

Mailosaur provides test email addresses and SMS numbers you can assert against in automated tests, with libraries for common frameworks such as Cypress, Playwright and Selenium. It's how QA teams prove sign-up and password reset flows still work after each release. See the Mailosaur pricing page.

Skip it if you don't run automated end-to-end tests; it won't help a marketer check a newsletter.

GlockApps: best for spam and placement spot checks

GlockApps sends your message to a set of seed addresses and reports where it landed, alongside spam filter checks and DMARC reporting. It's a useful bridge between QA and deliverability. See the GlockApps pricing page.

Skip it if you treat seed results as your inbox rate; seed accounts don't behave like engaged subscribers.

How are email testing tools priced?

Campaign suites sell tiered subscriptions where previews, checks, users and integrations unlock by plan, while developer and QA tools usually price by inboxes, test volume or team size. Your cost depends less on the headline tier and more on how many previews you run and who needs a seat.

Before buying, count campaigns per month, versions per campaign (languages, segments, dark mode), the number of reviewers, and whether you need accessibility and spam checks or only previews. Ask whether preview counts are capped, whether unused allowance rolls over, and which email clients are included on your tier. Teams sending a few campaigns a month may get by on a lower plan plus manual checks; agencies running many client templates usually can't.

What should you test before every campaign?

Test the things most likely to break and most costly when they do: links, rendering in your audience's main clients, images and alt text, accessibility basics and personalization. Run the full list on every new template and a short version on each send.

  1. Links: every link resolves, tracking parameters are correct and the unsubscribe link works.
  2. Rendering: previews in the clients your audience actually uses, including dark mode and a narrow mobile width.
  3. Images: the email still makes sense with images blocked, and every meaningful image has descriptive alt text.
  4. Accessibility: real text rather than text in images, sufficient color contrast, a logical reading order, a language attribute and descriptive link text.
  5. Personalization: merge fields have fallbacks, so nobody gets "Hi ,".
  6. Content and size: a plain-text version exists, the preheader reads well, and the HTML isn't so large that some clients truncate it.
  7. Sender details: the from name, reply-to and sending domain match what subscribers expect.
  8. Final human read: one person who didn't build the email reads it on a phone.

How far can you trust an inbox preview?

Trust it for layout: a preview shows how a client rendered your HTML at that moment, on that configuration. Don't trust it for placement, interactivity or every user setting, because previews can't reproduce each subscriber's client version, accessibility tools, zoom level or inbox filters.

What else do teams ask about email testing?

Most questions are about free options, frequency and overlap with deliverability tools. Short answers follow.

Can I test emails for free?

Partly. Sending tests to accounts you own, plus free tiers and free spam-score checkers, covers the basics, but broad client previews and accessibility checks usually need a paid plan.

Do I need to test every campaign or only new templates?

Run full previews on every new or changed template and a short check of links, personalization and images on every send.

Is a spam score the same as inbox placement?

No. A spam score flags content and configuration issues, while inbox placement depends heavily on your sender reputation and how subscribers engage.

Who should own email QA on a small team?

Whoever builds the email runs the checklist, and a second person who didn't build it does the final read before it goes out.

What's the verdict?

Campaign teams should shortlist Litmus for workflow and Email on Acid for checklist-driven QA, developers should look at Parcel, and engineering teams need Mailtrap for staging and Mailosaur for automated tests. None of them replaces deliverability monitoring, so budget for both if inbox placement matters.
