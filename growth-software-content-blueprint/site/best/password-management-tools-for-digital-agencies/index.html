Best Password Management Tools for Digital Agencies

The best password managers for digital agencies keep every client's credentials in a separate vault, grant access by role, and let you revoke a leaver's access quickly. 1Password, Bitwarden and Keeper cover most agency needs; the right one depends on how strictly you separate clients and whether you need self-hosting.

Which password manager should a digital agency choose?

Choose 1Password if you want the most polished sharing experience for a mixed technical and non-technical team, Bitwarden if cost control or self-hosting matters most, and Keeper if you need granular folder permissions and compliance reporting. Whatever you pick, the deciding test is whether you can set up one vault per client and remove a contractor's access without touching anyone else's.

  • Mixed teams that want low-friction sharing: 1Password.
  • Budget-conscious or technical agencies, including those that want to self-host: Bitwarden.
  • Agencies with compliance-minded clients that ask for access reports: Keeper.
  • Teams that value a simple admin console and breach monitoring: Dashlane.
  • Small agencies wanting a straightforward business vault: NordPass.
  • Developer-heavy agencies that want open-source, self-hosted team sharing: Passbolt.
  • Teams already on LastPass: review it carefully before renewing, for the reasons below.

Why do agencies need a business password manager at all?

Agencies hold logins for dozens of client platforms (ad accounts, CMS admins, hosting, analytics, social profiles), and those credentials leak when they're pasted into Slack, emailed, or stored in a shared spreadsheet. A business password manager replaces those habits with encrypted vaults, role-based sharing and a record of who can see what.

The risk is specific to agency work. Staff and freelancers rotate across accounts, clients leave and take their platforms with them, and a single shared Google Sheet of passwords can expose every client at once. Chat and document tools keep message history long after anyone remembers what was posted, so a password shared "just this once" stays searchable for years.

A password manager won't fix poor access hygiene on its own. Where a platform supports it, giving each person their own named login, or using the platform's own partner or delegated access, is better than sharing one credential. Use the vault for the logins that genuinely have to be shared, and for the recovery codes and MFA secrets that sit behind them.

What does each password manager do well, and where does it fall short?

1Password and Bitwarden are the two most defensible defaults for agencies, with Keeper the strongest choice when clients demand access evidence. The others are credible but narrower, and LastPass needs a deliberate risk decision rather than a default renewal.

1Password: best for mixed teams sharing client vaults

1Password organises credentials into vaults with per-group permissions, so you can create one vault per client and give the account team access to only that vault. Its apps and browser extension are approachable for account managers and designers, not just developers, which matters because adoption is what stops passwords going back into chat. Business plans add admin controls, activity reporting and SSO options. Limitations: confirm on the official pricing page which tier includes guest accounts for clients or freelancers, and what each tier costs for your team size. Skip it if you need to self-host. See 1Password's business pricing page.

Bitwarden: best for cost control and self-hosting

Bitwarden uses organisations and collections to group shared items, and its open-source codebase and self-hosting option appeal to technical agencies and clients with data-residency questions. It offers both cloud-hosted and self-hosted deployment for business tiers. Limitations: the admin console and permission model take more setup thought than 1Password's, and non-technical staff may need a short walkthrough. Skip it if your team has little patience for configuration. See Bitwarden's business pricing.

Keeper: best for granular permissions and access reporting

Keeper structures sharing around shared folders with record-level and folder-level permissions, and it markets compliance and reporting features for administrators. That suits agencies whose enterprise or regulated clients ask, "who on your side can access our accounts?" Limitations: some capabilities may be sold as add-ons, so check the official page to see what the base plan includes before you compare costs. Skip it if you want one simple plan with no add-on decisions. See Keeper's business page.

Dashlane: best for simple administration

Dashlane's business product is positioned around an approachable admin console, password-health views and dark-web monitoring. Collections let teams share groups of logins. Limitations: its sharing model is less layered than 1Password's vaults or Keeper's folders, which matters once you manage many clients with different teams. Skip it if you need fine-grained per-client roles. See Dashlane's pricing page.

NordPass: best for small teams wanting a straightforward vault

NordPass Business offers shared items and groups with an admin panel, which can be enough for a small agency with a handful of clients. Limitations: permissions, reporting and integrations are lighter than the leaders', so check the admin features you'll rely on during offboarding. Skip it if you expect to grow into complex client separation. See NordPass Business.

Passbolt: best for self-hosted, developer-led teams

Passbolt is an open-source password manager designed for team sharing, with a self-hosted community edition and paid editions. It fits agencies with in-house engineers who prefer to control the infrastructure. Limitations: self-hosting means your team owns updates, backups and server security, which is a real ongoing cost. Skip it if nobody on staff wants to maintain it. See Passbolt's pricing page.

LastPass: only if you've reviewed its security history

LastPass still offers business plans with shared folders and admin policies, and some agencies use it from years ago. It has publicly disclosed past security incidents, so before renewing, read the vendor's own disclosures and decide whether your clients would accept that history. Skip it if a client security questionnaire would ask about it. See LastPass pricing.

Which features matter most for agency client access?

Prioritise per-client vault separation, quick offboarding and an activity record over extras like password-health scores. Then check MFA enforcement, account recovery and SSO, because those decide whether the system survives a lost phone or a departing admin.

  • Vault separation: one vault or collection per client, plus an internal vault for agency systems.
  • Permissions: view-only versus edit versus manage, set per group rather than per person.
  • External sharing: guest or limited accounts for freelancers and, occasionally, client staff.
  • Offboarding: suspending a user removes access to every shared vault in one action.
  • MFA: enforce it for every member, and store client platforms' MFA recovery codes in the vault.
  • Audit: activity or access reports you can export when a client asks.
  • Recovery: a documented admin recovery path so one person's lost device doesn't lock the agency out.
  • Integrations: SSO and directory sync, so joiners and leavers flow from your identity provider.

What does a least-privilege handover checklist look like?

Grant the minimum access each role needs at onboarding, and reverse it in a fixed order at offboarding, starting with the identity provider and ending with rotating any credential the leaver could have copied. Run the same checklist when a client relationship ends.

  1. At project start: create the client vault, add only the delivery team's group, and record the client's named owner for each platform.
  2. Prefer named access: where a platform supports partner, agency or delegated access, use it instead of a shared password.
  3. When someone leaves: disable their identity-provider account, suspend them in the password manager, and remove them from client platforms where they had named logins.
  4. Rotate exposed secrets: change any shared password, API key or MFA seed they could view, prioritising ad accounts with payment methods attached.
  5. When a client leaves: hand credentials back through the client's own channel, rotate them, then archive or delete the vault.
  6. Every quarter: review vault membership against your current team and account list.

The handover is part of a wider offboarding flow; see client onboarding software for agencies for the start of that lifecycle, and client portals for agencies for sharing deliverables (not passwords) with clients.

What mistakes do agencies make when rolling out a password manager?

The biggest mistake is buying the tool and leaving the old spreadsheet and chat history in place. The second is putting every client in one shared vault, which recreates the all-or-nothing access problem inside an encrypted container.

  • Letting individuals keep client logins in personal vaults that leave with them.
  • Skipping MFA enforcement for "convenience" during rollout.
  • Giving freelancers full member accounts when limited or guest access would do.
  • Never testing the admin recovery process until it's needed.
  • Treating website hosting and CMS admin logins as low risk; plan maintenance access alongside your agency project management setup so ownership is clear.

What's the verdict?

For most agencies, 1Password is the easiest to roll out across a mixed team and Bitwarden is the strongest value or self-hosting choice. Keeper earns its place when clients want access evidence, while Dashlane, NordPass and Passbolt suit narrower situations.

What else do agencies ask about password managers?

Most follow-up questions are about clients, freelancers and recovery. These answers are general, so confirm current plan features on the official vendor pages.

Should clients have access to our password manager?

Usually not as full members. If a client needs to share a credential with you, use a limited or guest sharing option, or ask them to grant named access on the platform itself.

Is a shared browser password list good enough for a small agency?

It's better than chat, but browser-saved passwords usually lack per-client permissions, audit reports and clean offboarding. Once you have more than a couple of staff or any freelancers, a business password manager is easier to control.

Can we store MFA codes in the same vault as passwords?

Many business password managers can store one-time-code secrets, which is convenient for shared client logins. Weigh that convenience against having both factors in one place, and keep platform recovery codes in a restricted vault.

How quickly should access be removed when someone leaves?

On their last working day, before or at the time their identity-provider account is disabled. Then rotate any shared secrets they could have viewed.

Do we still need a password manager if we use SSO?

Yes, in most agencies. SSO covers your own tools, but many client platforms still rely on shared logins, API keys and recovery codes that need a secure home.
