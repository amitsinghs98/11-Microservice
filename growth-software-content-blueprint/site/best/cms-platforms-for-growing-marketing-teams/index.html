Best CMS Platforms for Growing Marketing Teams

When comparing CMS platforms for marketing teams, the question that matters is how much your marketers can publish and change without a developer, and what that freedom costs to maintain. We sort seven options into hosted, open-source and headless models and judge each on autonomy, governance, extensibility and total cost of ownership.

Which CMS should a growing marketing team choose?

Choose Webflow or Framer if marketers want to design and ship pages themselves on a hosted platform, WordPress if you want the largest ecosystem and full ownership, and HubSpot Content Hub if your CRM is HubSpot. Move to a headless option such as Storyblok or Contentful only when the same content has to feed more than one website or app.

  • Maximum ecosystem and ownership: WordPress (self-hosted).
  • Visual design control without a developer for every page: Webflow.
  • Website, CRM and marketing on one platform: HubSpot Content Hub.
  • Fast, design-led marketing sites: Framer.
  • Complex permissions, multisite and multilingual needs: Drupal.
  • Headless with a visual editor for marketers: Storyblok.
  • Structured content across many channels, backed by developers: Contentful.

What are the main CMS models, and how do they differ?

Hosted platforms handle servers, security and updates for you; open-source platforms give you the code and the maintenance; headless platforms store structured content and deliver it by API to a front end your developers build. Traditional open-source and hosted CMSs couple content to page templates, which is simpler for one website and limiting for many channels.

  • Hosted (Webflow, Framer, HubSpot Content Hub): fastest marketer autonomy, less control over hosting and code, and platform lock-in to weigh.
  • Open source (WordPress, Drupal): full ownership and huge extension libraries, but you own hosting, updates and plugin risk.
  • Headless (Storyblok, Contentful): reusable content across channels, but you need developers for the front end and preview.

What does each CMS do well, and where does it fall short?

Each block names the team the platform suits and the team that should skip it. Fit is our editorial assessment; confirm product details on the vendor pages linked.

WordPress: best for teams that want ownership and the biggest ecosystem

Self-hosted WordPress is free, open-source software with an enormous library of themes and plugins and a large pool of developers. Its block editor gives marketers reasonable page-building freedom once a theme with sensible patterns is in place.

Strengths: no licence fee, full control of data and hosting, and SEO plugins for almost any need. Limitations: every plugin is a maintenance and security liability, and someone must own updates. Skip it if nobody will be responsible for hosting, backups and patching.

Webflow: best for marketers who want visual control without code

Webflow combines a visual designer, hosting and a CMS for collections such as blog posts or case studies. Marketers can build and edit pages without a developer once the design system exists. Site and workspace plans are explained on the Webflow pricing page.

Strengths: design precision and managed hosting. Limitations: complex content relationships, granular permissions and large multilingual sites need careful planning and may sit on higher tiers. Skip it if your content model is closer to a database than a website. Our WordPress versus Webflow comparison goes deeper on this choice.

HubSpot Content Hub: best when the website should live next to the CRM

Content Hub is HubSpot's CMS and content product, so forms, landing pages, blog posts and contact records share one system. Smart content and reporting can use CRM data without a separate integration. Tiers are on the HubSpot Content Hub pricing page.

Strengths: attribution and personalisation from CRM data, with hosting handled. Limitations: developer flexibility is narrower than open source, and leaving HubSpot means migrating the site too. Skip it if your sales team uses a different CRM.

Framer: best for fast, design-led marketing sites

Framer is a hosted site builder with a design-tool feel and a built-in CMS for collections, aimed at teams that ship and iterate landing pages quickly. Plans are on the Framer pricing page.

Strengths: speed from design to live page. Limitations: less proven for very large content libraries, complex workflows or strict governance. Skip it if you publish hundreds of articles a year with several approvers.

Drupal: best for complex permissions, multisite and multilingual needs

Drupal is open-source software with strong support for structured content types, granular roles and multilingual sites, which is why it's common in universities, governments and large organisations.

Strengths: deep governance and content modelling without a licence fee. Limitations: development and maintenance need experienced specialists, and the marketer editing experience depends on how it's built. Skip it if you don't have, or won't hire, Drupal developers.

Storyblok: best for headless with an editor marketers can use

Storyblok is a headless CMS known for its visual editor, which lets marketers see and arrange components on a live preview while developers own the front end. Plans are on the Storyblok pricing page.

Strengths: a middle path between marketer autonomy and developer freedom. Limitations: you still build and host a front end, and preview depends on that integration. Skip it if you have no developers.

Contentful: best for structured content across many channels

Contentful is an API-first content platform for teams that reuse structured content across websites, apps and other touchpoints, with environments and roles for larger organisations. Plans are on the Contentful pricing page.

Strengths: strong content modelling and reuse at scale. Limitations: marketers can only do what developers have modelled and built. Skip it if you run a single marketing site.

How much developer help will marketers still need?

Even on the most marketer-friendly platforms, developers are usually needed for the design system, integrations and anything structural; marketers gain autonomy over pages and content within those limits. Vendor materials describe editor capabilities, not how often a real team hits the edge of them.

What does a CMS really cost to own?

The licence or plan is often the smallest part of CMS cost; hosting, developer time, plugins or add-ons, and the hours spent on updates and fixes usually decide the total. Compare platforms on a three-year view that includes those items, not on the monthly plan alone.

  • Open source: no licence, but you pay for hosting, premium plugins or modules, security monitoring and ongoing developer hours.
  • Hosted builders: plan fees cover hosting and updates, but localization, extra seats or higher traffic can push you up a tier.
  • Headless: plan fees plus front-end hosting and the developer time to build and maintain it.

Ask each vendor what changes the bill: editor seats, locales, environments, bandwidth or API calls. Those are the usual tier triggers, and they vary by platform.

How do roles, governance and localization change the choice?

Once several teams or regions publish, you need roles that limit who can edit and publish which content, plus a workflow for approvals and a clean way to manage translations. Drupal, Contentful and Storyblok are designed with those needs in mind; hosted builders handle them with more variation by tier.

Define your rules first: who can publish without review, which pages need legal sign-off, and who owns each locale. Our list of content governance tools for distributed teams covers the policy side, and the guide to localization and translation management software covers connecting a CMS to translators.

What should you plan for when migrating?

Plan for content mapping, URL redirects and SEO checks first, because traffic losses after a migration usually come from broken URLs and missing metadata rather than the new platform. Inventory what you have before you choose what to move.

  1. Inventory every URL, its traffic and its backlinks, and decide what to keep, merge or retire.
  2. Map old content types and fields to the new platform's model.
  3. Build a redirect map for every URL that changes.
  4. Carry over titles, meta descriptions, canonical tags and structured data.
  5. Train editors on the new roles and workflow before launch day.
  6. Monitor crawl errors and rankings for several weeks after launch.

Large sites should start with a content inventory and audit tool before mapping anything.

What else do marketing teams ask about choosing a CMS?

These answers cover SEO, AI features and when to go headless. They reflect our editorial assessment rather than vendor promises.

Which CMS is best for SEO?

None wins on SEO by itself; what matters is control over URLs, metadata, redirects, structured data and page speed. WordPress, Webflow, HubSpot and Drupal all offer these controls, while headless platforms leave much of it to your front end.

Is WordPress still a good choice for a growing team?

Yes, if someone owns hosting, updates and plugin choices. It becomes risky when nobody does, because outdated plugins are the usual source of problems.

When should a marketing team go headless?

Go headless when the same content must feed several websites, apps or product interfaces, and you have developers to build and maintain the front end. For one marketing site, a traditional or hosted CMS is usually simpler.

Does the CMS need built-in AI writing features?

It's a convenience, not a deciding factor. Choose on autonomy, governance and cost, and use a separate AI tool if drafting is your bottleneck.

What's the verdict?

Most growing marketing teams with one main website are best served by WordPress if they can own maintenance, Webflow or Framer if they want hosted design freedom, or HubSpot Content Hub if the CRM is already HubSpot. Choose Drupal, Storyblok or Contentful when governance, localization or multi-channel reuse is the actual requirement.
