Which capacity planning tool should a small agency choose?

Pick Runn if you want scenario planning with tentative projects, Float if you want a visual team schedule that's simple to maintain, and Productive if you'd rather forecast capacity inside the same system that holds budgets and time. If you're a very small team with only a couple of account leads, a well-kept spreadsheet may be enough until scheduling conflicts become weekly.

  • Forecasting with tentative projects and what-if scenarios: Runn.
  • A simple, visual team schedule with leave and capacity views: Float.
  • Capacity tied to budgets, time and profitability in one tool: Productive.
  • Lightweight forward schedule for teams already on Harvest: Harvest Forecast.
  • Booking people and rooms with clashes flagged: Resource Guru.
  • Agencies running delivery in Teamwork.com: its workload and resource planning features.
  • Larger teams wanting detailed resource requests and approvals: Hub Planner.

How is capacity planning different from task management and time tracking?

Task management tells you what has to be done this week, time tracking tells you what already happened, and capacity planning tells you whether next month's demand fits the hours you'll realistically have. You need all three, but they answer different questions and are often owned by different people.

A task board shows a designer with twelve open tasks; it doesn't tell you whether those tasks add up to more hours than the designer has after a week of leave. Timesheets show that last month's retainer ran over; they don't warn you that two pitches likely to close in December will need the same senior strategist. Capacity planning sits between the sales pipeline and the delivery schedule, at the level of roles and weeks rather than individual tasks.

That's why many agencies run a dedicated capacity tool even when their project tool has a workload view. What does each tool do well, and where does it fall short?

Runn and Float are the strongest dedicated choices, but for different temperaments: Runn leans toward forecasting and finance, Float toward day-to-day schedule clarity. The platform options win when you don't want another tool to keep in sync.

Runn: best for scenarios and tentative demand

Runn positions itself around resource and capacity forecasting, with tentative projects, placeholders for unhired roles and what-if scenarios. That's exactly the shape of the "can we take this pitch?" question. Limitations: its financial forecasting is only as good as the rates and assumptions you enter, and some scenario features may sit on higher tiers. Skip it if you only need a shared calendar of who's on what. See Runn's pricing page.

Float: best for a schedule your team keeps current

Float offers a visual schedule of people against projects, with time off, capacity and utilization views, aimed at project managers who update the schedule week to week. Limitations: it's stronger at the next few weeks than at modelling hiring scenarios months ahead, so check its tentative-project and reporting features against your needs. Skip it if finance-led forecasting is the main goal. See Float's pricing page.

Productive: best for capacity tied to budgets and profit

Productive is an agency operations platform covering projects, time, budgets, resourcing and profitability, so a capacity forecast can be read alongside margin. Limitations: you get most value only if you move delivery and time tracking into it, which is a bigger change than adding a planner. Skip it if you're happy with your current project tool. See Productive's pricing.

Harvest Forecast: best for teams already on Harvest

Forecast is Harvest's companion scheduling tool, sold separately, for planning who works on which project and when. It pairs naturally with Harvest timesheets. Limitations: it's deliberately simple, with fewer skills, scenario and pipeline features than Runn. Skip it if you need to model hiring. See Harvest Forecast.

Resource Guru: best for bookings with clash detection

Resource Guru focuses on booking people, equipment and rooms, with leave management and alerts when someone is double-booked. Limitations: it's more of a booking system than a demand forecaster. Skip it if sales-pipeline demand is central to your planning. See Resource Guru pricing.

Teamwork.com: best when delivery already lives there

Teamwork.com includes workload and resource planning views next to tasks, time and budgets, which avoids syncing a separate planner. Limitations: resource features may require a higher plan, and planning is task-driven, which can be too granular for long-range forecasting. Skip it if you don't use it for delivery. See Teamwork.com pricing.

Hub Planner: best for resource requests and approvals

Hub Planner offers scheduling with optional extensions for areas such as timesheets, resource requests and reporting. It suits agencies where project leads request people and a resource manager approves. Limitations: more configuration than a small team may want. Skip it if one person does all the scheduling. See Hub Planner pricing.

What should a weekly capacity review cover?

Hold a short, fixed meeting each week comparing booked and likely demand against available hours by role for the next several weeks, then decide what to move, decline or hire for. Keep the same agenda every week so trends become visible.

  1. Availability: confirm leave, public holidays and part-time patterns are entered.
  2. Confirmed demand: check booked hours by role against capacity; flag any role over or under your target utilization.
  3. Pipeline demand: add tentative projects weighted by likelihood, agreed with whoever owns sales.
  4. Actuals check: compare last week's tracked time with what was planned, and adjust estimates for retainers that keep overrunning.
  5. Skills gaps: note work that only one person can do.
  6. Decisions: reschedule, bring in a freelancer, push back a start date or open a hiring conversation, and record who owns each.

Overruns spotted here should flow into margin reporting; see agency profitability reporting tools.

When should a capacity forecast trigger hiring?

Hire, or line up freelancers, when a role stays over your target utilization for several consecutive weeks on confirmed work, not on a single busy week. Set the threshold in advance so the decision isn't made on gut feel during a crunch.

  • Sustained overload: a role above target on confirmed work for a set run of consecutive weeks in the forecast. Choose that number in advance for your own agency; it isn't a benchmark.
  • Pipeline pressure: weighted pipeline would push that role over capacity even if only part of it closes.
  • Single points of failure: one person holds a skill several clients depend on.
  • Freelancer dependency: you're repeatedly buying the same skill externally at a premium.

What mistakes do agencies make with capacity planning?

The commonest mistake is planning people at full availability, ignoring meetings, admin, sales and leave. The second is keeping the plan separate from sales, so the forecast never sees work that's about to land.

  • Planning by named person only, with no role view, so you can't see which skill is short.
  • Treating retainers as fixed hours when the time data says they vary.
  • Letting the plan drift because nobody owns the weekly update.
  • Buying a planner with deep features the team won't maintain.

What else do agencies ask about capacity planning software?

Most remaining questions are about utilization targets, spreadsheets and integrations. Treat these answers as general guidance and confirm features on each vendor's official pages.

What utilization target should a small agency use?

There's no universal number, because it depends on how much time each role spends on sales, admin and internal work. Set a target per role from your own time data, review it quarterly, and plan against it rather than against full availability.

Can a spreadsheet do capacity planning?

Yes, for a small team with a few clients. It breaks down when several people edit schedules, leave changes often, or you need to model tentative work, which is when a dedicated tool earns its cost.

Should freelancers be included in the capacity plan?

Yes, if they work regularly on client projects. Add them as people or placeholders with their actual availability, so the plan reflects the hours you can really draw on.

Does capacity planning need to integrate with time tracking?

It helps a great deal. Comparing planned with actual hours is how you correct estimates, so check for a native integration or an import route before you buy.

Who should own capacity planning in a small agency?

Usually an operations lead or the most senior delivery manager, with sales feeding in pipeline. What matters is one named owner who runs the weekly review.
