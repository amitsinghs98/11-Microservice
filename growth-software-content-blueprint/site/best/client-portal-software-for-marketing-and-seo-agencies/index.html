Which client portal software should an agency choose?

Pick a dedicated portal if clients need a branded front door for files, approvals, messages and invoices. Use the client access in your project tool if clients only need to follow projects and approve tasks.

  • Branded portal with messaging, files and billing in one place: Copilot.
  • Portal built around agency projects and client requests: SuperOkay.
  • Productized or request-based services (design, content, SEO deliverables): ManyRequests.
  • Secure document workspaces for more formal clients: Clinked.
  • Structured client workflows such as onboarding steps and sign-offs: Moxo.
  • Client access inside an agency project management tool: Teamwork.com.
  • Simple per-project client visibility for small studios: Basecamp.

Do you need a portal, or is guest access enough?

Guest access is enough when clients only need to see progress and approve tasks inside your project tool. A portal earns its cost when you want a controlled, branded experience that hides internal work and joins up files, approvals, messages and invoices.

The risk with guest access is exposure. Clients may see internal comments, estimates or other clients' names if permissions are set up hastily, and they have to learn your tool's interface. The risk with a portal is duplication: if tasks live in one place and the client-facing status in another, someone has to keep them in sync.

What does each portal do well, and what does it miss?

Dedicated portals are strongest on the client experience; PM tools are strongest on keeping one source of truth for the work. Choose based on which of those your clients complain about.

Copilot: best for a branded client portal with billing

Copilot positions itself as a client portal that brings messaging, files, forms, contracts and billing into a branded space. Its strength is a polished client experience without building one yourself. The limitation is that it doesn't replace your project management tool, so tasks still live elsewhere. Skip it if clients only need to see a project timeline. Check plan tiers on the Copilot pricing page.

SuperOkay: best for showing agency projects to clients

SuperOkay positions itself for agencies and freelancers who want clients to see projects, tasks, files and updates in a branded space. It suits small teams that want portal and lightweight project tracking together. Skip it if you already run detailed delivery in a heavier PM tool and only need a front end. See the SuperOkay pricing page.

ManyRequests: best for request-based services

ManyRequests is built for productized services, where clients submit requests, track status and download deliverables. It fits design, content and SEO deliverable agencies with defined service menus. Skip it if most of your work is bespoke campaigns. Plan details are on the ManyRequests pricing page.

Clinked: best for secure document workspaces

Clinked focuses on secure client workspaces for files, discussions and tasks, which suits agencies with more formal or regulated clients. It's less tailored to agency delivery than the options above. Skip it if your clients want a fast, informal experience. Confirm security features on the Clinked pricing page and documentation.

Moxo: best for structured client workflows

Moxo frames its product around client interaction workflows, such as onboarding steps, document collection and approvals. That's useful when every client goes through the same sequence. Skip it if you're a small agency that needs a simple shared space. See the Moxo pricing page.

Teamwork.com: best when client access inside PM is enough

Teamwork.com lets agencies invite clients into projects with controlled permissions, which keeps one source of truth. The client sees your project tool rather than a purpose-built portal. Skip it if branding and invoicing in the portal matter. Check client-user rules on the Teamwork.com pricing page.

Basecamp: best for simple, calm client visibility

Basecamp lets you invite clients into a project and choose what they see. It's simple and readable, but it isn't built for invoicing or a fully branded portal. Skip it if you want a formal portal experience. See the Basecamp pricing page.

How should a portal handle onboarding and monthly reporting?

Use the portal as the single place where a new client completes onboarding and where every monthly report lands. If those two moments run through the portal, clients form the habit of logging in.

Onboarding map:

  1. Welcome message and named contacts, posted on day one.
  2. Intake form for goals, brand assets and access to analytics and ad accounts.
  3. Signed agreement and first invoice, linked from the portal (see our guides to e-signature software for agency contracts and invoicing software for agencies).
  4. Kick-off agenda and the first deliverable plan.

Monthly reporting map: publish the report in the same folder each month, post a short summary message with the three decisions you need, and attach any assets awaiting approval. Will clients really use a portal instead of email?

Not automatically: clients use a portal when it's the fastest way to get what they need, and they ignore it when email still works. Based on the product information reviewed, portal features matter less than the agency's discipline in routing approvals and reports through it.

Which integrations and mobile features matter most?

Prioritise links to your project tool, file storage, accounting and calendar, because those stop double entry. Mobile access matters for client approvals if your clients review on a phone between meetings.

Check whether the portal connects natively to your stack or relies on automation platforms. Also confirm notification settings, so clients aren't flooded with alerts for every internal change.

What mistakes do agencies make with client portals?

The biggest mistake is launching a portal while still sending the same files by email. The second is giving every client the same structure without checking what each one actually needs.

  • Promising white-label features before confirming which plan includes them.
  • Leaving permission defaults in place, so clients see internal notes.
  • Running two sources of truth for task status without an owner.
  • Skipping a client-facing guide to logging in and approving.

What else do agencies ask about client portals?

The recurring questions are about guest access, white-labelling and data security. Answers below are general; confirm each vendor's current terms on its official site.

What is the difference between a client portal and guest access?

A portal is a client-facing space you design for the client, often branded, with only what they need to see. Guest access lets a client into your internal project tool with limited permissions, which is quicker to set up but shows them your working environment.

Can a client portal use our agency's own domain and branding?

Some dedicated portals offer custom branding or a custom domain, and availability can depend on the plan. Check exactly which elements can be white-labelled, including emails and login pages, before promising it to clients.

Should invoices go through the client portal?

Putting invoices in the portal gives clients one place to find everything, which can reduce chasing. Only do it if the portal's billing connects cleanly to your accounting system, otherwise you create a second record to reconcile.

Do clients actually log in to portals?

Adoption depends on whether the portal is the only place to get approvals and reports. If you keep emailing the same files, clients have no reason to log in.

What security questions should we ask a portal vendor?

Ask about single sign-on, two-factor authentication, permission levels per file and folder, audit logs and where data is stored. Get the answers in writing from the vendor's official documentation or security page.
