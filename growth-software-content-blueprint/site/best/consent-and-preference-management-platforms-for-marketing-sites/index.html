None of them makes a site compliant on its own; that depends on your configuration and legal advice.

Which consent and preference platform should you choose?

If you mainly need a compliant-looking cookie banner, script blocking and consent records on one or two sites, start with Cookiebot or CookieYes. If you also manage email and communication preferences for known contacts across systems, look at Didomi, Osano or OneTrust, which add preference centers and integrations to CRM and marketing tools.

  • Small and mid-size marketing sites wanting a quick banner and scanner: CookieYes or Cookiebot.
  • Teams running Google Ads and Analytics in Europe: Cookiebot or another Usercentrics CMP, given its Google consent mode partnership.
  • Brands collecting topic and channel preferences from customers: Didomi.
  • Mid-market teams wanting consent, preferences and privacy requests in one account: Osano.
  • Large, multi-region organizations with a privacy office: OneTrust.

What's the difference between cookie consent and preference management?

Cookie consent covers what anonymous visitors allow your site to store and track, usually through a banner. Preference management covers what known people want to receive from you, such as newsletter topics, channels and frequency, and keeps that choice in sync with your email platform and CRM.

The vendors draw the same line. OneTrust, for example, describes its consent and preferences suite as a consent management platform for unknown visitors alongside a universal consent and preference management product for known customers. Many marketing sites need only the first; teams with large email programs usually need both. If you're tidying the email side too, see our shortlist of marketing automation software for small teams.

What does each platform do well, and where does it fall short?

Lean tools such as CookieYes and Cookiebot get a banner, scanner and consent log running quickly, while Didomi, Osano and OneTrust go further into preferences, integrations and privacy operations. The trade-off is cost and setup effort, which rise sharply at the enterprise end.

CookieYes: best for small sites that need a banner fast

CookieYes provides a consent banner, a website scanner that updates the banner's cookie list and a consent log of visitors' choices. Limitations: check which scanning and logging features your plan includes, and it isn't built for cross-channel preference management. Skip it if you need to sync communication preferences into a CRM.

Cookiebot by Usercentrics: best for Google advertisers in Europe

Cookiebot is Usercentrics' CMP for smaller and mid-size sites. Cookiebot's site promotes its participation in Google's consent mode CMP partner program, and Usercentrics announced that Google certified its CMPs for publishers. That matters if you serve Google ads to European visitors; confirm Google's current CMP requirements in Google's own documentation. Limitations: large sites should check how tiers are sized before committing, and preference-center depth is lighter than the enterprise tools. Skip it if your main need is email preference management.

Osano: best for mid-market teams adding privacy requests

Osano offers cookie consent alongside a unified consent and preference hub that extends choices to email and marketing preferences and pushes updates to connected systems and Osano also markets tools for handling privacy requests. Limitations: the useful setup may span several products, so price the full set, not just the banner. Skip it if you only need a banner on a brochure site.

Didomi: best for preference centers for known customers

Didomi sells a CMP and a separate preference management platform with preference widgets, full-page preference centers and embedded forms, and integrations into CRM and marketing tools. Limitations: check how plans are sold on its offers page, and getting value from preferences requires clean data flows into your email platform. Skip it if you have no email or CRM program to feed.

OneTrust: best for enterprise privacy programs

OneTrust offers a CMP and a universal consent and preference management product that keeps consent records, with preference centers and integrations into large marketing and data platforms. Limitations: it's a broad enterprise platform with matching implementation effort; confirm pricing with the vendor. Skip it if you're a small team without a privacy owner to run it.

Will a consent platform make your marketing site compliant?

No. A platform gives you the mechanisms (a banner, script blocking, records and a preference center), but whether they meet the law depends on how you configure them, which scripts actually wait for consent and what your notices say.

The most common gap is technical rather than legal: a tag fires before consent because it was hard-coded in a template or added through a plug-in outside the tag manager. Audit what loads before a visitor chooses, then repeat after every site release.

How should consent control your marketing scripts?

Every non-essential script, including analytics, ad pixels, pop-ups, chat and heatmaps, should load only after the matching consent category is granted. Route those tags through your tag manager and the CMP's categories so a new script can't bypass the banner.

Check how each platform blocks scripts (automatic blocking, tag-manager triggers or manual tagging), and how it passes consent signals to Google tags. What should you confirm with privacy counsel?

Confirm which regions' rules apply, what the banner must offer in each, how long to keep consent records and what your notices must say. Counsel decides those questions; the platform only implements the answers.

  • Which jurisdictions your visitors and customers are in, and whether opt-in or opt-out applies to each.
  • Whether "reject all" must be as prominent as "accept all" on the first layer.
  • How to classify each cookie and script, and who signs off new ones.
  • How long to keep consent records, and in what form you'd produce them for a regulator.
  • How to handle browser privacy signals, where they apply.
  • How preference changes reach email, CRM and ad audiences, and how fast.
  • What contract terms and data-processing agreement the vendor offers.

What else do buyers ask about consent and preference platforms?

These answers cover the practical questions marketing teams ask before buying. None of them is legal advice.

Do I need a consent platform if I only use Google Analytics?

If you have visitors from regions that require consent for analytics cookies, you likely need a way to collect and record that consent. Confirm the requirement for your audience with privacy counsel.

What is Google consent mode?

It's Google's method for passing a visitor's consent choices to Google tags so they adjust their behavior. Certified consent platforms can send those signals for you.

Can one platform handle several websites?

Most can, but plans are often priced per domain or by site size. Check how the platform counts domains and subpages before you commit.

Should the preference center live in the consent platform or the email tool?

If preferences must sync across several systems, a central preference center helps. If you only use one email platform, its own preference page may be enough.

What's the verdict?

For a typical marketing site, Cookiebot or CookieYes covers the banner, scanning and consent records at the lowest setup effort. Move to Osano, Didomi or OneTrust when preferences for known contacts, privacy requests or many regions become the real problem.
