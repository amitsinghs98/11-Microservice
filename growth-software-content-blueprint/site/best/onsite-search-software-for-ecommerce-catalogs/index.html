Which onsite search tool should you choose?

Choose by catalog size and by who will own search: developers, merchandisers or nobody in particular. Developer-led and large catalogs fit Algolia or Constructor, merchandiser-led mid-market teams fit Searchspring or Klevu, and smaller stores fit Doofinder, Boost or Searchanise.

  • Developer-built, fully controlled search: Algolia.
  • Large catalogs ranking for revenue: Constructor.
  • Search plus personalisation suite: Bloomreach Discovery.
  • Mid-market AI search with low build effort: Klevu.
  • Merchandiser-controlled results: Searchspring.
  • Quick install on many platforms: Doofinder.
  • Shopify filters and collection pages: Boost.
  • Low-cost first upgrade: Searchanise.

Who is this list for, and what should search fix?

What do synonyms, typo tolerance, facets and zero-result recovery mean in practice?

They're the four features that decide whether a shopper finds a product: matching different words for the same thing, forgiving spelling mistakes, narrowing results with filters, and offering a sensible alternative when nothing matches. Every tool here claims them; the differences are in how much control you get and how much is automatic.

  • Synonyms: "sofa" and "couch", or "trainers" and "sneakers", should return the same products. Check whether synonyms are learned automatically, edited manually, or both.
  • Typo tolerance: "addidas" should still find Adidas, but tolerance must not turn a correct short query into wrong matches.
  • Facets: filters such as size, colour and price, built from clean product attributes.
  • Zero-result recovery: spelling suggestions, related categories or popular products instead of an empty page.
  • Merchandising: rules to pin, boost or bury products for specific queries or campaigns.

Will better search actually lift revenue on your store?

It can, but only if search is used heavily and currently fails often; a store where few visitors search will see little change. Check your own search usage and zero-result rate before you believe any vendor uplift figure.

How do you run a query audit and a merchandising test before buying?

Export your top search queries and your zero-result queries, then run the same list through each shortlisted tool in a trial or sandbox. Score the results yourself; a vendor demo using its own sample catalog proves little.

  1. Pull the top 100 queries by volume and every query that returned zero results over the last 90 days from your analytics.
  2. Add 20 hard cases: misspellings, synonyms, plural forms, SKUs, and multi-attribute queries such as "black waterproof boots size 9".
  3. Index your real catalog in each trial, then record whether the first row of results is relevant for each query.
  4. Run one merchandising test: pin a product for a seasonal query, then time how long it takes a merchandiser to do it without help.
  5. Check analytics: can you see zero-result queries, click-through by query and revenue from search sessions?
  6. Ask how pricing scales as your catalog, query volume and languages grow.

What are the strengths and limits of each search tool?

Core matching is similar across the field; the differences are who controls results, how much is automated and how pricing scales. Each block names who should pick the tool and who should skip it.

Algolia: best for teams with developers who want full control

Algolia is a hosted search and discovery API with typo tolerance, synonyms, faceting, merchandising rules and analytics, plus front-end libraries for building the search interface. It suits brands that want search tuned precisely and have developers to build and maintain it.

Pricing: not quoted here; check the official Algolia site for how it meters usage (searches, records, SKUs or a quote).

Limitations: flexibility means build work; it's not a drop-in app for a small store. Usage-based pricing needs modelling against your query and catalog volume.

Skip it if nobody on your team can own a search implementation.

Constructor: best for large catalogs optimising for revenue

Constructor is an enterprise product discovery platform covering search, browse, autocomplete and recommendations, positioned around ranking results by business outcomes such as conversion and revenue rather than text relevance alone. It suits large retailers with high query volumes.

Pricing: not quoted here; check the official Constructor site for how it meters usage (searches, records, SKUs or a quote).

Limitations: enterprise sales and implementation; small catalogs won't generate enough behavioural data to use its learning features fully.

Skip it if you have a few hundred SKUs.

Bloomreach Discovery: best for enterprise teams that also want personalisation

Bloomreach Discovery provides search, merchandising and category-page ranking as part of a wider suite that includes marketing automation. It suits large brands that want search and personalisation from one vendor.

Pricing: not quoted here; check the official Bloomreach site for how it meters usage (searches, records, SKUs or a quote).

Limitations: suite pricing and scope can exceed what a search-only project needs.

Skip it if you only need better on-site search.

Klevu: best for mid-market stores wanting AI search without heavy build

Klevu offers ecommerce search with natural-language handling, merchandising controls and integrations for platforms such as Shopify, BigCommerce and Magento. It suits mid-market teams that want strong search without building on an API.

Pricing: not quoted here; check the official Klevu site for how it meters usage (searches, records, SKUs or a quote).

Limitations: Klevu and Searchspring are now under common ownership, so ask about each product's roadmap before you sign a long contract.

Skip it if you need a fully custom front end built from APIs.

Searchspring: best for merchandisers who want hands-on control

Searchspring combines site search, category merchandising and personalisation with a strong focus on giving merchandisers rules, boosts and campaign controls. It suits teams where a merchandiser, not a developer, owns search results.

Pricing: not quoted here; check the official Searchspring site for how it meters usage (searches, records, SKUs or a quote).

Limitations: it's a fit for mid-market and up; as with Klevu, check the roadmap given the shared ownership.

Skip it if you want fully automated ranking with little manual control.

Doofinder: best for small and mid-sized stores across many platforms

Doofinder is a hosted search layer that installs on many ecommerce platforms with little code, and it covers typo tolerance, synonyms, facets and basic merchandising. It's a practical choice for stores that want better search quickly.

Pricing: not quoted here; check the official Doofinder site for how it meters usage (searches, records, SKUs or a quote).

Limitations: less enterprise-grade relevance tuning and fewer advanced features than Algolia or Constructor.

Skip it if you have a very large catalog or complex B2B pricing.

Boost AI Search & Discovery: best for Shopify stores that want search and filters in one app

Boost is a Shopify app for product filters, search and merchandising on collection pages. It suits Shopify stores whose biggest problem is filtering large collections rather than free-text search.

Pricing: not quoted here; check the official Boost site for how it meters usage (searches, records, SKUs or a quote).

Limitations: Shopify only, and heavy filter setups need care to avoid slow collection pages.

Skip it if you're not on Shopify.

Searchanise: best for budget-conscious stores

Searchanise is a search and filter app for Shopify, BigCommerce and other platforms, with autocomplete, synonyms, filters and analytics at a lower entry cost. It suits small stores improving on default search for the first time.

Pricing: not quoted here; check the official Searchanise site for how it meters usage (searches, records, SKUs or a quote).

Limitations: fewer merchandising and relevance controls than mid-market tools.

Skip it if search drives a large share of your revenue and needs ongoing tuning.

What mistakes do stores make when buying onsite search?

The biggest mistake is blaming the search engine for messy product data; no tool can facet on attributes you don't have. The second is buying advanced relevance features with no one assigned to use them.

  • Skipping the query audit and judging tools on a vendor demo.
  • Not checking how pricing scales with SKUs, records, queries or languages.
  • Leaving zero-result queries unreviewed after launch; schedule a monthly check.
  • Measuring search in isolation instead of alongside category and merchandising performance; see our shortlist of merchandising analytics tools.

What else do buyers ask about ecommerce onsite search software?

Most questions concern catalog size, languages and whether platform search is enough. Short answers follow; confirm details with each vendor.

Is my ecommerce platform's built-in search good enough?

For small catalogs with simple product names, often yes. Upgrade when zero-result queries are common, shoppers misspell product names, or merchandisers need control over results.

How is onsite search software usually priced?

Common models charge by search requests, indexed records or SKUs, or by quote for enterprise contracts. Model your current and next-year volumes before comparing offers.

Does onsite search support multiple languages?

Most of the tools here support several languages, but the depth of stemming and synonym handling varies by language. Test your real queries in each language during the trial.

What is a good zero-result rate?

There's no universal benchmark, so track your own rate over time. Review the zero-result queries each month and fix them with synonyms, redirects or new products.

What's the verdict?

Small stores should try Doofinder, Searchanise or, on Shopify, Boost before paying for anything bigger. Mid-market teams with a merchandiser should shortlist Searchspring and Klevu, and large or developer-led catalogs should compare Algolia and Constructor.
