Which AI image generator should your ad creative team choose?

Choose Adobe Firefly if commercial-safety positioning and Photoshop integration matter most, Midjourney if art direction and visual range come first, and ChatGPT or Gemini if your team wants to edit images conversationally. Use Ideogram for ads with text in the image, Recraft for vector and illustration work, and Photoroom for product photography.

  • Brand-safe production inside Adobe workflows: Adobe Firefly.
  • Concept art and mood boards: Midjourney.
  • Iterating on a draft by describing changes: ChatGPT image generation or Google Gemini.
  • Headlines and typography inside the image: Ideogram.
  • Custom-trained styles and fine control: Leonardo.ai.
  • Vector graphics, icons and brand palettes: Recraft.
  • Ecommerce product shots and backgrounds: Photoroom.

What separates an ad-production tool from a generic image generator?

Ad production needs repeatability: reference images that lock a style or subject, inpainting to fix one area, and output sizes that match each placement. A generic generator gives you one striking image; an ad tool gives you thirty consistent ones.

Assess each candidate on six controls. Prompt control: does it follow layout and negative instructions? Reference images: can you supply a style, product or character reference? Inpainting and outpainting: can you edit a region or extend the canvas? Output sizes: does it produce the aspect ratios your placements need, at usable resolution? Licensing: what do the terms say about commercial use and ownership? Credit limits: how many usable variants does a month's allowance really produce?

Which tool fits which ad-production job?

Firefly and Photoroom fit production, where the brief is fixed and the product must look right. Midjourney, Leonardo.ai and Recraft fit exploration and style, and the conversational tools fit fast iteration.

Adobe Firefly: best for teams already in Photoshop and Express

Adobe positions its own Firefly models as designed for commercial use and trained on content it has rights to use, and it builds generative fill and expand into Photoshop. Style and composition references help keep variants consistent. Usage draws on generative credits by plan; see Adobe's Firefly page.

Strengths: editing control inside tools designers already use. Limitations: the Firefly app also offers partner models, and Adobe's commercial-safety positioning may not extend to them, so check which model made each asset. Skip it if nobody on the team uses Adobe software.

Midjourney: best for concept work and art direction

Midjourney produces highly stylized images and supports style and image references, plus an editor for changing regions and extending the frame. It's sold as subscription tiers; read Midjourney's site and terms for how commercial use and image visibility differ by tier and company size.

Strengths: range and aesthetic quality for mood boards. Limitations: exact product fidelity and in-image text are harder, and there's no enterprise-style asset governance. Skip it if every image must show your real product accurately.

ChatGPT image generation: best for iterating by conversation

ChatGPT generates and edits images inside a chat, so you can ask for one change at a time and keep context between versions. Access and limits depend on the ChatGPT plan, and developers can use OpenAI's image models through the API; see ChatGPT's pricing page.

Strengths: low learning curve and decent text handling for quick drafts. Limitations: limited batch production and brand-asset management. Skip it if you need dozens of size variants from one master.

Google Gemini: best for teams living in Google Workspace

Google's Gemini app generates and edits images from prompts and uploaded photos, and Google's image models are also available to developers through its cloud platform. Google says it marks generated images with an invisible SynthID watermark. See Google Gemini for plan access.

Strengths: convenient editing of existing photos. Limitations: feature access changes by plan and region, so confirm what your workspace gets. Skip it if you need a dedicated creative workspace with brand kits.

Ideogram: best for images with readable text

Ideogram is known for rendering typography inside images, which suits posters, offers and social ads with a headline. It also offers region editing and canvas extension. Plans are subscription with credits; see Ideogram's site.

Strengths: fewer redraws for text-led ads. Limitations: a narrower editing toolset than Adobe's. Skip it if your ads rarely carry text in the image.

Leonardo.ai: best for custom styles and granular control

Leonardo.ai, now part of Canva, offers multiple models, trainable custom styles, image guidance and a canvas for inpainting, plus an API. Plans are token-based; see Leonardo.ai's site.

Strengths: control for teams willing to tune settings. Limitations: more knobs mean more training time. Skip it if generalist marketers will be the main users.

Recraft: best for vectors, icons and illustration systems

Recraft generates vector and raster images, exports SVG and lets you set brand colors and custom styles, which helps keep an illustration set consistent. See Recraft's site for plans.

Strengths: editable vector output. Limitations: weaker for photoreal lifestyle scenes. Skip it if your ads are mostly photography.

Photoroom: best for ecommerce product imagery

Photoroom removes backgrounds, generates AI backgrounds and scenes around a real product photo, and supports batch editing, with an API for catalog-scale work. See Photoroom's site for plans.

Strengths: the product stays your real product. Limitations: it's built for product shots, not open-ended concepts. Skip it if you don't sell physical goods.

Will generated images actually stay on-brand across a campaign?

Only with reference images, a saved style and a human checking every variant; no vendor page we reviewed promises consistency across a full campaign. Drift shows up first in faces, hands, product details and color accuracy.

Can you use AI-generated images in paid ads?

Usually, if the vendor's terms allow commercial use on your plan and the image doesn't reproduce someone else's trademark, likeness or copyrighted work. Ownership and copyright protection for AI output remain legally unsettled, so get legal review before high-spend campaigns.

  • Read the commercial-use, ownership and indemnity sections of each vendor's terms for your exact plan.
  • Don't prompt for real people, competitor logos, celebrity likeness or named artists' styles.
  • Keep a record of the tool, model, prompt and date for each published asset.
  • Check each ad platform's policies on synthetic or altered imagery.
  • Have trademarks and product claims reviewed as you would any other ad.

How do you run a repeatable creative test across tools?

Give every tool the same brief, the same reference images and the same three placements, then score consistency, edit effort and usable output per credit. This is a protocol for you to run; we haven't run it.

  1. Write one brief: product, audience, offer, mandatory brand colors and a headline.
  2. Supply the same product photo and one style reference to each tool.
  3. Request a square feed image, a vertical story image and a wide banner.
  4. Make two edits per image: replace the background and change one object.
  5. Score each result for brand match, product accuracy, text legibility and minutes of retouching.
  6. Record the credits used and divide by the number of usable images.

Images that pass then need a home; ad creative management platforms handle versioning and performance tagging at scale.

How do credit limits and team workflow affect the choice?

Credits decide volume and workflow decides speed, so model both before you compare prices. A tool with a generous allowance but no shared libraries can cost more in designer time than it saves.

Estimate the usable variants you need each month, then multiply by the number of attempts a usable image typically takes in your own trial. Check whether high-resolution output, upscaling or editing draws extra credits. On workflow, ask whether the team shares styles and references, whether a reviewer can comment, and whether assets export cleanly to your design and approval tools. What else do ad creative teams ask about AI image generation?

Rights, disclosure and replacing photographers come up most. Here are direct answers.

Do we own the images an AI tool generates?

Vendor terms usually assign or license output rights to you, but copyright protection for AI output is unsettled in many jurisdictions, so treat ownership as a legal question.

Do AI-generated ads need to be labeled?

Some ad platforms and jurisdictions require disclosure of synthetic or materially altered imagery, especially of people, so check each platform's current policy before launch.

Can AI image generation replace product photography?

For backgrounds and scenes around a real product photo, often yes; for the product itself, keep real photography so customers see what they will receive.

Should we buy one image tool or several?

Most teams do best with one production tool plus, at most, one exploration tool, because every extra subscription adds a style library and a rights review to maintain.
