Which shared inbox should a customer-facing team choose?

Front is the strongest choice for teams that run many aliases across support, sales and operations with rules and SLAs, while Hiver suits teams that want to stay inside Gmail. Help Scout fits support-led teams that may want a knowledge base and chat later, Missive fits small teams that also want shared chat, and a native Google or Microsoft shared mailbox is enough only at low volume.

  • Many aliases, rules and SLAs across departments: Front.
  • Teams who want to work in Gmail without a new app: Hiver.
  • Support-led teams wanting docs and chat on the same platform: Help Scout.
  • Small teams mixing email, team chat and SMS: Missive.
  • Sales and service working from the same CRM record: HubSpot's inbox.
  • Very small teams on Google Workspace with low volume: Google Groups collaborative inbox.
  • Very small teams on Microsoft 365 with low volume: Outlook shared mailbox.

How is a shared inbox different from a ticketing suite?

Shared inbox software lets a team work a common email address as a group, with assignment, internal notes, collision warnings and tags, while the customer still sees an ordinary email conversation. A ticketing suite adds structured tickets, customer portals, complex routing and deep reporting, which help larger support teams but add setup and cost.

That distinction matters because shared inboxes serve more than support. Sales teams work inbound@ and partner aliases, finance works billing@ and accounts payable, and operations works vendor and logistics threads. A full help desk can feel heavy for those teams, and it often bills every member as a support agent.

The line blurs as tools grow: Front and Help Scout both add SLAs and reporting, and help desks keep adding inbox-style views. What does each shared inbox do well, and where does it fall short?

Front and Hiver are the most complete shared inboxes for mixed departments, Help Scout leans toward support, Missive toward small collaborative teams and HubSpot toward CRM-centered work. The native mailboxes are a starting point, not a destination, once volume grows.

Front: best for many aliases across departments

Front brings email, chat, SMS and social channels into shared and personal inboxes with assignment, internal comments, collision indicators, tags, rules, SLAs and analytics, plus a broad integration library. It's built for teams beyond support, including sales, account management and operations. Limitation: rules and analytics depth, and some admin controls, sit in higher tiers. Skip it if you have one alias and three people. See Front's pricing page.

Hiver: best for staying inside Gmail

Hiver turns Gmail labels into shared inboxes with assignment, notes, collision alerts, tags, SLAs, templates and analytics, so people work in an interface they already know. Limitation: it's built for Google Workspace, so mixed or Outlook teams need another option. Skip it if you want to move people out of email into a single dedicated workspace. See Hiver's pricing page.

Help Scout: best for support-led teams

Help Scout's inbox offers assignment, notes, collision detection, saved replies, workflows and customer properties, with Docs for a knowledge base and Beacon for chat on the same platform. Limitation: it's shaped around support, so sales teams may find pipeline views missing. Skip it if the main users are finance or operations teams who never need a help center. See Help Scout's pricing page.

Missive: best for small teams mixing email and team chat

Missive combines shared email accounts with team chat, comments on any conversation, assignment, rules and integrations, including SMS and messaging channels. It suits small agencies and operations teams who discuss emails before replying. Limitation: reporting and enterprise controls are thinner than Front's. Skip it if you need formal SLA reporting for leadership. See Missive's pricing page.

HubSpot inbox: best for CRM-centered conversations

HubSpot's conversations inbox brings shared email, chat and forms into one view, assigns conversations to owners and logs every message on the contact record, so sales and service see the same history. Limitation: routing and reporting depth depend on paid Sales or Service Hub seats. Skip it if you use another CRM. See HubSpot's inbox page.

Google Groups collaborative inbox: best for very small Google teams

Google Workspace lets admins set up a group as a collaborative inbox where members can assign conversations and mark them resolved. It costs nothing extra. Limitation: it lacks dependable collision warnings, rich internal notes, SLAs and reporting. Skip it if customers expect a reply within hours. See Google's admin documentation.

Outlook shared mailbox: best for very small Microsoft 365 teams

A Microsoft 365 shared mailbox lets a group read and send from a common address with shared calendar and contacts, set up by an admin. Limitation: there's no assignment workflow, collision detection or response-time reporting built in. Skip it if more than a few people answer it. See Microsoft's documentation.

Which shared inbox features matter most when you shortlist?

Assignment, collision detection and internal notes are the minimum; SLAs, audit trails and integrations decide whether the tool scales past a handful of people. Templates and tags are table stakes in every dedicated tool.

  • Assignment: manual, round-robin and rule-based, with a clear owner on every open conversation.
  • Collision detection: a visible warning when a teammate is viewing or drafting a reply.
  • Internal notes and mentions: discussion on the email thread itself, not in a separate chat.
  • Tags: consistent categories for reporting, ideally applied by rules.
  • SLAs: targets and breach alerts by inbox, customer tier or tag.
  • Templates: shared, owned and versioned, with variables for names and account details.
  • Integrations: CRM, billing and project tools, so context appears beside the email.
  • Audit trails: who was assigned, who replied and when, especially for finance aliases.
  • Per-user pricing: how the vendor treats occasional users and whether every alias needs its own seat.

Will a shared inbox really stop duplicate and missed replies?

The vendors document assignment and collision warnings designed to prevent both, but the tools only work if every message has an owner and the team agrees on when to assign. We can't measure how much any tool reduces errors from public materials alone.

How should you route several aliases in one shared inbox?

Give each alias its own inbox with a named owning team, route by rules on alias, sender and keywords, and keep one triage view for anything the rules can't place. Review the rules monthly, because a routing rule nobody owns becomes a hiding place for missed email.

A hypothetical design for a 15-person company with five aliases:

  1. support@ goes to the support inbox, round-robin among on-shift agents, with an SLA by customer tier taken from the CRM.
  2. sales@ and partners@ go to the sales inbox; known contacts route to the account owner, new senders to a rotating rep.
  3. billing@ goes to a restricted finance inbox, visible only to finance and the support lead, with every action in the audit trail.
  4. ops@ goes to operations, tagged by vendor, with no SLA but a weekly unresolved-thread review.
  5. Catch-all triage: anything matching no rule lands in a shared view the support lead clears daily.

When a conversation crosses teams, reassign it rather than forwarding, so the history stays in one thread. If the website also feeds conversations into the inbox, our guide to live chat software for leads and support covers routing chat alongside email.

What else do buyers ask about shared inbox software?

The usual questions are about native mailboxes, occasional users, departments sharing one tool and migrating old email. Short answers follow; confirm current details with each vendor.

Is a Google Group or Microsoft 365 shared mailbox enough?

For two or three people handling low volume, often yes. You'll miss reliable assignment, collision warnings, internal notes on the email itself and response-time reporting, which is usually the point at which teams move to a dedicated tool.

Do customers see that we're using a shared inbox?

Generally no. Replies go out from the shared address, such as support@ or billing@, and internal notes and assignments stay internal, but check how each tool handles signatures and reply-from settings.

Can one shared inbox tool serve sales, support and finance?

Yes, if it supports separate inboxes or teams with their own permissions and rules. Keep finance and HR aliases restricted to the people who need them, and check that the audit trail shows who viewed and replied.

How do shared inbox tools charge for occasional users?

Most charge per user, and some offer cheaper or free seats for collaborators who only comment. Check the vendor's definition carefully, because a manager who sometimes replies may need a full seat.

What should we do with old email when we switch?

Decide whether you need history in the new tool or only going forward. Many tools can import recent mail from a connected account, but confirm the import window and whether tags and notes carry over before you commit.

What's the verdict on shared inbox software?

Front is the best fit for multi-department teams that need rules, SLAs and audit trails across many aliases, and Hiver is the best fit for teams who want those features without leaving Gmail. Help Scout suits support-led teams, Missive suits small teams who talk through email together, and HubSpot's inbox makes sense when HubSpot is already your CRM.

Start with a native shared mailbox only if volume is low and nobody asks for response-time reports, and move up once missed or duplicated replies appear.
