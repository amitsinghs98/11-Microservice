Which content marketing platform should a B2B team choose?

Pick HubSpot Content Hub if your CRM is already HubSpot and you want site, blog and attribution in one place, and pick Optimizely Content Marketing Platform if a large team needs formal briefs, approvals and an asset library before anything is published. Smaller teams usually get more from a calendar-first tool such as CoSchedule or a write-and-distribute tool such as StoryChief than from an enterprise suite.

  • Content tied to CRM and pipeline reporting: HubSpot Content Hub.
  • Enterprise planning, workflow and governance: Optimizely Content Marketing Platform.
  • A shared marketing calendar for a small team: CoSchedule.
  • Writing once and publishing to blog, social and newsletter: StoryChief.
  • Teams that need writers as well as software: Contently.
  • Structuring and approving copy before it reaches the CMS: Content Workflow by Bynder.

All of these sit within the wider content operations software hub, which maps the neighbouring categories covered below.

What separates a content platform from a CMS or an AI writer?

A content marketing platform manages the work around content: ideas, briefs, assignments, approvals, scheduling and performance. A CMS stores and renders the published page, and an AI writer drafts text; neither one tells you who owns the next step or whether a piece shipped on time.

The confusion matters because vendors blur the lines. HubSpot bundles a CMS with content tools, several platforms now include AI drafting, and some CMSs add simple workflow states. Decide which job is actually broken before you shortlist. If publishing is the bottleneck, read our guide to CMS platforms for growing marketing teams; if drafting speed is the issue, an AI writing tool with human editorial checks is the cheaper fix.

What does each platform do well, and where does it fall short?

Each platform below is strongest at one stage of the content lifecycle and weakest at another, so read the "skip it if" line before the strengths. Fit judgments are our editorial assessment; product descriptions should be checked against the linked vendor pages.

HubSpot Content Hub: best when content has to be tied to CRM records

Content Hub combines HubSpot's website and blog CMS with content tools, so a post, the form on it and the contact it creates live in one system. For B2B teams, the practical draw is reporting that connects content to contacts and deals already in the HubSpot CRM. Tiers and seat rules are listed on the HubSpot Content Hub pricing page.

Strengths: one record from first visit to closed deal, and no separate CMS to maintain. Limitations: editorial workflow is lighter than a dedicated planning tool, and the value drops sharply if sales runs on another CRM. Skip it if your website lives on WordPress or Webflow and you don't plan to move it.

Optimizely Content Marketing Platform: best for governed, multi-team programs

Optimizely CMP, formerly known as Welcome, is built for enterprise content operations: request intake, briefs, task workflows, approvals, a shared calendar and an asset library. It suits marketing departments where legal, product and regional teams all touch a piece before it ships. Pricing is by quote through the Optimizely CMP product page.

Strengths: the deepest workflow and governance on this list. Limitations: it needs an owner, configuration time and buy-in across teams. Skip it if fewer than ten people touch content, because the overhead will outweigh the control.

CoSchedule: best for a small team that needs one shared calendar

CoSchedule centres on a marketing calendar where blog posts, social messages and campaigns sit side by side, with tasks attached to each item. It is the easiest of these to roll out, and it suits teams whose main pain is visibility rather than approvals. Check current tiers on the CoSchedule pricing page.

Strengths: quick to adopt and easy for stakeholders to read. Limitations: structured briefs, review rounds and permissions are less developed than in enterprise tools. Skip it if compliance review is your bottleneck; our list of editorial calendar software for multi-author teams covers stronger assignment options.

StoryChief: best for writing once and distributing everywhere

StoryChief combines a collaborative editor with publishing to connected CMSs, social channels and newsletters, so one approved piece can go out across channels without copy-pasting. It also offers workspaces for agencies and multi-brand teams. Plans are listed on the StoryChief pricing page.

Strengths: it shortens the gap between "approved" and "live". Limitations: performance reporting stays at the channel level, so pipeline attribution still needs your CRM. Skip it if you publish to a single blog and nowhere else. For more on channel reach, see our guide to B2B content distribution and syndication platforms.

Contently: best for teams that need writers as well as software

Contently pairs a content platform with access to a freelance creator network and managed services. That combination fits a B2B team that has budget but no in-house writers. Pricing is by quote via Contently's website.

Strengths: one contract for planning, production and talent. Limitations: you pay for services you won't use if your writers are already in place. Skip it if the problem is coordination, not capacity.

Content Workflow by Bynder: best for structuring copy before it reaches the CMS

Content Workflow, formerly GatherContent, lets teams build structured templates, collect copy against them and move items through custom workflow stages before handing off to the CMS. It is especially useful for website rebuilds and product content with many contributors. Details are on the Bynder Content Workflow page.

Strengths: consistent structure and a clear status for every item. Limitations: it doesn't schedule social posts or report on performance. Skip it if you mostly publish ad hoc blog posts rather than templated pages.

Will a platform fix a content process that is already slow?

Usually not on its own: software makes an agreed process visible and repeatable, but it can't decide who approves what or how many review rounds a piece gets. Which setup fits a startup, a 10-person team or a multi-client agency?

A startup with one or two marketers rarely needs a platform at all, a 10-person team usually needs a shared calendar with briefs and approvals, and a multi-client agency needs strict client separation above everything else. The scenarios below are hypothetical models, not client cases.

Startup: two marketers, four posts a month

Keep a shared spreadsheet or board for the calendar and publish straight to your CMS. Spend the budget on writing and distribution, and revisit the decision when a second approver joins.

In-house team: ten people across content, product marketing and design

Handoffs now cost real time. A calendar-first tool such as CoSchedule, or HubSpot Content Hub if you're already on HubSpot, gives you ownership and status without an enterprise rollout. Standardise briefs at the same time; our list of content brief and assignment management tools helps.

Agency: five or more clients with separate approvers

Client separation, guest approvals and per-client reporting outrank feature depth. StoryChief's multi-brand workspaces fit some agencies; others are better served by the tools in our roundup of content workflow software for agencies.

When is a spreadsheet plus a CMS enough?

A spreadsheet plus your CMS is enough while one person owns the calendar, there's a single approver and nobody misses a deadline because they didn't know about it. Move to a platform when status checks happen in chat, briefs vary by writer or published work can't be traced back to an approval.

Make the spreadsheet work harder before replacing it: one row per piece, with owner, stage, due date, approver, target keyword and published URL. If that sheet is kept current for a quarter, migrating it later is easy; if it isn't, a platform won't change the habit. What mistakes do B2B teams make when buying one?

The commonest mistake is buying an enterprise suite to solve a problem that is really unclear ownership. The second is assuming the platform's analytics will prove pipeline impact without a CRM connection.

  • Buying for features nobody configures: unused workflows and asset libraries are pure cost.
  • Ignoring who needs access: reviewers, legal and freelancers may need seats or guest access, which changes the bill.
  • Skipping the CMS question: confirm the platform publishes to your CMS natively, or plan for manual handoffs.
  • Migrating everything: move only active and upcoming work, and archive the rest.

What else do B2B buyers ask about content platforms?

The questions below cover pricing transparency, AI features and the overlap with project management tools. Answers reflect our editorial assessment of the categories, not vendor guarantees.

Do content marketing platforms publish their prices?

Some do and some don't. Enterprise platforms such as Optimizely CMP and Contently are generally sold by quote, while lighter tools tend to list tiers publicly, so check each vendor's pricing page.

Can a project management tool replace a content platform?

Often, yes, for teams under about ten people. Tools like Asana or monday.com handle tasks and calendars well but lack native publishing and content-specific analytics.

Should I choose a platform with built-in AI writing?

Treat built-in AI as a convenience, not a deciding factor. Choose on workflow, permissions and publishing fit, then add a dedicated AI tool if drafting is still the bottleneck.

How long does it take to roll out a content platform?

A calendar tool can be usable in days, while an enterprise platform with intake forms, approval rules and integrations typically takes weeks of configuration. Plan the rollout around your agreed workflow, not the vendor's defaults.

What's the verdict?

Buy the lightest tool that removes your current bottleneck: CoSchedule or StoryChief for small teams, HubSpot Content Hub when the CRM is HubSpot, and Optimizely CMP only when governance across many teams is the real problem. If one person still runs the calendar comfortably, keep the spreadsheet and revisit next quarter.
