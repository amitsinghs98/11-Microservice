Quick answer

The audience is the asset. A B2B newsletter that brings in consulting work or sponsorships is only as valuable as your ability to take the subscriber list, its consent history and your archive with you. With that as the first filter:

  • Solo expert who wants automations and digital products: Kit.
  • Growth-focused publication with referrals and ad revenue: beehiiv.
  • Paid publication that wants to own its website and member data: Ghost.
  • Writer who values network discovery over brand control: Substack.
  • Technical or privacy-minded writer who wants simplicity: Buttondown.
  • Small company newsletter on a modest budget: MailerLite.
  • Company newsletter that must feed sales pipeline in a CRM: HubSpot Marketing Hub.

Three kinds of B2B newsletter

The right platform depends on which of these you are running. Many buying mistakes come from choosing a creator tool for a company newsletter, or the reverse.

The solo expert newsletter

A consultant, fractional executive or analyst writing to build authority and generate inbound work. Priorities: a fast editor, a simple landing page, tagging by interest, and a short welcome sequence. Monetization is usually indirect.

The company newsletter

A marketing team publishing on behalf of a brand. Priorities: brand controls, multiple editors with approvals, CRM sync so sales can see engagement, and reporting that ties opens and clicks to pipeline. Our email marketing software comparison for small businesses covers platforms that handle newsletters alongside other campaigns.

The paid B2B publication

An independent publication charging for subscriptions or selling sponsorships. Priorities: payment handling, paywalls, tiered access, a website archive for search, and clean export of paying members.

The platforms

Kit

Best for: consultants and solo experts who want a newsletter plus simple funnels.

Kit, formerly ConvertKit, combines a broadcast editor with landing pages, forms, tagging and a visual automation builder. It also supports selling paid newsletters and digital products, and runs a recommendations network for cross-promotion. Plans are tiered by subscriber count; see the Kit pricing page.

  • Strengths: tagging and automations suit an expert who segments readers by service interest.
  • Limitations: design options are deliberately plain, and there is no full publication website in the way Ghost or beehiiv provide.
  • Skip it if: several editors need approval workflows for a company newsletter.

beehiiv

Best for: publications that plan to grow through referrals and earn from sponsorships.

beehiiv is built around newsletters, with a hosted website, a referral program, paid subscriptions, recommendation features and an ad network, according to its product pages. Plans are tiered by subscribers, with some growth and monetization features reserved for higher plans; see the beehiiv pricing page.

  • Strengths: growth and monetization features in one place, without stitching tools together.
  • Limitations: automation is less flexible than a general marketing automation platform, and the ad network suits audience sizes many niche B2B newsletters will not reach for some time.
  • Before you choose: check which plan unlocks referrals, custom domains and paid subscriptions.

Ghost

Best for: paid publications that want their own website, archive and member database.

Ghost is open-source publishing software with built-in newsletters and paid memberships connected to Stripe. You can self-host it or pay for Ghost(Pro) hosting, which is priced by member count; see the Ghost pricing page. Ghost states on its site that it does not take a percentage of subscription revenue, though Stripe's own fees still apply.

  • Strengths: strong ownership, since the software is open source and members and content can be exported.
  • Limitations: email automation is basic, and self-hosting means you own updates, backups and mail delivery configuration.
  • Skip it if: you need branching nurture sequences tied to a CRM.

Substack

Best for: independent writers who value audience discovery inside a network.

Substack is free to start and takes a share of paid subscription revenue, with payment processing fees on top; confirm the current terms on Substack's official site. Its app, recommendations and comments can help new readers find you.

  • Strengths: almost no setup cost, and a built-in discovery network.
  • Limitations: limited segmentation and automation, limited design control, and the Substack brand sits alongside yours, which many company newsletters will not accept. A revenue share also grows as your paid audience grows.
  • Before you choose: check what is included in the subscriber and post export, including paid subscriber status.

Buttondown

Best for: technical writers and consultants who want a simple, Markdown-friendly tool.

Buttondown is a small, independent newsletter service with a Markdown editor, an API, paid subscriptions through Stripe, custom domains and a privacy-conscious approach to tracking, according to its documentation. Pricing tiers are based on subscribers; see the Buttondown pricing page.

  • Strengths: clean, scriptable and easy to leave.
  • Limitations: no visual automation builder or extensive templates, and a smaller ecosystem of integrations.
  • Skip it if: non-technical colleagues will be editing.

MailerLite

Best for: small teams running a company newsletter alongside occasional campaigns.

MailerLite is a general email marketing platform with a drag-and-drop editor, landing pages, a website builder, automations, segmentation and paid newsletter subscriptions via Stripe. A free plan exists with feature and subscriber limits; see the MailerLite pricing page.

  • Strengths: a broad toolset for the price, suitable for teams that do more than one newsletter.
  • Limitations: fewer creator-growth tools such as referral programs or ad networks.
  • Before you choose: check account approval requirements and which features the free plan excludes.

HubSpot Marketing Hub

Best for: company newsletters where engagement must appear in the CRM for sales follow-up.

HubSpot's email tools sit on its CRM, so newsletter opens and clicks are attached to contact and company records. Pricing depends on edition, seats and marketing contacts; see HubSpot's Marketing Hub pricing.

  • Strengths: the newsletter becomes a lead signal rather than a separate silo.
  • Limitations: expensive for a newsletter alone, and paid subscriptions are not what it is built for.
  • Skip it if: you are a solo expert or a paid publication.

Subscriber costs, ownership and export

Most creator platforms price by subscriber count, so the bill moves with list size, not send volume. Substack's model is different: no platform fee, but a share of paid revenue. For a paid publication, model both at your expected paying-subscriber count, because a percentage fee can overtake a flat subscriber tier as revenue grows. Inactive subscribers also cost money on tiered plans, which is a good reason to prune regularly.

On ownership, check three things before you commit:

  • Subscriber export: does it include tags, sign-up source, consent date and paid status?
  • Payment portability: are paid subscriptions held in your own Stripe account, so they can move with you?
  • Content export: can you export posts and keep URLs working on your own domain?

Migration checklist

  1. Export subscribers with consent dates, sources, tags and unsubscribe status. Keep a dated backup.
  2. Export paying members and confirm how Stripe subscriptions will be reconnected or migrated.
  3. Export the archive and plan redirects so existing post URLs keep working.
  4. Rebuild the welcome sequence and any automations by hand; they rarely transfer.
  5. Set up SPF, DKIM and DMARC on your sending domain before the first send.
  6. Import unsubscribes as suppressed contacts, not active ones.
  7. Send the first issues to your most engaged readers, then widen. The deliverability tools guide covers what to monitor during that ramp-up.
  8. Tell readers about the move in the issue before you switch, so the new sender name is expected.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Choosing on a free plan cap alone; the guide to what free email plans actually allow shows where upgrade triggers tend to hit.
  • Publishing a company newsletter on a platform whose brand sits above yours.
  • Ignoring how the platform handles paid subscriber data until you try to leave.
  • Paying for referral and ad features before the audience is large enough to use them.

FAQs

Is a LinkedIn newsletter enough for a B2B consultant?

It can help reach, but you do not control the subscriber list in the same way, so treat it as distribution and keep your own email list as the primary channel.

Can a company newsletter run on a creator platform?

Yes, if one or two people edit it and CRM sync is not essential. Larger teams usually need approvals and CRM data that general marketing platforms handle better.

Which platforms suit a paid B2B publication?

Ghost, beehiiv, Substack, Kit and Buttondown all support paid subscriptions. Compare who holds the Stripe relationship, the fee model and the archive website.

Do I need double opt-in?

It is not universally required, but it improves list quality and gives you a clearer consent record. Check the rules that apply where your subscribers are.

Verdict

Our editorial assessment: solo experts are well served by Kit or Buttondown, growth-minded publications by beehiiv, and paid publications that care about ownership by Ghost. Substack is the low-effort option if you accept its revenue share and brand presence. Company newsletters belong on MailerLite or, where sales alignment matters, HubSpot.
