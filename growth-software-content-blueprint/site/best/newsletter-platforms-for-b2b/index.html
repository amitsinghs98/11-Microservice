Which newsletter platform should you choose?

Match the platform to the newsletter's job: a solo expert needs a fast editor and growth tools, a company newsletter needs CRM data and brand control, and a paid publication needs billing you can take with you. Pick the one whose weaknesses you can live with, because every option here gives something up.

  • Consultant growing an email-first practice: Kit, for its automations and product sales alongside the newsletter.
  • Expert who wants referrals and growth tooling built in: beehiiv.
  • Paid B2B publication that wants to own the site and the billing: Ghost.
  • Writer who values discovery over control: Substack.
  • Technical writer who prefers Markdown and an API: Buttondown.
  • Small company sending a simple monthly update: MailerLite.
  • Company newsletter that must report into the CRM: HubSpot Marketing Hub.

Which kind of B2B newsletter are you running?

Most B2B newsletters are one of three types, and each puts different demands on the platform. Decide which one you run before comparing features, because a tool that suits a paid publication is often overbuilt for a company update.

The solo expert newsletter

A consultant, fractional executive or specialist writes weekly to build trust that turns into calls. Writing speed matters most, followed by a clean archive page, simple tagging by interest and a way to sell a workshop or template. Segmentation rarely needs to be deep.

The company newsletter

A B2B company sends product news, articles and event invites to customers and prospects. The newsletter has to respect consent records, share data with the CRM and match brand templates. Reporting on which accounts engaged matters more than referral programs.

The paid B2B publication

An analyst or niche publisher charges for research or a premium tier. Billing, tiered access, failed-payment handling and the ability to move paying subscribers without forcing them to re-enter card details become the critical questions.

How did we choose these platforms?

We compared editor experience, paid subscriptions, segmentation, automation, referral tools, analytics, custom domains and exportability, then weighted ownership and export more heavily than growth features. Platforms were included only if they serve at least one of the three newsletter types well.

What is each platform best and worst at?

Each platform is strongest for one of the three newsletter types and weakest somewhere a B2B buyer will notice. The blocks below say where that line falls.

Kit: best for consultants who sell

Kit (formerly ConvertKit) pairs a newsletter editor with tagging, visual automations, landing pages and the ability to sell digital products and paid newsletters. Its recommendation network lets creators suggest each other's newsletters at sign-up. Plans scale by subscriber count; check the Kit pricing page for current tiers.

Skip it if the newsletter belongs to a company with a sales CRM, where Kit's tag-based model becomes a second source of truth.

beehiiv: best for growth-focused experts

beehiiv was built for newsletter publishers, with a web archive, referral programs, recommendations, paid subscriptions and an ad network. It's a strong fit if list growth is the main goal. Many of the growth and monetization features are tied to plan level, so read the beehiiv pricing page closely.

Skip it if you'd never run ads in a client-facing B2B newsletter and don't need referrals; you'd be paying for tools you won't use.

Ghost: best for owning a paid publication

Ghost is open-source publishing software with built-in memberships, newsletters and paid tiers through Stripe. You can self-host it or use the hosted Ghost(Pro) service, and Ghost positions itself as taking no cut of subscription revenue. See the Ghost pricing page for hosted plans.

Skip it if you want referral programs and audience discovery out of the box; Ghost leaves most growth work to you.

Substack: best for discovery, worst for brand control

Substack is free to start, handles paid subscriptions and takes a share of paid revenue. Its app and recommendations can bring readers you wouldn't reach alone. Design options are narrow, and readers who follow you only inside the app aren't the same as email subscribers you can export. See Substack's site for current terms.

Skip it if the newsletter must look and feel like your firm's brand, or if you need CRM integration.

Buttondown: best for technical writers

Buttondown is a small, independent platform with a Markdown editor, an API, custom domains and paid subscriptions through Stripe. It suits developers and technical consultants who prefer plain text to drag-and-drop. See the Buttondown pricing page.

Skip it if you rely on visual templates or need deep automation.

MailerLite: best for simple company updates

MailerLite is a general email platform with a drag-and-drop editor, automations, landing pages and a free tier. It handles a monthly company newsletter well without creator-specific features. See the MailerLite pricing page.

Skip it if you're building a paid publication; it isn't designed around subscriptions and archives.

HubSpot Marketing Hub: best when the CRM is the point

For a company newsletter, the value of HubSpot is that every open and click lands on the contact and company record your sales team already uses. Pricing depends on seats and marketing contacts; see the HubSpot Marketing Hub pricing page.

Skip it if a newsletter is all you need. Paying for a marketing automation suite to send one email a month rarely makes sense.

How does subscriber-based pricing change as you grow?

Most platforms here charge by subscriber band, so your bill rises in steps as the list grows, while Substack charges nothing up front and takes a share of paid revenue instead. Which model is cheaper depends on how many readers pay, so model both at your current size and at three times that size.

Three assumptions shape the comparison:

  • Free list, no paid tier: subscriber-band pricing is the only cost, so compare the band you'll be in a year from now, not today.
  • Paid tier with a modest share of paying readers: a revenue-share platform can look cheap early and expensive later, because its cost grows with revenue rather than list size. Add the payment processor's fees in every model.
  • Company newsletter: the real cost is often the contact tier of a CRM-linked tool, which also counts contacts who never open the newsletter.

Check whether unsubscribed or inactive contacts count toward your band, and whether features you'll need (custom domains, paid subscriptions, referral programs, removing platform branding) require a higher plan. Do you really own your audience on these platforms?

You own the email addresses on every platform here, because each lets you export your subscriber list, but you don't always own everything around them. Paid subscriptions, in-app followers, referral history and engagement data are harder to move, and that's where lock-in lives.

What should a migration checklist cover?

A newsletter migration has to move subscribers, consent records, paid subscriptions and the archive without breaking deliverability. Work through the steps in order and don't send from the new platform until authentication is in place.

  1. Export everything: subscribers with sign-up dates and source, tags or segments, unsubscribes and bounces, and the post archive.
  2. Carry consent across: keep the record of how and when each person opted in, and import suppressed contacts as suppressed.
  3. Plan paid-subscriber moves: confirm whether subscriptions can stay in your Stripe account or need a new billing relationship.
  4. Authenticate the sending domain: set up SPF, DKIM and DMARC for the new platform before the first send.
  5. Redirect the archive: map old post URLs to new ones so links in past issues and search results still work.
  6. Rebuild automations: welcome sequences and tagging rules rarely transfer automatically.
  7. Warm up: send first to your most engaged readers, then the rest, and watch bounces and complaints.

What else do B2B writers ask about newsletter platforms?

The common questions are about LinkedIn, company use of creator tools and analytics. Short answers follow.

Is a LinkedIn newsletter enough for a B2B consultant?

It can be a useful discovery channel, but you don't control delivery or get a portable email list, so treat it as a feed into an email newsletter you own rather than a replacement.

Can a company newsletter run on a creator platform?

Yes, if CRM reporting isn't required. Once sales needs to see which accounts engaged, a tool that writes activity to the CRM is usually the better home.

Which newsletter metrics matter for B2B?

Clicks, replies and the accounts that engage tell you more than open rates, which privacy features in mail clients have made unreliable.

Should a newsletter use double opt-in?

Double opt-in gives you a stronger consent record and a cleaner list, at the cost of some sign-ups that never confirm. For B2B lists where consent may be questioned, it's usually worth it.

What's the verdict?

Consultants should start with Kit or beehiiv, paid publications that care about ownership should look hardest at Ghost, and company newsletters belong wherever the CRM data lives. Substack is the right call only if discovery matters more to you than brand and data control.
