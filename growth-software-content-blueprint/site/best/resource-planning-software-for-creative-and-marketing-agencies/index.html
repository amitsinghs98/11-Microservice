Which resource planning software should a creative or marketing agency choose?

Pick a dedicated scheduler such as Float, Runn or Resource Guru if your project tool works and you only need clear capacity. Pick a platform such as Productive, Teamwork.com, Scoro or Kantata if you want planning tied to budgets, time and profitability.

  • Visual team scheduling that creative teams adopt quickly: Float.
  • Forecasting demand and testing scenarios against pipeline: Runn.
  • Booking people, rooms and equipment with clash detection: Resource Guru.
  • Planning tied to budgets and agency profitability: Productive.
  • Workload views inside an agency project tool: Teamwork.com.
  • Quotes, projects, planning and billing in one work management system: Scoro.
  • Larger, multi-office agencies needing professional services automation: Kantata.

How is resource planning different from a task board?

A task board tracks deliverables; resource planning tracks people's hours, skills and availability over time. Agencies need the second view because the same few specialists are usually shared across many clients.

Capacity has five parts: who you have, what they're skilled at, when they're available (after leave and holidays), how much of their time should be billable, and the demand coming from signed and likely work. Boards cover none of these directly. That's why a busy board can hide an overbooked designer and an underused copywriter in the same week.

What does each tool do well, and where does it fall short?

Dedicated schedulers are easier to adopt and clearer to read; platforms give you margin and forecast context. Decide which matters more before booking demos.

Float: best for visual scheduling creative teams will use

Float centres on a drag-and-drop schedule of people and projects, with leave, tentative bookings and capacity reports. Its strength is clarity, which helps adoption among designers and writers. Its limitation is financial depth compared with a full agency platform. Skip it if you want budgets and invoicing in the same tool. Check plans on the Float pricing page.

Runn: best for forecasting and scenarios

Runn emphasises forecasting capacity against demand, including tentative projects, so you can test whether a new pitch would fit. That suits growing agencies hiring against pipeline. Skip it if you only need next week's schedule. See the Runn pricing page for which tier includes scenario features.

Resource Guru: best for clash-free bookings

Resource Guru handles bookings of people, meeting rooms and equipment, with clash management and leave. It's practical for studios with shared kit, such as video agencies. It's lighter on long-range forecasting and finances. Skip it if forecasting is your main need. See the Resource Guru pricing page.

Productive: best for planning tied to budgets

Productive links resource bookings to project budgets and time, so you can see how a plan affects margin. That's the strongest argument for a platform. Skip it if you won't also move projects and time into it. Check tiers on the Productive pricing page.

Teamwork.com: best for workload views inside agency PM

Teamwork.com offers workload and resource views alongside projects and time, which keeps one tool for delivery and capacity. It's less specialised than a dedicated scheduler. Skip it if you need skill-based forecasting months ahead. See the Teamwork.com pricing page.

Scoro: best for quote-to-cash agencies

Scoro combines CRM, quotes, projects, planning and billing, so capacity sits beside the sales pipeline. Rolling out that many modules takes time and an owner. Skip it if you want a quick win on scheduling alone. See Scoro's pricing page.

Kantata: best for larger multi-office agencies

Kantata is a professional services automation platform aimed at larger firms that need skills-based staffing, forecasting and financials at scale. It's likely more system than a ten-person studio needs. Skip it if you have fewer than a few dozen people. Details are on the Kantata official site.

What does a 10-person capacity review look like?

A capacity review compares available billable hours by role with booked and forecast demand for the next few weeks. The example below is hypothetical, built from stated assumptions, and shows the questions to ask rather than benchmark figures.

Assumptions: ten people (two leads, three designers, two writers, two SEO specialists, one account manager); a standard working week; leave already entered; a role-specific billable target agreed by the owners.

  1. Available hours: for each role, working hours minus leave, multiplied by that role's billable target.
  2. Booked demand: confirmed retainer and project hours already scheduled.
  3. Forecast demand: tentative work weighted by how likely it is to sign.
  4. Gap by role: available minus booked minus forecast. In this example, imagine designers show a shortfall while writers have spare hours.
  5. Decision: move work, re-sequence deadlines, book a freelancer (see our freelancer management platforms guide), or delay a pitch.
  6. Check against reality: compare last month's plan with tracked time from your agency time tracking software.

Which alerts and reports should you rely on?

Rely on over-allocation alerts per person and utilization by role over the coming weeks. Treat long-range forecasts as scenarios, because pipeline changes faster than plans.

Useful reports include planned versus actual hours, utilization by role, and unassigned demand. Feed profitability views with our guide to profitability reporting tools for agency owners.

What mistakes do agencies make with resource planning?

The commonest mistake is planning at task level for months ahead, which creates a schedule nobody maintains. Another is setting one utilization target for every role.

  • Leaving leave, holidays and freelancers out of the plan.
  • Never comparing planned hours with tracked time.
  • Letting only one person update the schedule.
  • Buying a platform before deciding who owns weekly capacity reviews.

What else do agencies ask about resource planning?

Most follow-up questions concern utilization targets, freelancers and how far ahead to plan. The answers below are general guidance; confirm features and limits on each vendor's official site.

What is the difference between resource planning and a task board?

A task board shows the work and its status; resource planning shows people, their hours and skills over time. You need both, because a board can look healthy while a designer is booked at double capacity next week.

What utilization target should a creative agency use?

There's no universal number, and it should differ by role. Leaders and account managers spend time on non-billable work, so set targets per role and review them against actual time data.

Should freelancers appear in the resource plan?

Yes, as named or placeholder resources with their availability. Leaving them out hides your real capacity and makes demand look unmanageable when it isn't.

How far ahead should an agency plan resources?

Plan named people for the next few weeks and roles or placeholders for the months beyond. Long-range plans built on unconfirmed pipeline should be treated as scenarios, not commitments.

Do we need resource planning software with fewer than ten people?

Often a shared calendar and a weekly conversation are enough. Software starts paying back when several projects compete for the same specialists, or when leave and freelancers make availability hard to see.
