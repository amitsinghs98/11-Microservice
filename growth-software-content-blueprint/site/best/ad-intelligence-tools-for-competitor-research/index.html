Best Ad Intelligence Tools for Competitor Research

The best ad intelligence tools for competitor research start with the free transparency libraries from Meta, Google, LinkedIn and TikTok, then add a paid platform such as SpyFu, Semrush, Similarweb, Sensor Tower or Adbeat only when you need history, estimates or display coverage. Every paid tool sees a sample of the market, not the whole of it, so treat what it shows as evidence for a hypothesis rather than a map of a rival's budget.

Which ad intelligence tool should you choose?

If your competitors mostly advertise on social and Google, the free platform libraries answer most creative and messaging questions at no cost. Pay for a platform only when you need something the libraries don't give you: keyword-level search history, cross-network display placements, spend estimates or exports you can analyze at scale.

  • Seeing every active Meta ad a rival runs, by country: Meta Ad Library.
  • Checking a competitor's Google Search, Display and YouTube ads by advertiser: Google Ads Transparency Center.
  • B2B messaging and offers on LinkedIn: LinkedIn Ad Library.
  • Short-form video trends and top ads by industry: TikTok Creative Center.
  • Paid search keywords and ad copy history on a budget: SpyFu.
  • Search ads inside an SEO platform you already pay for: Semrush.
  • Channel mix and paid traffic share in a market view: Similarweb.
  • Brand-level digital ad spend estimates across networks: Sensor Tower (Pathmatics).
  • Display and native ad placements and landing pages: Adbeat.

What can ad intelligence actually tell you about a competitor?

Ad intelligence reliably tells you which creative, offers and messages a competitor has put in front of people, and roughly where and when. It can't tell you which ads worked, what the competitor paid or how their account is structured, because none of that is public.

That distinction shapes every buying decision in this category. Platform libraries publish what is running, because regulators and public pressure required it. Paid tools add their own observations: crawled search results, panels of browsers or devices, and modelled estimates. Each method has blind spots. Search crawlers sample certain keywords, locations and times of day. Panels over-represent the people who joined them. Spend models are calibrated on partial data and then extrapolated.

So the honest output of this research is a list of observations with dates, not a verdict. "Competitor X has run the same free-trial offer on Meta in three countries since spring" is useful. "Competitor X spends a fixed amount a month on Google" is a model's guess presented as a fact. If your real question is brand perception rather than ads, a social listening platform for competitor research answers it better.

What is each tool best for, and when should you skip it?

Start with the libraries for the platforms your competitors use, then add one paid tool that fills the biggest gap. Few teams need more than one paid ad intelligence subscription at a time.

Meta Ad Library: best for active Facebook and Instagram creative

What it does: a public, searchable archive of ads running across Meta's apps, searchable by advertiser or keyword and filterable by country, platform and media type. See the Meta Ad Library.

Strengths: it comes from Meta itself, so what you see actually ran, with start dates and variations. Limitations: for most commercial ads outside regulated categories and regions, there's no spend, reach or engagement, and inactive ads may drop out of view. Skip it if your competitors don't advertise on Meta, which is rarer than buyers assume.

Google Ads Transparency Center: best for Google ads by advertiser

What it does: shows ads from verified advertisers across Google's surfaces, filterable by region, format and date. See the Google Ads Transparency Center.

Strengths: authoritative and free, and it covers display and video as well as text ads. Limitations: it shows ads, not the keywords or audiences behind them, so it can't tell you what queries a rival bids on. Skip it if your question is "which keywords?"; use SpyFu or Semrush for that.

LinkedIn Ad Library: best for B2B offers and messaging

What it does: a public archive of ads that ran on LinkedIn, searchable by advertiser, keyword and country. See the LinkedIn Ad Library.

Strengths: the clearest public view of how B2B rivals frame offers, gated content and events. Limitations: basic filtering, and you'll capture findings by hand. Skip it if you sell to consumers. For managing your own LinkedIn campaigns, see our guide to LinkedIn ads management and reporting tools.

TikTok Creative Center: best for trend and top-ad discovery

What it does: TikTok's free inspiration hub, with top-performing ads, trending hashtags, sounds and creators by region and industry. See the TikTok Creative Center.

Strengths: shows formats and hooks that are working in a category right now. Limitations: it's curated, so it's weaker for tracking a specific competitor's full history. Skip it if you need a complete record of one advertiser's ads.

SpyFu: best for search keyword and ad copy history on a budget

What it does: estimates which keywords a domain buys in Google search and shows historical ad copy, so you can see which headlines and offers a rival kept running. See SpyFu's site for current plans.

Strengths: focused, relatively affordable, and its ad history is useful for spotting long-running messages. Limitations: data comes from sampled search results, so smaller or local advertisers may be patchy, and social coverage is minimal. Skip it if your competitors spend mainly on social.

Semrush: best for search ads alongside SEO research

What it does: Semrush's advertising research shows estimated paid keywords, ad copy and positions for a domain, inside the same platform many teams use for SEO. See Semrush's site.

Strengths: one login for organic and paid competitor views, which helps when you're deciding whether to pay for a term or rank for it. Limitations: advertising data is sampled by keyword database and location, and deeper ad intelligence may sit in separately priced toolkits or apps. Skip it if you only need ads and don't use its SEO tools. Our keyword research tools comparison covers its organic side.

Similarweb: best for channel mix and market-share views

What it does: estimates website traffic by channel, including paid search and display, and lists top paid keywords and publishers for a domain. See Similarweb's site.

Strengths: it puts ads in context: how much of a rival's traffic appears to come from paid channels compared with organic, direct and referral. Limitations: figures are modelled and less reliable for small sites, and the useful tiers are sales-led. Skip it if you need ad creative rather than traffic context.

Sensor Tower (Pathmatics): best for brand-level spend estimates

What it does: Pathmatics, now part of Sensor Tower, estimates digital ad spend, impressions and creative across social, display and video networks. See Sensor Tower's site.

Strengths: the closest thing in this list to a share-of-voice view across networks. Limitations: estimates, not invoices, and pricing is aimed at brands and larger agencies. Skip it if you're a small team whose questions are about messaging, not market share.

Adbeat: best for display and native placements

What it does: tracks display and native ads, the publishers and networks they run on, and the landing pages they send traffic to. See Adbeat's site.

Strengths: useful for affiliate, lead-gen and direct-response teams studying funnels across the open web. Limitations: little help for search-only or social-only programmes. Skip it if you don't buy display or native inventory.

How far can you trust spend and impression estimates?

Trust the direction more than the number: an estimate that a rival's paid activity doubled is more reliable than any single monthly figure. Vendors build these numbers from samples and models, and they rarely publish error ranges you could check.

How do you turn competitor ads into tests instead of copies?

Record what you observed, why it might matter, and the test you'll run, in that order. Copying a rival's ad imports their positioning and audience, which may not be yours, and you still don't know whether it worked for them.

  1. Log the observation with a date and source: "Rival's LinkedIn ads switched from demo offers to a pricing calculator in the past two months."
  2. Note the signal strength: longevity and the number of variants are the best public proxies for an ad that's earning its keep, though even these are not proof.
  3. Write the hypothesis in your terms: "Our buyers may respond to a cost-estimate offer more than a demo."
  4. Design a fair test: your own creative, your audience, a clear success metric and a stop date.
  5. Review outcomes, not rivals: keep the test if it beats your control, whatever the competitor does next.

What mistakes do teams make with ad intelligence?

The biggest mistake is treating estimates as facts in client reports. The second is buying a broad intelligence suite when the free libraries already cover the channels that matter.

  • Reporting estimated spend as actual spend: label every estimate with its source and the date it was pulled.
  • Ignoring geography: many tools sample a few countries well and others thinly, so check coverage for your markets.
  • Assuming long-running means profitable: some ads run because nobody switched them off.
  • Skipping export terms: some licences restrict how you can share data with clients, so read them before you put screenshots in decks.
  • Researching without a question: decide what decision the research informs before you open the tool.

What else do buyers ask about ad intelligence tools?

Most questions are about legality, accuracy and whether free tools are enough. Short answers follow.

Is it legal to research competitors' ads?

Viewing public ads and the platforms' own transparency libraries is normal practice. Copying another advertiser's creative or trademarks is a different matter, so use what you find as inspiration for your own tests.

Are the free ad libraries enough for a small business?

Often, yes. If competitors advertise mainly on Meta, Google and LinkedIn, the libraries show their live creative and offers; paid tools mostly add history, keyword estimates and scale.

Can any tool show a competitor's exact ad spend?

No. Spend figures in third-party tools are modelled estimates, and only the advertiser and the ad platform know the real numbers.

How often should we run competitor ad research?

A light monthly check suits most accounts, with a deeper review before planning cycles or launches. Set alerts where a tool offers them so you notice big changes between reviews.

What's the verdict?

Use the Meta, Google, LinkedIn and TikTok libraries first, add SpyFu or Semrush when paid search history matters, and reserve Similarweb, Sensor Tower or Adbeat for market-share or display questions. Whatever you choose, present the output as dated observations and hypotheses, not facts about a rival's budget.
