Quick answer

Recordings are cheap to collect and expensive to watch. The tool that wins is the one that helps you find the right twenty sessions out of thousands, while masking data you should never capture.

  • No budget, solid basics: Microsoft Clarity.
  • Marketing teams that also want surveys and feedback: Hotjar.
  • Funnels and form analytics alongside replay: Mouseflow.
  • Small business owners who want simple visual reports: Crazy Egg or Lucky Orange.
  • Product-led SaaS that wants replay tied to product analytics: PostHog.
  • Large digital teams needing enterprise governance: FullStory or Contentsquare.

When qualitative tools help, and when they do not

Heatmaps and session replay answer "what happened on this page, and where did it go wrong?" They are most useful once analytics has pointed to a specific page or step with a problem, such as a pricing page with high exits or a checkout step where mobile users drop.

Typical signals they surface:

  • Click maps: clicks on elements that are not links, or ignored calls to action.
  • Scroll maps: how far down the page most visitors get, and whether key content sits below that point.
  • Rage clicks and dead clicks: repeated clicks out of frustration, or clicks that do nothing.
  • Replays: hesitation, back-and-forth navigation, errors and form struggles in context.

They are weaker at telling you how big a problem is across all traffic, or whether a fix worked. A heatmap of a few hundred visits is not a measurement of your whole audience. That is the job of your web analytics tool and, where traffic allows, a controlled experiment.

The tools

Microsoft Clarity

Best for: small teams and agencies that want heatmaps and recordings on every client site without new subscriptions. Skip it if: you need surveys, collaborative research workflows or vendor support commitments.

According to Microsoft, Clarity provides heatmaps, recordings and automatic insights such as rage clicks, dead clicks, excessive scrolling and quick backs, and integrates with GA4. It is positioned as free. Filtering is capable for the price, but research features are thinner than in paid tools. Review data-masking settings on the Microsoft Clarity site.

Hotjar

Best for: marketing and UX teams that want to watch behaviour and ask visitors why in one tool. Skip it if: you mainly need product analytics or high-volume capture.

Hotjar, part of Contentsquare, pairs heatmaps and recordings with surveys and feedback widgets. Plans are limited by daily sessions captured, so high-traffic sites see a sample unless they upgrade. Teams outgrowing it can compare options in our guide to Hotjar replacements. See the Hotjar pricing page.

Mouseflow

Best for: conversion specialists who want funnels and form analytics beside replay. Skip it if: you only need occasional heatmaps.

Mouseflow offers heatmaps, recordings, funnels, form analytics and frustration detection. Funnels built from recorded sessions help you jump from a drop-off step straight to examples of it. Plans scale by monthly recordings and websites, so agencies should check per-site limits. See the Mouseflow pricing page.

Crazy Egg

Best for: site owners who want clear visual reports and occasional simple tests. Skip it if: you need deep segmentation or product analytics.

Crazy Egg is known for heatmaps, scroll maps and click-breakdown reports, and also offers recordings, surveys and basic A/B testing. Its built-in testing is useful for small sites but is not a substitute for a dedicated platform. Plans are tiered by tracked pageviews and recordings; see the Crazy Egg pricing page.

Lucky Orange

Best for: small ecommerce and service businesses that want replay, surveys and live chat together. Skip it if: you already run a separate chat tool and want specialist research depth.

Lucky Orange combines dynamic heatmaps, recordings, surveys, form analytics and live chat. The bundle can replace several small tools, but overlap with an existing chat or survey product reduces its value. Plans scale by sessions; see the Lucky Orange pricing page.

PostHog

Best for: SaaS teams that want to filter replays by product events, such as "users who started onboarding but never invited a teammate." Skip it if: your users are marketers who want point-and-click heatmap reports.

PostHog includes session replay alongside product analytics, feature flags and experiments, with usage-based pricing and a monthly free allowance per product. Linking replay to events is its main advantage. Monitor recording volume as usage grows. See the PostHog pricing page.

FullStory

Best for: larger digital teams that want autocaptured interaction data, searchable journeys and data export. Skip it if: you need transparent self-serve pricing.

FullStory captures interactions automatically, so you can define events after the fact, and surfaces frustration signals across journeys. It offers privacy controls designed for regulated industries, according to its documentation. Pricing is typically contract-based; start at the FullStory website.

Contentsquare

Best for: enterprise ecommerce and media teams that need zone-level analysis across many templates and markets. Skip it if: you are a small team; Hotjar, from the same company, is the lighter option.

Contentsquare measures engagement by page zone, maps customer journeys and adds experience analytics for large sites. Implementation and contracts reflect its enterprise focus. Start at the Contentsquare website.

Sample limits and privacy controls

Vendors cap usage in different units: sessions per day, recordings per month, tracked pageviews or events. A cap that looks generous for one site can be tight across ten client sites. Ask whether the tool samples randomly once the cap is reached, stops recording, or bills an overage, because sampling changes what your heatmaps represent.

Privacy needs equal attention. Check that:

  • Form inputs are masked by default, and you can mask other elements such as account details.
  • Recording starts only after consent where your consent rules require it.
  • Data residency, retention periods and a data processing agreement meet your obligations.
  • You can exclude pages such as account areas and payment steps entirely.

None of this is legal advice; confirm requirements with your adviser.

A weekly review routine

Without a routine, recordings pile up unwatched. A 60-minute weekly session works for most small teams:

  1. Pick one page or funnel step (5 minutes). Choose it from analytics: highest traffic with the weakest conversion, or a recent drop.
  2. Check frustration signals (10 minutes). Review rage clicks, dead clicks and error-related sessions on that page.
  3. Watch ten to fifteen filtered sessions (25 minutes). Filter to converters and non-converters separately, and to mobile and desktop.
  4. Compare heatmaps by segment (10 minutes). Look for differences between traffic sources or devices.
  5. Log observations (10 minutes). Record each pattern, how often you saw it, and a draft hypothesis in a shared backlog.

Rotate the page each week, and revisit a page after any change ships.

Questions to ask before buying

  • What unit is our plan limited by, and what happens when we exceed it?
  • How long are recordings retained on each plan?
  • Can we filter replays by events, UTM source, device and conversion status?
  • How are single-page applications and dynamic content handled in heatmaps?
  • Can agency users manage several client sites with separate access?

FAQs

How many recordings should we watch to find a problem?

There is no fixed number. In practice, a focused batch of well-filtered sessions on one page often reveals repeated patterns. Stop when new sessions stop showing new issues, then size the issue in analytics.

Is session replay the same as product analytics?

No. Replay shows individual visits; product analytics counts events across all users. Some tools, such as PostHog and FullStory, combine both, which helps you move between the count and the example.

Will a replay script slow my site?

Any third-party script adds some load. Vendors design their scripts to load asynchronously, but measure page speed before and after installation and remove tools you no longer review.

Can we use these tools on pages with personal data?

Only with masking, consent and contractual safeguards configured. Excluding sensitive pages entirely is often the simplest option.
