Which task management software should a marketing ops team choose?

Choose the tool that makes cross-team dependencies, approvals and recurring work visible without heavy admin. For most marketing ops teams that's Asana, monday.com or Wrike; the rest suit narrower situations.

  • Cross-functional launches with clear dependencies and portfolio views: Asana.
  • Visual boards and no-code automations that non-technical teams adopt quickly: monday.com work management.
  • Request-heavy teams that need intake forms, proofing and workload views: Wrike.
  • Teams consolidating tasks, docs and goals into one tool: ClickUp.
  • Campaign and asset tracking that behaves like a database: Airtable.
  • Spreadsheet-minded teams and PMO-style reporting: Smartsheet.
  • Marketing teams embedded in an Atlassian engineering organisation: Jira.

What does marketing ops need that personal productivity tools miss?

Marketing ops coordinates work across people and teams, so it needs intake, dependencies, approvals, recurring templates and reporting. Personal to-do tools handle none of those well once more than a few people are involved.

A product launch might involve product marketing, content, design, web, paid media, sales enablement and legal review. The failure modes are familiar: a blog post waits on a screenshot nobody requested, the landing page ships before legal signs off, or the monthly newsletter is rebuilt from scratch every time. A good task system turns each of those into a template, a dependency or a form.

What does each tool do well, and where does it fall short?

The leaders differ mainly in structure versus flexibility. Pick the one whose default way of working is closest to how your team already plans campaigns.

Asana: best for cross-functional launches

Asana positions itself around dependencies, timelines, forms, rules and portfolio views, which maps neatly to multi-team launches. It's opinionated enough that projects look consistent across teams. Check which tier includes workload and reporting views. Skip it if you need database-style flexibility. See the Asana pricing page.

monday.com work management: best for fast adoption

monday.com is positioned around visual boards, flexible columns, no-code automations and dashboards. Non-technical stakeholders tend to pick it up quickly. Heavy automators should check how each plan treats automations and integrations. Skip it if you want strict, consistent project structures by default. See the monday.com pricing page.

Wrike: best for request-driven teams

Wrike positions itself around request forms, custom workflows, proofing and workload views, which suits teams fielding many requests from other departments. It's powerful but takes more setup and training than Asana or monday.com. Skip it if you're a team of five. See the Wrike pricing page.

ClickUp: best for consolidating tools

ClickUp is positioned as an all-in-one workspace spanning tasks, docs, goals, custom fields and automations. That breadth can replace several subscriptions. Without an owner setting conventions, spaces and fields multiply quickly. Skip it if your team values simplicity over coverage. See the ClickUp pricing page.

Airtable: best when campaigns behave like data

Airtable is positioned as a relational, database-style app builder with views, forms and automations. It's excellent for content inventories, asset libraries and campaign trackers where records link to each other. Task-management features such as dependencies and workload need building rather than coming ready-made. Skip it if you want a project tool out of the box. See the Airtable pricing page.

Smartsheet: best for spreadsheet-minded planners

Smartsheet is positioned as spreadsheet-style work management with Gantt views, forms, automations and cross-sheet reporting. It fits ops teams that report into a PMO or finance. Creative review and visual workflows are less natural. Skip it if your team dislikes grid interfaces. See the Smartsheet pricing page.

Jira: best inside an Atlassian organisation

Atlassian positions Jira for business teams as well as software tracking, so marketing can share dependencies with product and engineering. For launches tied to releases, that visibility matters. Marketers often find the interface technical. Skip it if engineering doesn't already use Atlassian. See the Jira pricing page.

What does a campaign launch workflow look like in a task tool?

Build one launch template with phases, dependencies and approval gates, then copy it for every launch. The template is where most of the time saving comes from, not the tool itself.

  1. Intake: a request form captures goal, audience, launch date, budget and owner, and creates the project.
  2. Brief approval: a gated task; nothing downstream starts until it's approved.
  3. Production: parallel tasks for copy, design, landing page and ads, each with due dates set relative to launch.
  4. Review and legal: approval tasks with dependencies blocking publication.
  5. Launch: a checklist covering tracking links, email scheduling and sales enablement.
  6. Post-launch: a recurring reporting task at one and four weeks.

Campaign naming and tracking links need their own discipline; see UTM governance and campaign naming tools. Email and nurture steps run in your marketing automation software, not the task tool.

How should a recurring publishing workflow be set up?

Use a recurring template with fixed stages and owners, triggered on a schedule rather than recreated by hand. Pair it with workload views so editors can see when a writer is overloaded.

A typical stage sequence is brief, draft, edit, SEO check, design, approval, scheduled and published, with an automation moving the task and reassigning it at each stage. Content-heavy teams may prefer a purpose-built tool; compare content workflow software and editorial calendar software for multi-author teams. Why do marketing teams abandon task tools after a few months?

Usually because the tool was set up for one person's preferences and nobody maintained the templates, fields and rules. Based on the product information reviewed, every tool here can be configured well or badly, and the configuration owner matters more than the vendor.

What's the verdict?

Asana is the safest default for cross-functional launches, monday.com wins on adoption, and Wrike suits request-heavy teams with more admin capacity. Choose ClickUp to consolidate, Airtable for data-shaped campaigns, Smartsheet for grid-minded planners and Jira when engineering already lives in Atlassian.

What else do marketing ops teams ask about task management?

The common follow-ups concern tool consolidation, automation limits and adoption by non-marketing teams. These answers are general, so check current plan details on each vendor's site.

Is a task manager different from project management software?

In practice the categories overlap heavily. The useful distinction is fit: marketing ops needs intake forms, recurring workflows, approvals and cross-team reporting, which some tools handle better than lightweight to-do apps.

Do automation limits matter?

Yes. Automation allowances can differ by plan, so a team that automates handoffs heavily should confirm what each tier permits. Check the vendor's stated limits against your expected volume before you choose.

Should sales or product teams use the same tool?

It helps if launches depend on them, because dependencies across tools are invisible. If they won't move, give them guest or request-form access rather than forcing a second system.

How many custom fields should we create?

Start with the few that drive reporting, such as channel, campaign, owner, stage and launch date. Every extra required field slows intake and invites inconsistent data.

Can these tools replace our marketing automation platform?

No. Task tools coordinate people and work, while marketing automation platforms send emails, score leads and trigger campaigns. They connect, but they don't substitute for each other.
