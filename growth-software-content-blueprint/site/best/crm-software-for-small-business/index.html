Quick answer

Most small businesses do not need the most powerful CRM. They need one that the whole team will open every day, that shows where each deal stands, and that does not double in price when the next hire joins. With that in mind, these are our editorial picks by situation:

  • Visual deal pipeline for a small sales team: Pipedrive. The pipeline board is the product, and per-seat pricing is published.
  • Marketing and sales on one contact record: HubSpot. The free CRM is a low-risk start, but paid tiers climb quickly.
  • Broad features for a modest budget: Zoho CRM, especially if you already use other Zoho apps.
  • Phone-heavy or inside sales with built-in calling: Freshsales.
  • A team that expects to need enterprise depth later: Salesforce Starter Suite, accepting a steeper admin path.
  • Teams that already run work in monday.com: monday CRM.
  • The simplest possible relationship tracker: Less Annoying CRM.
  • Firms that live in Gmail and Google Workspace: Copper.

If you are choosing for an agency with client accounts, our agency-specific CRM shortlist weighs different criteria. Everything in this cluster is mapped on the CRM and lead generation hub.

When a spreadsheet stops being enough

A shared spreadsheet is a reasonable CRM for a founder with twenty active conversations. It breaks in predictable ways, and the signs usually show up before anyone admits it:

  • Two people contact the same prospect in the same week because neither saw the other's note.
  • You cannot answer "what will close this month?" without asking everyone individually.
  • Follow-ups depend on someone's memory or a separate to-do app.
  • Email threads with customers live in personal inboxes, so the history leaves when a person does.
  • Web form leads arrive by email and get copied in by hand, sometimes days later.

If two or more of those are true, a CRM will probably pay for itself in recovered follow-ups alone. If none are true, a free tier is enough to start building the habit, and our guide to free CRM plans and the limits that trigger an upgrade covers that path.

Who this guide is for

This page is written for owners and operators choosing a first or second CRM for roughly 2 to 25 users. We segment advice for four common models:

  • Service businesses (trades, clinics, professional services): many small deals, quotes, repeat customers and appointment-driven work.
  • SaaS companies: trials, product signals, subscription renewals and a handoff from sales to customer success. Our CRM guide for B2B SaaS sales teams goes deeper.
  • Ecommerce: mostly transactional customers, where the email platform often holds the useful customer data and a sales CRM matters mainly for wholesale or B2B accounts.
  • Consultative sales: fewer, larger deals with long cycles, several stakeholders and proposals.

The eight CRMs

Pipedrive

Best for: small sales teams whose day revolves around moving deals through stages.

Pipedrive organises work around a drag-and-drop deal pipeline, with activities attached to each deal so reps always see the next step. Higher tiers add automation, more reporting and features such as document handling, and the vendor sells extras as add-ons: its pricing page lists LeadBooster, Projects, Campaigns, Web Visitors and Smart Docs, each with a "starting from" price (checked 4 November 2026).

Pricing: the Pipedrive pricing page lists Lite at US$14, Growth at US$39, Premium at US$59 and Ultimate at US$79 per seat per month billed annually, with a 14-day free trial (checked 4 November 2026). Monthly billing costs more; the page says annual billing saves "up to 42%".

Strengths: fast to learn, transparent per-seat pricing, a clear view of deal stages. Limitations: marketing tools are thin without the Campaigns add-on, and add-ons can quietly lift the per-seat cost. Skip it if your work is mostly service tickets or marketing automation rather than deals. Teams weighing a switch can read our look at Pipedrive alternatives for small teams.

HubSpot

Best for: businesses that want marketing forms, email and sales pipeline on one contact timeline.

HubSpot's free CRM tools cover contacts, companies, deals, tasks and basic forms, which makes it an easy starting point. Paid Sales Hub and Marketing Hub tiers add sequences, automation, forecasting and richer reporting. The product information we reviewed describes seat-based pricing that differs by hub and tier.

Pricing: free tools, then per-seat Starter, Professional and Enterprise tiers. Professional and Enterprise have historically carried onboarding fees; confirm current terms on the HubSpot Sales Hub pricing page.

Strengths: a polished interface, a large integration marketplace, and marketing and sales on one record. Limitations: the jump from Starter to Professional is the main cost cliff, and marketing contact tiers can add to the bill. Skip it if you only need a pipeline and will never use the marketing side. Our HubSpot and Salesforce comparison for growing businesses covers the upgrade path.

Zoho CRM

Best for: cost-conscious teams that want customisation, workflow rules and wider business apps.

Zoho CRM offers modules, custom fields, workflow rules, process management (Blueprint) and an AI assistant (Zia) across its editions. It also sits within a wider suite: invoicing, help desk and email marketing are available as separate Zoho apps or through the Zoho One bundle.

Pricing: per user across several editions, with a free edition for very small teams. Check user limits on the Zoho CRM pricing page.

Strengths: a lot of capability per dollar, and it can grow into a full back office. Limitations: the interface and settings depth take longer to configure well, and a poorly planned setup gets messy. Skip it if nobody on the team will own the configuration.

Freshsales

Best for: inside sales teams that want calling, email and pipeline in one place.

Freshsales, Freshworks' sales CRM, includes contact and deal management, built-in phone and email, sequences on higher tiers and the Freddy AI assistant. It pairs naturally with Freshdesk for support teams.

Pricing: per user, tiered; see the Freshsales pricing page for current tiers and whether a free plan is offered.

Strengths: telephony without a separate dialer, and a sensible default setup. Limitations: its integration ecosystem is smaller than HubSpot's or Salesforce's, and telephony credits are a separate cost to model. Skip it if your sales motion is mostly email and in-person meetings.

Salesforce Starter Suite

Best for: small teams that already expect to need Salesforce-grade customisation or integrations.

Starter Suite is Salesforce's packaged entry tier, combining sales, service and basic email marketing with guided setup. Its value is the path it opens into Sales Cloud, the AppExchange and Salesforce's broader platform.

Pricing: per user, with annual contracts typical. Check current editions on the Salesforce sales pricing page.

Strengths: very wide extensibility and a large consultant and admin market. Limitations: in our assessment, most small teams will need outside admin help sooner than they expect, and costs grow with each higher edition. Skip it if you have no one to own administration.

monday CRM

Best for: teams that already manage projects in monday.com and want sales in the same workspace.

monday CRM uses the board-and-column model of monday.com for leads, deals and accounts, with automations and email sync. Its advantage is that post-sale delivery work can stay in the same tool.

Pricing: per seat, sold in seat bundles, so a team of 4 may pay for more seats than it uses. Check bundle sizes on the monday CRM pricing page.

Strengths: flexible and easy to understand visually. Limitations: the flexibility means you design your own sales process, and sales-specific reporting is less opinionated than in Pipedrive. Skip it if you want a CRM that tells you how to sell.

Less Annoying CRM

Best for: very small firms that want contacts, notes, follow-ups and a simple pipeline with no configuration project.

Less Annoying CRM deliberately keeps scope narrow: contacts, calendar, tasks, pipelines and notes, with free support. The vendor publishes a single per-user plan rather than tiers.

Pricing: one per-user monthly price with a free trial; confirm the current figure on the Less Annoying CRM pricing page.

Strengths: predictable cost and minimal training. Limitations: limited automation, marketing and advanced reporting. Skip it if you need sequences, lead scoring or complex automation in the next year.

Copper

Best for: relationship-driven firms that work inside Gmail and Google Calendar.

Copper's main idea is that the CRM appears inside Google Workspace through its Chrome extension, logging emails and meetings and creating contacts with little data entry.

Pricing: per seat across tiers; see the Copper pricing page.

Strengths: low data-entry friction for Google users. Limitations: much less appealing if you use Microsoft 365, and automation and reporting are lighter than in the larger platforms. Skip it if your team is not on Google Workspace.

Total cost at 3, 10 and 25 users

Seat price is only the visible part. A realistic annual cost is seats x price x 12, plus required add-ons, plus any onboarding fee, plus admin time. We can show verified figures only for Pipedrive, so it serves as the worked example. Apply the same formula to the other vendors using their official pricing pages.

What changes the real bill at each size:

  • 3 users: free tiers (HubSpot, Zoho) are genuinely viable. The risk is seat bundles; a vendor that sells seats in fixed blocks may charge for more users than you have.
  • 10 users: automation and reporting needs usually push teams one tier up. This is where HubSpot's Starter-to-Professional jump and its onboarding fee, if one applies, matter most.
  • 25 users: admin time becomes a real cost. A part-time admin or consultant can cost more than the licences on Salesforce or a heavily customised Zoho build.

Free tiers, trials and onboarding

A free plan suits a team that is building the habit; a trial suits a team that already knows what it needs. HubSpot and Zoho offer free editions with limits on users, automation or reporting. Pipedrive, Salesforce, Copper and Less Annoying CRM rely on trials, so run your real pipeline during the trial rather than demo data.

Onboarding differs as much as price. Pipedrive, Less Annoying CRM and Copper can be live in an afternoon for a small team. HubSpot's free tools are quick, but Professional tiers are designed around a guided onboarding. Zoho and Salesforce reward a planned setup: fields, stages, permissions and integrations agreed before import.

Recommendations by business type

Service businesses

Prioritise simple pipelines, quoting and reminders. Pipedrive or Less Annoying CRM suit most; Zoho suits firms that also want invoicing in the same family of apps.

SaaS companies

Look for lifecycle stages, product-usage integrations and renewal tracking. HubSpot and Salesforce are the usual shortlist, and the SaaS guide linked above adds Attio and Close.

Ecommerce

Many direct-to-consumer brands are better served by their email platform than by a sales CRM; see our ecommerce email platform comparison. Add a CRM such as HubSpot or Zoho when wholesale, B2B or high-value accounts need a pipeline.

Consultative sales

Account and contact relationships, proposal stages and forecasting matter most. HubSpot, Salesforce and Pipedrive's higher tiers fit. Pair the CRM with a dependable pipeline source; our B2B lead generation software roundup covers capture and prospecting tools.

Migration and adoption checklists

Data migration checklist

  1. Export every source: spreadsheets, old CRM, email contacts and form tools.
  2. Deduplicate on email address and company domain before import, not after.
  3. Agree pipeline stages and required fields first; map old columns to them.
  4. Import companies, then contacts, then deals, so associations attach correctly.
  5. Record consent and source fields so marketing permissions survive the move.
  6. Spot-check 20 records by hand and keep the original export read-only for 90 days.

Adoption checklist

  1. Name one owner who decides fields, stages and naming rules.
  2. Connect email and calendar on day one so logging is automatic.
  3. Run the weekly pipeline meeting from the CRM screen, not a slide deck.
  4. Retire the old spreadsheet on a fixed date.
  5. Review unused fields and automations after 60 days and remove clutter.

Buying mistakes and questions to ask

  • Buying for the team you might be in three years. Pay for that when it arrives; migration is usually cheaper than years of unused enterprise features.
  • Ignoring the marketing side. If you will run email nurture, check whether the CRM handles it or needs a separate tool; our marketing automation shortlist for small teams covers options.
  • Forgetting the people who are not sellers. Founders, finance and delivery staff may each need a seat.

Questions to ask each vendor:

  • Which features in our demo are on the tier we are quoting?
  • Are there onboarding fees, seat minimums or bundle sizes?
  • What does renewal look like, and is an introductory discount time-limited?
  • How do we export all data, including notes and email history, if we leave?

FAQs

Is a free CRM good enough for a small business?

For one to three people building the habit, often yes. Upgrade when you need automation, more pipelines or reporting that the free tier withholds.

How long does a small-business CRM rollout take?

A simple pipeline CRM can be usable within a day. A configured Zoho or Salesforce setup with migration and integrations more often takes several weeks, depending on data quality.

Should marketing and sales share one CRM?

Usually yes, so lead source and campaign history stay on the same record. The exception is ecommerce, where the email platform may be the better system of record for customers.

Can I switch CRMs later?

Yes, but email history, attachments and automations rarely move cleanly. Confirm export options before you sign.

Verdict

Based on the product information reviewed, Pipedrive is the most straightforward choice for a small team that sells through a pipeline, and its pricing is easy to model. HubSpot is the stronger pick when marketing and sales need one record, provided you budget for the Professional jump. Zoho gives the most capability per dollar if someone owns the setup. Salesforce makes sense when you already know you will need its platform. For everyone else, pick the simplest tool your team will actually use, and revisit the decision once you pass ten users.
