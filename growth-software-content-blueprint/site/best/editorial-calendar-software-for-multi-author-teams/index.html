Which editorial calendar tool should a multi-author team choose?

Choose CoSchedule if you want a marketing calendar out of the box, Asana or monday.com if content shares a workspace with the rest of marketing, and Airtable if you want the calendar to double as a structured content database. Notion, Trello and ClickUp suit teams willing to build their own system and keep it tidy.

  • A ready-made marketing calendar: CoSchedule.
  • Dependencies and approvals inside a broader marketing workspace: Asana.
  • Visual boards with workload views for managers: monday.com.
  • A calendar backed by structured fields and filtered views: Airtable.
  • Briefs, drafts and calendar in one document space: Notion.
  • A simple card-per-piece pipeline: Trello.
  • One configurable tool for every kind of work: ClickUp.

For the wider category, including governance and asset tools, start at the content operations hub.

Why treat the calendar as an operating system rather than a schedule?

With several authors, a date on a grid tells you almost nothing; what you need is the owner, the current stage, what the piece depends on and who approves it. A calendar that records those four things lets an editor spot a slipping piece a week early instead of on the publish date.

That's why the criteria here favour assignment, status and dependency features over pretty month views. If your bottleneck is drafting quality or brief consistency rather than scheduling, look at tools for managing content briefs and assignments instead, and if publishing to many channels is the issue, the broader B2B content marketing platforms go further.

What does each tool do well, and where does it fall short?

The right pick depends on whether you want a calendar that works on day one or a system you shape around your own stages. Fit judgments are our editorial assessment; confirm tier-specific features on each vendor's page.

CoSchedule: best for a calendar that works out of the box

CoSchedule's marketing calendar puts blog posts, social messages and campaigns on one view, with task lists attached to each item so authors see their own steps. It's the fastest way to replace a shared spreadsheet when you don't want to design a system. Tiers are on the CoSchedule pricing page.

Strengths: purpose-built for marketing content and readable by stakeholders. Limitations: less useful when content work has to link to product launches or design requests tracked elsewhere. Skip it if your team already runs everything in a work-management tool.

Asana: best when content depends on other teams' work

Asana handles task dependencies, approvals and timeline views, which helps when a post waits on a product screenshot or a legal review. Content can live in one project with a calendar view while linked tasks sit in design or product projects. See the Asana pricing page for which features each tier includes.

Strengths: clear ownership and dependency tracking across teams. Limitations: there's no content model, so fields like keyword and target URL are custom fields you maintain. Skip it if you only need a content calendar and nothing else.

monday.com: best for managers who need workload at a glance

monday.com boards make status columns, owners and due dates easy to scan, and workload-style views help an editor see who is overcommitted. Check seat options and plan features on the monday.com pricing page.

Strengths: visual, quick to adopt and flexible. Limitations: boards multiply quickly, and reporting quality depends on everyone using the same columns. Skip it if nobody will own the board structure.

Airtable: best for a calendar that is also a content database

Airtable stores each piece as a record with typed fields, so the same base can show a calendar, a kanban by stage and a filtered list of pieces per author. That structure pays off when you also track keywords, funnel stage and published URLs. Plans are on the Airtable pricing page.

Strengths: the strongest data structure here, and easy reporting by field. Limitations: you build the base and automations yourself, and a poorly designed base gets messy. Skip it if nobody on the team enjoys designing systems.

Notion: best for keeping briefs, drafts and the calendar together

Notion databases can hold each piece as a page with its brief and draft inside, shown through calendar and board views. Writers get one place for context, which reduces "where's the brief?" messages. Plans are on the Notion pricing page.

Strengths: rich documents attached to every calendar item. Limitations: approvals, dependencies and deadline alerts are weaker than in task tools. Skip it if formal sign-off is mandatory.

Trello: best for a small team that wants the simplest pipeline

Trello's card-per-piece boards map neatly onto stages such as idea, brief, draft, edit and scheduled, and a calendar view can show due dates. It's easy for freelancers to pick up. Tiers are listed on the Trello pricing page.

Strengths: almost no training. Limitations: reporting across many authors and boards is thin. Skip it if you need workload or cross-team dependencies.

ClickUp: best for teams that want one tool for every kind of work

ClickUp combines tasks, docs, calendars, custom fields and workload-style views, so content, campaigns and client work can share one hierarchy. The flexibility is the selling point and the risk. Plans are on the ClickUp pricing page.

Strengths: wide feature set in one subscription. Limitations: without a named owner, statuses and fields drift across spaces. Skip it if you want opinions rather than options.

Do writers actually keep a shared calendar up to date?

Only when updating it is the easiest way to get their work moving, for example when the editor assigns, reviews and approves inside the tool and nowhere else. Vendor documentation describes the features; it can't show how often authors update status without being chased.

What does a monthly B2B editorial calendar look like?

A workable monthly B2B calendar mixes a fixed rhythm of recurring pieces with a small number of campaign assets, and gives every item an owner, an approver and a date one stage before publication. The model below is hypothetical, for a team of four writers and one editor.

  • Week 1: one search-led article, the monthly newsletter and a customer-facing product update. Briefs for week 3 are due.
  • Week 2: one comparison or pricing article and one thought-leadership post by a subject expert, with legal or product review built in.
  • Week 3: one search-led article and a sales-enablement piece such as a one-pager. Next month's topic list is agreed.
  • Week 4: one refresh of an existing high-value page, plus the monthly performance review. Recurring items for next month are generated.

Set recurring items (newsletter, refreshes, reviews) as repeating tasks so the calendar fills itself, and hold one slot each month open for reactive work.

Which handoff rules keep a multi-author calendar honest?

Every stage needs one owner, a definition of done and a date, and a piece only moves forward when the next owner accepts it. Without those rules, a calendar shows intentions rather than reality.

  1. Brief to writer: the brief is approved and the writer confirms the due date within one working day.
  2. Writer to editor: the draft meets the brief's length and sources rule before the status changes.
  3. Editor to approver: one named approver per piece, with a fixed response window.
  4. Approver to publisher: the published URL is added to the record on the day it goes live.
  5. Late work: if a stage overruns, the owner updates the date and flags the dependency rather than leaving it red.

Where approvals involve clients or several reviewers, a dedicated tool helps; see our guide to content approval software for marketing and creative teams. Rules about who may publish what belong in your content governance setup.

How do you move from a spreadsheet without losing work?

Migrate only active and upcoming pieces, map spreadsheet columns to fields before importing, and run both systems for no more than two weeks. After that, the spreadsheet becomes read-only.

  1. Freeze new rows in the spreadsheet and agree a cut-over date.
  2. Standardise stage names and owner names so they import cleanly.
  3. Map columns to fields: title, owner, stage, due date, approver, keyword, target URL.
  4. Import via CSV where the tool supports it (check the vendor's import documentation first), then spot-check a sample of records.
  5. Rebuild recurring items as repeating tasks rather than copying old rows.
  6. Archive published history separately; it rarely needs to live in the new tool.

What else do teams ask about editorial calendars?

These answers cover free plans, freelancer access and calendar-to-CMS publishing. Check tier details on vendor pages, because feature placement changes.

Is a free plan enough for a multi-author team?

Sometimes, for a small team with simple stages. Free tiers tend to limit views, automations or guest access, which are exactly the features multi-author teams need, so check the limits before you commit.

How should freelancers access the calendar?

Give freelancers guest or limited access to their own items only. Check how each tool counts guests against paid seats, because that often decides the real cost.

Can an editorial calendar publish straight to my CMS?

Work-management tools generally don't publish; purpose-built content platforms are more likely to. If one-click publishing matters, confirm the CMS connection on the vendor's integration page.

How many stages should an editorial workflow have?

Five or six is usually enough: idea, brief, draft, edit, approval and scheduled. More stages add handoffs without adding control.

What's the verdict?

If you want a calendar that works immediately, choose CoSchedule; if content depends on other teams, choose Asana or monday.com; if you want structured data behind the calendar, choose Airtable. Whatever you pick, the handoff rules above matter more than the tool.
