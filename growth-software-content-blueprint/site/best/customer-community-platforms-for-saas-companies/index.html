Best Customer Community Platforms for SaaS Companies

Which customer community platform should a SaaS company choose?

Choose Gainsight Customer Communities or Khoros if your community is a support deflection channel that has to connect to tickets, CRM and customer success data, and choose Discourse if you want a searchable, extensible forum you can own outright. Choose Circle or Bettermode when the community mixes discussion, courses and events for a smaller customer base, and stay on a Slack or Discord server only while your community is mostly conversation among power users.

  • Support deflection tied to your help desk and CS platform: Gainsight Customer Communities.
  • Large, regulated or multi-brand programs with heavy moderation: Khoros Communities.
  • Searchable forum you can self-host or extend: Discourse.
  • Discussion plus courses, events and member spaces in one place: Circle.
  • Embeddable community widgets inside your product or site: Bettermode.
  • Associations-style communities with structured categories: Higher Logic Vanilla.
  • Salesforce shops building a portal with cases and knowledge: Salesforce Experience Cloud.
  • Early beta groups and developer chat: a Discord or Slack server, until search and ownership start to hurt.

What jobs do customer community platforms do, and when is a forum or portal enough?

Customer community platforms give customers a branded, indexed place to answer each other, learn the product, give feedback, attend events and advocate for you. If you only need one of those jobs, a simpler forum, a help-center portal or a chat server is often enough, and you should add a full platform only when two or more jobs overlap.

The five jobs pull in different directions. Peer support wants search, accepted answers and a path to escalate into a ticket. Education wants structured content and progress, which is the territory of a customer education platform. Feedback wants idea voting and status updates, which a dedicated feedback management tool often handles better. Events want registration and reminders. Advocacy wants recognition, badges and a way to find your best champions.

A plain forum is enough when peer support is the only job and your help desk already covers the rest. A help-center portal is enough when customers mostly need to read articles and file tickets, and our knowledge base and self-service portal roundup covers that route. A chat server is enough for a few hundred engaged users who know each other. The signal to move up is when your team keeps answering the same question twice because chat history can't be searched, or when you need sign-in through your product.

What does each community platform do well, and where does it fall short?

The enterprise suites earn their cost through integrations and moderation at scale, while the lighter platforms win on speed of launch and member experience. Each block below names the buyer it suits and the buyer who should skip it.

Gainsight Customer Communities: best for support-led deflection

Gainsight's community product (formerly inSided) is positioned around peer support, ideation and product updates for B2B software customers. Its pull for SaaS teams is the link between community activity and customer success data, so a CSM can see which accounts are engaged. Pricing is quote-based; ask for the current packaging on Gainsight's community page. Skip it if you don't run a help desk or CS platform it integrates with, because that connection is most of the value.

Khoros Communities: best for large or multi-brand programs

Khoros (which absorbed the Lithium community platform) targets large organizations with heavy moderation, gamification and several brands or languages. It suits teams with a community manager and a budget for implementation. Packaging is by quote on the Khoros communities product page. Skip it if you are a small SaaS team launching your first community, because the setup cost will outrun the early value.

Discourse: best for an owned, searchable forum

Discourse is open-source forum software with categories, trust levels, accepted answers and a large plugin library. You can self-host it for free under its open-source licence or pay for hosting; the Discourse pricing page lists the hosted tiers. Its threads are well suited to search, which is what makes peer support compound. Skip it if you need native courses, polished events or a consumer-style feed, or if nobody on the team can maintain a self-hosted server.

Circle: best for discussion, courses and events in one place

Circle combines spaces, posts, live events and courses under one brand, which suits SaaS companies running customer academies or office hours alongside discussion. Plans are tiered on the Circle pricing page. Skip it if you need deep help desk escalation or ticket linking, and confirm which plan includes SSO, custom domains and API access before you commit.

Bettermode: best for embedding community in your product

Bettermode (formerly Tribe) emphasizes customizable spaces and embeddable widgets, so you can surface discussions inside your app or marketing site. That suits product-led teams that want community content next to the feature it relates to. Check the Bettermode pricing page for plan limits. Skip it if your main need is enterprise moderation or regulated-industry controls.

Higher Logic Vanilla: best for structured category forums

Higher Logic acquired Vanilla Forums, and the product keeps its focus on category-based forums, ideation and knowledge-base style answers. It is a reasonable middle ground between a self-hosted forum and an enterprise suite. Pricing is through the Higher Logic Vanilla page. Skip it if you want an all-in-one community with courses and events in the core product.

Salesforce Experience Cloud: best for Salesforce-based portals

Experience Cloud builds customer portals and communities on Salesforce records, so cases, knowledge articles and accounts are native. It is the natural choice when Service Cloud already runs support. Licensing is described on the Experience Cloud overview. Skip it if you have no Salesforce admin, because the build and maintenance work is real.

Discord or Slack: best while the community is still small

A Discord or Slack server is fast to launch and natural for developers and beta users. The trade-off is that conversations are not public web pages, history is hard to search and you don't control the member data in the same way. Skip it if peer support is meant to reduce ticket volume, because unindexed chat rarely deflects anything.

Will a customer community actually reduce support tickets?

A community can deflect repeat questions, but only when threads are searchable, answers are marked as accepted and the help desk links back to them. Vendors' own deflection claims describe their best customers, so treat them as ceilings rather than forecasts.

What are the launch stages, roles and health indicators for a customer community?

Launch in three stages, seeding, opening and scaling, and give each stage an owner and two or three health indicators. Most early communities fail from too few answers, not from the wrong software.

  1. Seed (the first few weeks): invite a small group of engaged customers alongside your own support and product staff. Pre-load the top questions from your ticket data as threads with good answers. Health indicators: share of questions answered, time to first reply.
  2. Open (next quarter): link the community from your help center, in-app help and ticket replies. Add SSO so customers sign in with their product account. Health indicators: share of answers given by customers rather than staff, search visits landing on threads.
  3. Scale: add events, ideation and champion programs. Health indicators: returning contributors per month, accounts with active members, ideas that reached a status update.

Roles: a community manager owns moderation and programming; support owns accepted answers and escalation; product owns idea responses; customer success uses engagement as one input to account health scoring. For measurement depth beyond built-in dashboards, see our guide to community analytics tools.

Before you sign, confirm three ownership points with the vendor: whether you can export members, posts and reactions in a usable format; whether you can use your own domain; and what happens to your content if you cancel.

What mistakes do SaaS teams make when buying a community platform?

The commonest mistake is buying for the advocacy program you hope to run in two years instead of the peer-support job you have today. The second is launching without SSO, which splits customer identity across two logins.

  • No escalation path: an unanswered thread with no route into a ticket is worse than no community.
  • Ignoring search: if threads aren't indexed by your help center search and public search engines, deflection stays low.
  • Duplicating tools: buying a community for ideation when you already run a feedback board creates two backlogs.
  • Measuring vanity metrics: member counts and page views say little; answered questions and returning contributors say more.

What else do buyers ask about customer community platforms?

Most questions are about timing, ownership and whether a chat server will do. The short answers are below.

When should a SaaS company launch a customer community?

Usually once you have enough engaged customers that the same questions recur in tickets, and someone can own moderation for at least a few hours a week. Launching earlier tends to produce an empty forum that signals neglect.

Is Slack or Discord good enough for a customer community?

For a small beta group or developer audience, often yes. Once you need searchable answers, public indexing, SSO with your product or reliable data export, a forum-style platform is a better home.

Do community platforms replace a knowledge base?

No. A knowledge base holds the answers your team maintains, while a community holds answers customers give each other. The two work best when the help center search covers both.

What integrations matter most for a support-led community?

SSO with your product, a link from threads into tickets in your help desk, and a feed of engagement into your CRM or customer success platform. Check each on the specific plan, not just the feature page.

Can we move our community to another platform later?

Usually, but migrations lose things such as reactions, badges and URL structures unless the vendor exports them. Ask for a sample export and a redirect plan before you sign.

What's the verdict on customer community platforms?

For most SaaS companies, the right first community is a searchable forum with SSO and a link into the help desk, which Discourse, Circle or Bettermode can provide without an enterprise contract. Move to Gainsight Customer Communities, Khoros or Experience Cloud when community data needs to flow into customer success and support at scale.
