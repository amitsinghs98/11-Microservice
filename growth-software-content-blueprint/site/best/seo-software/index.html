Which SEO software should you choose?

Most teams should buy one all-in-one suite, keep Google Search Console connected, and add a specialist only when a single job outgrows the suite. Semrush suits generalist agencies, Ahrefs suits link-led research, SE Ranking suits agencies watching per-client cost, and Moz Pro suits in-house generalists.

Those picks follow the criteria in the methodology section below, not a ranking. The specialists earn a place for narrower reasons:

  • Deep technical crawls you configure yourself: Screaming Frog SEO Spider.
  • Very large daily keyword sets with many people logged in: AccuRanker.
  • Multi-location and local service businesses: BrightLocal.
  • First-party search data for any site you own, at no cost: Google Search Console.

How did we choose and judge these platforms?

We judged each platform on the jobs an SEO team does every week and on the limits that force an upgrade, using official product pages and documentation. No hands-on testing was done, and no vendor paid for inclusion or influenced the order.

The rubric has four parts:

  • Coverage of core jobs: keyword research, backlink analysis, site audits, rank tracking and reporting.
  • Limits that bite: projects, tracked keywords, seats, crawl allowances, export rows and report counts.
  • Total team cost: the bill once extra seats, reporting add-ons and white-labelling are counted, not the headline plan.
  • Buyer fit: agency, in-house, local or ecommerce workflows, and the skill level each tool assumes.

The order groups all-in-one suites first, then single-job specialists, then the free baseline.

Which SEO jobs does one platform need to cover?

Five jobs carry most of the weight: keyword research, rank tracking, technical audits, backlink analysis and client or stakeholder reporting. A suite that does all five adequately usually beats a stack of five specialists until one job grows large enough to need its own tool.

  • Keyword research: discovery depth, intent labels, country and city data, and export caps. Our keyword research tools guide for agencies compares these in detail.
  • Rank tracking: keyword quotas, daily or weekly updates and client workspaces. See rank trackers built for multi-client work.
  • Technical audits: crawl limits, JavaScript rendering and scheduling. Larger sites should read our guide to technical audit tools for large websites.
  • Backlinks: referring-domain quality, new and lost links and competitor gaps, covered in our backlink analysis tools roundup.
  • Reporting: scheduled, branded reports and data connectors. Agencies often buy this layer separately, as our SEO reporting software comparison explains.

What does each platform do well, and who should not buy it?

Semrush and Ahrefs are the broadest suites, SE Ranking and Moz Pro trade some depth for easier economics or a gentler learning curve, and the four specialists each do one job well. Every block below ends with the buyer who should look elsewhere.

Semrush: best for generalist agencies

What it does: a broad marketing suite covering keyword and competitor research, backlink analytics, site audits, position tracking, local SEO, content tools, advertising research and reporting.

Pricing model: tiered subscriptions, with extra users and some toolkits billed on top. We couldn't re-confirm current figures for this draft, so check the Semrush plans page.

Strengths: breadth. Paid-search and local data sit beside organic research, which makes it a credible single login for an agency selling several channels.

Limitations: the bill grows with add-ons, so the plan you see first is rarely the plan you pay for once seats and reporting are counted.

Who should not buy: solo operators who'll never open the PPC or content modules, and teams that only need one job such as tracking. A specialist will cost less. To weigh agency economics, see our SE Ranking versus Semrush breakdown.

Ahrefs: best for link-led research

What it does: Site Explorer for backlink and organic competitor research, Keywords Explorer, Site Audit, Rank Tracker and Content Explorer. Ahrefs also offers free Webmaster Tools for sites you verify.

Pricing model: tiered plans with usage allowances and paid extra users; current tiers are on the Ahrefs pricing page.

Strengths: link research is the product's centre of gravity, and its competitor content data is useful for planning.

Limitations: lower tiers carry tighter usage allowances, and rank-tracking update frequency varies by plan, which matters if you report daily movement.

Who should not buy: agencies whose client reports depend on daily rankings, and budget-limited teams that need several people logged in at once. Our Ahrefs alternatives by job map each function to a narrower tool.

SE Ranking: best for cost-conscious agencies

What it does: rank tracking, keyword and competitor research, backlink research, website audits, local tools and a report builder.

Pricing model: tiers scale with tracked keywords and projects, and agency features such as white-label reporting may be an add-on. Confirm the current structure on the SE Ranking subscription page.

Strengths: in our assessment, project and keyword allowances are where it competes, which suits agencies with many small clients.

Limitations: it's a strong generalist rather than the deepest source for any single dataset.

Who should not buy: link-building shops that need the largest link index they can get, and enterprise teams that need governance controls on a self-serve plan.

Moz Pro: best for in-house generalists

What it does: Keyword Explorer, Link Explorer, site crawls, rank tracking and on-page checks. Its Domain Authority score is Moz's own metric, not a Google signal.

Pricing model: tiered plans with limits on keyword queries, tracked keywords and seats. See the Moz Pro pricing page.

Strengths: an approachable interface and a large library of educational material for non-specialists.

Limitations: agencies with dozens of clients should check campaign and query caps before committing.

Who should not buy: PPC-heavy teams, and agencies that want white-label client portals as a core feature.

Screaming Frog SEO Spider: best for hands-on technical audits

What it does: a desktop crawler that finds broken links, redirects, duplicate content, metadata gaps, hreflang problems and structured-data errors, with JavaScript rendering and connections to Google Analytics, Search Console and PageSpeed Insights.

Pricing model: a free version with a crawl cap and a paid annual licence per user, listed on the SEO Spider pricing page.

Strengths: very deep configuration at a modest licence cost. Crawl size is limited mainly by your machine's memory and storage.

Limitations: it isn't a keyword or backlink tool, and desktop crawling makes collaboration harder than a cloud crawler.

Who should not buy: marketers who want a prioritized to-do list rather than raw crawl data.

AccuRanker: best for large daily keyword sets

What it does: dedicated rank tracking with tagging, filtering, Search Console and Analytics connections, and report exports.

Pricing model: priced by the number of tracked keywords; check tiers and seat terms on the AccuRanker pricing page.

Strengths: a keyword-based price ladder is easy to budget against a growing client list.

Limitations: research, links and audits still need other tools.

Who should not buy: small sites tracking a few hundred keywords, where a suite's built-in tracker is cheaper.

BrightLocal: best for multi-location and local work

What it does: local rank and grid tracking, citation building and auditing, review monitoring and Google Business Profile reporting.

Pricing model: plans scale with the number of locations; see the BrightLocal pricing page.

Strengths: it's built around location-level work that general suites often treat as an add-on.

Limitations: thin for national keyword research, backlink analysis or large technical audits.

Who should not buy: ecommerce or SaaS teams without physical locations. Our local SEO software guide for multi-location businesses compares it with other local options.

Google Search Console: the free baseline

What it does: reports the queries, clicks, impressions and pages Google associates with a verified property, plus indexing and experience reports. It's free from Google Search Console.

Strengths: first-party data that no third-party estimate replaces.

Limitations: it covers only sites you can verify, with no competitor research and limited history.

Who should not buy: nobody should skip it, but don't treat it as a complete stack. Our free SEO tools roundup covers what else costs nothing.

What does SEO software really cost a team?

The real cost is the base plan plus every seat beyond those included, plus reporting or white-label add-ons, plus any overage on tracked keywords, projects or locations. For agencies, those extras often decide the comparison more than the headline price does.

Take a hypothetical agency: three people who all need logins, 12 client sites, around 1,800 keywords tracked daily, and branded monthly reports. Before comparing vendors, price each one with the same five lines:

  1. The lowest plan whose project or website cap covers 12 clients.
  2. Seats: how many users the plan includes, and the price of each extra one.
  3. Tracking: whether 1,800 daily keywords fit, and what each extra block costs.
  4. Reporting: whether branded or white-label reports are included or a paid add-on, and whether that add-on requires annual billing.
  5. Billing term: the annual discount against the flexibility of paying monthly while the client list changes.

Run that sheet for two suites and one suite-plus-specialist combination, and the cheapest headline plan often stops being the cheapest bill. Our SEO software pricing guide for 5, 10 and 20 clients models these scenarios in more depth.

Which setup fits your kind of team?

Small teams need one suite plus Search Console; growing agencies need to price seats and keyword quotas side by side; local and ecommerce teams need a specialist for the one job a suite handles thinly. The sub-sections below map each scenario to a starting point.

Solo consultant or two-person agency

One suite plus Search Console is usually enough. SE Ranking or an entry Ahrefs tier keeps costs predictable; add Screaming Frog when an audit outgrows the suite's crawler.

Agency with 10 to 30 clients

Seat and project limits now drive cost. Price SE Ranking and Semrush for the full team, and consider AccuRanker once keyword counts pass the suite's allowance. At this size you also need a client pipeline, which our CRM options for marketing agencies cover.

In-house marketing team

Pick the suite your team will actually open each week; Moz Pro or Semrush suit generalists. Pair either with a reporting layer from our marketing analytics software guide so organic results sit beside other channels.

Local business or multi-location brand

Lead with BrightLocal or a suite with location-level tracking, and keep Google Business Profile work in the same workflow.

Ecommerce store

Large catalogs with faceted navigation need a crawler that copes with scale, so budget for Screaming Frog or a cloud crawler beside Semrush or Ahrefs for category research. Copy teams may also want a content optimization tool for product and category pages.

Specialist workflows

Link builders should start from Ahrefs or a dedicated link index. Teams tracking brand mentions in AI answers should read our AI search visibility tools comparison before paying for a suite add-on.

What mistakes do SEO software buyers make?

The most expensive mistake is paying for two all-in-one suites that duplicate each other's core jobs. The second is comparing headline plans instead of the full team bill.

Map jobs to tools first with our guide to building an SEO stack without duplicate features, then ask each vendor:

  • Does each client site count as a project, and do verified and unverified sites count differently?
  • Are tracked keywords updated daily or weekly, and do mobile results or extra locations count separately?
  • What does each extra user cost, and is there a cap?
  • Is white-label reporting included, and is it tied to annual billing?
  • What happens to historical data if you downgrade or cancel?

What else do buyers ask?

The questions below cover the decisions that come up after the shortlist: suite versus specialists, the Semrush and Ahrefs choice, and how far to trust third-party metrics. Each answer is short enough to settle the point and move on.

Do I need an all-in-one suite or separate SEO tools?

Most small teams get more value from one suite plus free Google tools. Separate tools make sense once a single job, such as tracking thousands of keywords daily, outgrows what the suite includes.

Is Semrush or Ahrefs better for an agency?

It depends on the workflow you sell. Semrush covers more channels, including paid search and local, while Ahrefs centres on link and competitor content research.

Are third-party traffic and authority scores accurate?

They're estimates built from each vendor's own data and models. Use them to compare sites inside one tool, and use Search Console for your own sites' actual performance.

How often should we review our SEO subscriptions?

Check plan limits and prices every quarter and before every annual renewal. Vendors change tiers, allowances and add-ons often enough that last year's best fit can drift.

What's the verdict?

Based on the product information reviewed, Semrush is the broadest single suite, Ahrefs is the natural home for link-led research, and SE Ranking is the one to price first if per-client cost matters most. Screaming Frog, AccuRanker and BrightLocal earn their place only when one job outgrows a suite.

Whatever you choose, keep Search Console connected, price the whole team rather than the plan name, and revisit the decision as your client list changes.
