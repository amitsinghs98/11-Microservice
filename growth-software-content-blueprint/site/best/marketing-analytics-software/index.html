Which marketing analytics tool should a performance team choose?

Most teams should start with Google Analytics 4 for site behavior and Looker Studio for reporting, then add Supermetrics or Funnel when channel data has to be combined reliably. Add Dreamdata for B2B revenue attribution, Mixpanel for product events, or Whatagraph if client-ready reports are the main output.

  • Site and conversion tracking on a small budget: Google Analytics 4.
  • Free, flexible reporting on top of your data: Looker Studio.
  • Pulling ad and channel data into sheets, dashboards or a warehouse: Supermetrics.
  • Normalizing many channels into one governed data set: Funnel.
  • B2B teams tying campaigns to pipeline and revenue: Dreamdata.
  • SaaS teams analyzing signup and in-product events: Mixpanel.
  • Agencies sending polished multi-client reports: Whatagraph.

Which questions should your analytics answer, and where does the data live?

List the five decisions you make most often, such as where to move budget, and write down the source system for each number those decisions need. The tool you buy should close the gap between those sources, not add another place to look.

  • "Which channel should get more budget next week?" Spend and clicks live in the ad platforms; conversions live in web analytics or the CRM. You need a connector layer and agreed conversion definitions.
  • "Where do visitors drop out before converting?" Event data in web or product analytics. Conversion research tools help here; see our roundup of CRO tools for landing pages.
  • "Which campaigns produce pipeline, not just leads?" CRM opportunities joined to touchpoints. That's attribution territory, covered in our guide to marketing attribution without false certainty.
  • "Is email paying for itself?" Email platform data plus revenue; our walkthrough of measuring email ROI alongside deliverability sets out the method.
  • "Is organic search worth the investment?" Search Console, analytics and CRM data together; see our guide to measuring SEO tool ROI.

How do analytics, connectors and dashboards differ?

Analytics tools collect and model behavior data, connectors move data from channel platforms into one place, and dashboards display whatever they're given. Buying a dashboard won't fix missing tracking, and buying analytics won't combine your ad spend.

In this list, Google Analytics 4 and Mixpanel are collection tools. Supermetrics and Funnel are connector and data-preparation layers. Looker Studio and Whatagraph are presentation layers. Dreamdata straddles collection and modeling for B2B revenue. Teams that already run a warehouse should also read our comparison of marketing data integration platforms for lean teams.

What is each tool good and bad at?

Google's two free tools cover a surprising amount, which is why the paid tools have to earn their place by saving time or answering a question the free stack can't. Each block below names that question.

Google Analytics 4: best default for web and app tracking

Good at: event-based tracking, conversion definitions, audiences for Google Ads and exploration reports. Bad at: combining non-Google ad spend, and its numbers shrink where visitors decline consent. Skip it if privacy constraints rule out Google tags; our list of privacy-focused web analytics tools and our guide to GA4 alternatives and complements cover the options. See the official Google Analytics site.

Looker Studio: best free reporting layer

Good at: shareable dashboards on Google sources, sheets and many partner connectors. Bad at: data modeling, and non-Google sources often need a paid partner connector. Skip it if nobody will own report maintenance. See Looker Studio.

Supermetrics: best for moving channel data where you already report

Good at: pulling ad, social and analytics data into sheets, Looker Studio or a warehouse. Bad at: cost predictability as sources and accounts grow. Skip it if you only report on one or two Google sources. Check plan limits on Supermetrics' pricing page.

Funnel: best for normalizing many channels into one data set

Good at: collecting, mapping and harmonizing channel data before it reaches dashboards or a warehouse. Bad at: quick starts; the mapping work is the point. Skip it if you run fewer than a handful of channels. See Funnel's pricing page.

Dreamdata: best for B2B revenue attribution

Good at: joining website, ad and CRM activity at account level to show which touches precede pipeline. Bad at: anything when CRM stages and opportunity data are messy. Skip it if you sell low-consideration B2C products. See Dreamdata's pricing page.

Mixpanel: best for product and funnel events

Good at: funnels, retention and cohort analysis on events you define. Bad at: paid media reporting; it isn't a channel tool. Skip it if you have no product to instrument. Event-based pricing is on Mixpanel's pricing page.

Whatagraph: best for client-ready multi-channel reports

Good at: turning connected channel data into presentable reports quickly. Bad at: deep, ad hoc analysis. Skip it if your audience is an internal analyst rather than a client. See Whatagraph's pricing page.

How do connector and event costs add up?

Connector tools typically charge by data source, destination or account, and event analytics by tracked event or user volume. Your bill grows with channels and traffic, not with how many people read the dashboard.

Before you compare quotes, count four things: the number of data sources, the number of ad accounts or properties per source, the destinations you'll send data to, and your monthly event or user volume. Then ask each vendor what happens when you add a client, a channel or a traffic spike, and whether historical data backfills cost extra. Which KPI definitions should you agree before buying?

Agree definitions for conversion, cost per acquisition, return on ad spend, pipeline contribution and the attribution window before any tool is configured. Without them, two dashboards will report two different truths and the tool will take the blame.

Conversion
The single action that counts, such as a qualified demo request, written down with the event name that records it.
Cost per acquisition
Spend divided by conversions for the same period and channel, with the spend source named.
Return on ad spend
Attributed revenue divided by ad spend, stating which attribution model and window produced the revenue figure.
Pipeline contribution
The value of opportunities created where marketing touched the account within an agreed window.
Attribution window
How long after a click or view a conversion can still be credited, kept the same across tools wherever they allow it.

Will a new analytics tool settle which channel really works?

Not on its own: every tool here reports what its data and model allow, and consent gaps, cross-device journeys and offline conversations leave holes. A tool helps you see more clearly, but it can't remove the judgment call.

What belongs on the implementation checklist?

Write a tracking plan, fix consent and tagging, connect sources, validate against the platforms' own numbers, then build dashboards. Teams that build dashboards first end up redesigning them once the data is fixed.

  1. Write the tracking plan: events, parameters, owners and the decision each event supports.
  2. Set up consent handling and check what is collected before and after consent.
  3. Standardize UTM parameters and campaign naming across every channel.
  4. Connect ad, CRM and email sources, and record which accounts are included.
  5. Validate a week of data against each platform's own reporting and note expected gaps.
  6. Build one dashboard per decision, not one per channel.
  7. Schedule a monthly review of broken connectors, renamed campaigns and new events.

What else do teams ask about marketing analytics software?

The usual follow-ups cover GA4's limits, warehouses and agency reporting. Here are short answers.

Is GA4 enough for a performance team?

It's enough for site behavior and conversions on many small teams. It isn't enough when you need non-Google spend, CRM revenue and offline conversions in one view.

Do we need a data warehouse for marketing analytics?

Not at first. A warehouse earns its keep when several sources must be joined, history must be kept beyond platform limits, or analysts need SQL access.

Should an agency use the same analytics stack for every client?

Standardize the connector and reporting layers where you can, because that's where agency time goes. Leave each client's tracking tool in place unless it's clearly broken.

How long does a marketing analytics implementation take?

As a planning assumption, a tracking fix and a first dashboard take a few weeks, while multi-source attribution takes longer because CRM data usually needs cleaning first.

What's the verdict?

Start with GA4 and Looker Studio, add Supermetrics or Funnel when combining channels costs real hours, and add Dreamdata or Mixpanel only when you have a revenue or product question the free stack can't answer. Buy for decisions, not for dashboards.
