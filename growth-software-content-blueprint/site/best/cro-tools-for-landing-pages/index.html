Quick answer

Most landing-page programmes stall in the same place: the team buys a testing tool before it knows what to test. Start with measurement and observation, then add experimentation once you have hypotheses and enough traffic to judge them.

  • Free starting point for behaviour research: Microsoft Clarity, paired with Google Analytics 4 for conversion data.
  • Heatmaps plus on-page surveys in one product: Hotjar.
  • Lead-gen forms that lose people mid-way: Zuko for field-level form analytics.
  • Testing messaging before you spend traffic: Lyssna for preference and five-second tests.
  • Marketers who build and test pages without developers: Unbounce.
  • One vendor for research and testing at mid-market scale: VWO.
  • Enterprise experimentation and personalisation: Optimizely.

The CRO workflow: discovery, hypothesis, test

Conversion rate optimisation is a loop, and each stage needs a different kind of tool. Buying for the wrong stage is the most common way CRO budgets get wasted.

  1. Find the leak (quantitative). Analytics tells you which pages, sources and devices underperform. Without this, you are guessing where to look.
  2. Understand why (qualitative). Heatmaps, session replay, form analytics and on-page surveys show what visitors do and say on the leaking page. Our guide to heatmap and session replay tools for conversion research goes further on this step.
  3. Write a hypothesis. A useful format: "Because we observed X, we believe changing Y for audience Z will improve metric M."
  4. Pre-test cheaply where you can. Unmoderated user tests can kill weak ideas before they consume weeks of traffic.
  5. Test or ship. Run a controlled experiment when traffic allows it. When it does not, ship the change, monitor it, and treat the result as directional.
  6. Measure downstream. A lift in form fills that produces worse leads is not a win. Connect results to pipeline or revenue, which is where a realistic approach to marketing attribution for small teams matters.

Testing and analytics are different jobs. Analytics describes what happened; a test isolates the effect of one change by randomly splitting visitors. Neither replaces the other.

The tools

Google Analytics 4: the baseline for finding leaks

Best for: any team that needs landing-page conversion rates by source, campaign and device. Skip it if: you need a lightweight, cookie-light setup; our roundup of privacy-conscious web analytics tools covers that route.

GA4 is event-based, so landing-page conversions depend on correctly configured key events and UTM discipline. Its explorations can isolate a page's funnel, and it connects with Google Ads for paid campaigns. The limitation for CRO is that it shows what happened, not why, and reports can be confusing for non-analysts. Google lists a free standard tier and a paid Analytics 360 tier; check the official Google Analytics site for current terms. Before you choose, audit whether your conversion events actually fire.

Microsoft Clarity: free heatmaps and replay

Best for: teams that want click maps, scroll maps and session recordings without a budget line. Skip it if: you need surveys, funnels built for marketers, or a contract with formal support terms.

Based on Microsoft's product information, Clarity offers heatmaps, session recordings and automatic flags for behaviours such as rage clicks, dead clicks and quick backs, plus a GA4 integration. Microsoft positions it as free. The limitation is a thinner research toolkit than paid tools, and you still own consent and masking configuration. Review the Microsoft Clarity site and its privacy documentation before installing it on pages that collect personal data.

Hotjar: behaviour research plus voice of the visitor

Best for: marketers who want heatmaps, recordings and on-page surveys or feedback widgets from one vendor. Skip it if: your only need is heatmaps, where a free tool may be enough.

Hotjar, now part of Contentsquare, combines observation with asking visitors directly, which is useful when replay shows hesitation but not the reason. Paid plans scale with the number of sessions captured, so high-traffic pages may record only a sample unless you upgrade. Check the Hotjar pricing page for current session allowances. Teams already weighing a switch can compare options in our Hotjar alternatives breakdown.

Zuko: form analytics for lead-gen pages

Best for: B2B and lead-gen pages where the form is the conversion. Skip it if: your page converts with a single button or a short email field.

Zuko focuses on field-level form behaviour: which field people abandon on, how long they spend in each one, and which fields trigger errors or corrections. It does one job, so you will still need analytics and a way to test changes. Review plans on the Zuko website.

Lyssna: pre-test messaging before spending traffic

Best for: low-traffic pages and teams choosing between headlines, hero layouts or value propositions. Skip it if: you have enough traffic to run live tests quickly and your audience is hard to recruit from a general panel.

Lyssna (formerly UsabilityHub) runs unmoderated tests such as five-second tests, first-click tests, preference tests and surveys, with an optional participant panel. It does not measure real visitor behaviour, so treat results as a filter for ideas, not proof of lift. Panel recruitment is usually charged separately from the subscription. See the Lyssna pricing page.

Unbounce: build and test pages without developers

Best for: paid-media teams that launch many campaign pages and want built-in A/B testing. Skip it if: your landing pages must live inside your main CMS or ecommerce theme.

Unbounce combines a drag-and-drop page builder with A/B testing and Smart Traffic, an AI feature that routes visitors to the variant predicted to convert best for them. The trade-off is that testing applies to pages hosted on Unbounce, and Smart Traffic optimises allocation rather than producing a clean, conventional test readout. Plans are tiered by traffic and features; see the Unbounce pricing page.

VWO: research and testing under one contract

Best for: mid-market teams that want heatmaps, recordings, surveys, form analytics and A/B testing in one platform. Skip it if: you only need occasional tests and would use few of the modules.

VWO sells separate products for testing, behaviour insights, personalisation and server-side experimentation, so you can start with one and add others. Its visual editor lets marketers build simple variants, while complex changes still need developer time. Pricing is modular and scales with tracked traffic, so model your monthly visitors before a sales call. Review the VWO pricing page.

Optimizely: enterprise experimentation and personalisation

Best for: large organisations running many concurrent experiments across web and product, with dedicated developers and analysts. Skip it if: you are a small team running a handful of landing-page tests a quarter.

Optimizely offers web experimentation, feature experimentation and personalisation, with a statistics engine designed to let teams monitor results without the usual peeking problems. It is typically sold through a sales process with annual contracts. Start at the Optimizely website.

Traffic requirements and pricing models

Research tools need relatively little traffic: a few hundred recordings of a key page can reveal obvious friction. Testing tools need far more, because detecting a difference reliably requires a large sample.

As an illustration using the standard two-proportion sample-size formula (95% confidence, 80% power): a page converting at 4% needs roughly 6,700 visitors per variant to detect a lift to 5%. A page with 3,000 visitors a month would need about four and a half months for a two-variant test. Smaller expected lifts need much larger samples. This is arithmetic, not a vendor claim, and your testing tool's own calculator should take precedence.

Pricing models follow the same split. Analytics and replay tools usually price by sessions or pageviews; testing platforms by monthly tracked users or visitors, often with annual commitments; page builders by plan tier and traffic; research panels per participant. A fair budget comparison counts the traffic you will actually send through each tool. For how these costs sit alongside automation and email spend, see our small-team marketing automation shortlist.

Prioritised landing-page checklist

Work through these in order. Earlier items are cheaper to fix and often matter more than anything you would test.

  1. Tracking works. Conversion events fire once per conversion, and UTMs are consistent across campaigns.
  2. Message match. The headline repeats the promise of the ad or email that sent the visitor. If you draft headline variants with AI, our review of AI copywriting software for ads and landing pages covers the editorial checks they need.
  3. Speed and mobile layout. The main offer and call to action load quickly and are visible on a phone without hunting.
  4. One clear action. Competing links and secondary offers are removed or demoted.
  5. Form friction. Every field is justified; error messages are specific; abandonment is measured by field.
  6. Proof near the decision. Genuine, permissioned testimonials, logos or data sit close to the call to action.
  7. Objection handling. Pricing, commitment and data-use questions are answered on the page.
  8. Behaviour review. Heatmaps and replays checked for rage clicks, dead clicks and drop-off points.
  9. Visitor feedback. A short exit or on-page survey asks what stopped people.
  10. Test the biggest remaining hypothesis. Only now commit traffic to an experiment.

Recommendations by scenario

  • Startup with one landing page and little traffic: GA4, Clarity and a few Lyssna tests. Ship improvements rather than waiting months for significance.
  • B2B demand-gen team with a long form: Zuko plus a replay tool. Fix field-level friction, then check lead quality in the CRM. Where a demo request is the goal, pairing the form with instant scheduling and lead routing can matter as much as the form itself.
  • Paid-media agency launching many client pages: Unbounce for build-and-test speed, with Clarity or Hotjar for behaviour research.
  • Growing ecommerce or SaaS team with steady traffic: VWO for an integrated programme, or a dedicated testing tool from our A/B testing comparison.
  • Enterprise with an experimentation team: Optimizely, budgeted alongside the engineering time it needs.

Buying mistakes and questions to ask

Common mistakes: buying a testing platform for a page with too little traffic, testing on pages with broken tracking, and declaring winners on form fills without checking lead quality.

Questions to ask vendors:

  • What counts towards the usage limit: sessions, pageviews, tracked users or tested visitors?
  • What happens when we exceed it: sampling, overage charges or a forced upgrade?
  • How are form inputs and personal data masked by default?
  • Does the testing script cause page flicker, and how is it mitigated?
  • Can results be exported or sent to our analytics and CRM?

FAQs

Do I need an A/B testing tool to do CRO?

No. Many of the highest-value fixes come from tracking repairs, message match and form friction found through research. Testing becomes worthwhile once you have hypotheses and enough traffic to measure them.

Is a free heatmap tool good enough for landing pages?

Often, yes, for observing behaviour. Paid tools add surveys, richer filtering, integrations and support.

Can I run CRO on a page with very low traffic?

Yes, but rely on research and pre-testing rather than live experiments. Make larger, evidence-based changes, then monitor conversion over time and treat the result as directional.

How do privacy rules affect CRO tools?

Replay and heatmap scripts can capture personal data. Configure masking, respect your consent banner and check each vendor's data-processing terms with your legal adviser.

Verdict

Our editorial assessment: buy for the stage you are actually stuck on. Most landing-page teams get further with GA4, a replay tool and a disciplined checklist than with an enterprise testing contract. Add Zuko or Lyssna where forms or messaging are the bottleneck, and move to Unbounce, VWO or Optimizely when traffic and team capacity can support real experiments.
