Best Free SEO Tools for Small Agencies

The free SEO tools worth building on are the ones that read a client's own data: Google Search Console, Bing Webmaster Tools, GA4, PageSpeed Insights and Google Business Profile. Add a capped crawler and two limited suite accounts, and a small agency can deliver real work before it pays for a subscription.

Which free SEO tools should a small agency set up first?

Start with Google Search Console, then add GA4, Google Business Profile for local clients, and Bing Webmaster Tools as a second index view. Those four are free from the search engines themselves, and every later step in your workflow depends on the first-party data they hold.

  • First-party search data: Google Search Console, with Bing Webmaster Tools alongside it.
  • Crawling small brochure sites: the free version of Screaming Frog SEO Spider.
  • Backlink and site-health data on sites you can verify: Ahrefs' free access for site owners.
  • A handful of competitor lookups for a pitch: a free Semrush account, which is capped.
  • Client dashboards at no cost: Looker Studio connected to Search Console and GA4.
  • Local presence: Google Business Profile itself.

Who is a free stack realistic for?

It suits freelancers and agencies of one to three people with a few retainers, where you already have access to every client site. Once you're pitching prospects you can't access or reporting on many accounts, the hours spent on workarounds start to cost more than a subscription.

It also suits in-house marketers who need to show a finance team exactly what free tools can't do before a paid tool gets approved. If you've already outgrown that stage, our shortlist of SEO platforms by team type is the better starting point, and the SEO software hub maps every related guide.

How did we choose these tools?

A tool made the list only if an agency can use it for ongoing client work at no cost, beyond the end of any trial, and it covers a distinct job: keyword discovery, technical checks, performance, local presence or reporting. Time-limited trials are named separately, so nobody plans a workflow around something that expires.

What does each tool do, and where does it stop?

Each free tool does one job well and stops before the next one, which is why a free stack needs several of them. The blocks below give the job, the limit that matters and the point at which to skip it.

Google Search Console: best for every client from day one

What it does: shows how Google crawls, indexes and displays a verified site, including the queries and pages that earn impressions and clicks. Key capabilities: the performance report, URL inspection, indexing and sitemap reports, and a Core Web Vitals report built on field data. Pricing model: free from Google.

Strengths: it's Google's own data about the client's site, which no third-party estimate replaces. Limitations: you see nothing about competitors, history only goes back a limited window, and interface exports are capped, so bigger pulls need the API or a dashboard connector. Skip it if: never. Get Owner or Full access during onboarding, not halfway through month one.

Bing Webmaster Tools: best as a second index view

What it does: Bing's counterpart to Search Console, with crawl, indexing and query data for verified sites, plus a site scan for on-page issues. Pricing model: free. Strengths: sites can be imported from Search Console, so setup is quick. Limitations: its keyword data reflects Bing searchers, so don't present it as Google demand. Skip it if: the client gets negligible Bing traffic and you're short of setup time.

Google Analytics 4: best for tying organic visits to leads

What it does: event-based analytics with key events (conversions). Pricing model: the standard product is free, and Google sells an enterprise tier separately. Strengths: most clients already have it, and it connects natively to Looker Studio.

Limitations: configuration takes real effort, some reports apply data thresholds, and exploration data retention depends on a setting many properties leave at its default. Skip it if: the client has moved to another analytics tool. Our guide to GA4 alternatives and complementary analytics covers that situation.

PageSpeed Insights: best for explaining slow templates

What it does: runs a Lighthouse lab test and shows real-user field data where Google has enough of it. Pricing model: free, with an API for batch checks. Strengths: recommendations name specific resources, which gives developers something to act on. Limitations: the interface tests one URL at a time, and lab scores move between runs, so test representative templates rather than chasing one number. Skip it if: you need ongoing monitoring across many pages, which is a job for dedicated website performance monitoring.

Google Business Profile: best for any client with a location

What it does: manages the listing that appears in Maps and the local pack, including categories, hours, photos, posts, reviews and performance insights. Pricing model: free. Limitations: it reports on your own listing only. There are no rank grids, citation audits or competitor views. Skip it if: the client has no physical location or service area. Multi-location clients will soon want the tools in our local SEO software guide.

Looker Studio: best for templated monthly reports

What it does: Google's dashboarding tool, with native connectors for Search Console, GA4 and other Google sources. Pricing model: free, with a paid Pro tier for team management. Strengths: one template can be copied per client. Limitations: connectors for non-Google sources are often third-party and paid, and a broken data source can go unnoticed until a client asks. Skip it if: clients expect white-label reports that blend SEO with paid and social data.

Screaming Frog SEO Spider (free version): best for small-site audits

What it does: a desktop crawler that finds broken links, redirect chains, duplicate titles, missing metadata and similar on-site issues. Pricing model: a free version capped at a set number of URLs per crawl, plus a paid annual licence that removes the cap and unlocks advanced features. The cap and licence price weren't re-confirmed for this draft, so check the SEO Spider product page before you plan around them.

Strengths: it shows the raw data a crawler sees, which is the fastest way to learn technical SEO. Limitations: it runs on your machine and needs some technical skill. Skip it if: client sites regularly exceed the cap. Our technical SEO audit tools for large websites cover what comes next.

Ahrefs free access: best for backlink checks on sites you control

What it does: Ahrefs offers website owners limited free access to its site-level tools, including a cloud site audit and backlink data, once ownership is verified. Pricing model: free, with paid plans above it. Strengths: Ahrefs' own link index at no cost, and a crawler that doesn't need a desktop app. Limitations: it's built around sites you own, so it won't support competitor research. Skip it if: your main need is prospect analysis. See the current tiers on Ahrefs' pricing page.

Semrush free account: best for a few lookups during a pitch

What it does: limited access to Semrush's research tools without a paid plan. Pricing model: free, capped by daily report requests and project count. Check Semrush's plans page for the current limits. Strengths: competitor visibility that first-party tools can't give you. Limitations: the caps run out quickly in real research. Skip it if: you need repeatable monthly competitor tracking for several clients.

Google Keyword Planner: best for commercial keyword themes

What it does: generates keyword ideas with volume and bid estimates inside Google Ads. Pricing model: free, but it needs a Google Ads account. Limitations: it's built for advertisers, groups close variants, can show broad volume ranges depending on the account, and has no organic difficulty score. Skip it if: you need precise organic estimates. Our keyword research tools comparison explains what paid databases add.

What does a zero-budget client workflow look like?

It runs in four passes: onboarding access, a baseline audit, keyword discovery from real queries, and a monthly templated report. Rankings come from Search Console average position, explained honestly, rather than from a daily rank checker.

  1. Onboarding, week one: request access to Search Console, GA4 and Business Profile, then verify the site in Bing Webmaster Tools and Ahrefs.
  2. Baseline audit: crawl with the Screaming Frog free version, or Ahrefs' cloud audit if the site exceeds the cap. Then run the five most important templates through PageSpeed Insights.
  3. Keyword discovery: export Search Console queries with high impressions and a low click-through rate, expand them in Keyword Planner, and spend a few Semrush free reports on competitor gaps.
  4. Monthly reporting: a Looker Studio template with Search Console clicks and impressions, GA4 organic key events and three sentences of commentary.

Which paid tasks have a no-cost substitute?

Rank tracking, content gap research, schema validation and brief writing all have workable free substitutes. Each one is slower or less precise than the paid version, so use them while client count is low.

  • Rank tracking: Search Console's query and page filters, compared month on month. It's an average across impressions, not a daily position check, but it reflects real searches.
  • Content gap research: the live results page, People Also Ask boxes and Google Trends, reviewed by hand.
  • Schema validation: Google's Rich Results Test.
  • Content briefs: a free AI assistant with careful editing. Our roundup of free AI writing tools and their limits shows where they fall short.

When does a paid plan become necessary?

Pay when a client needs daily rank tracking for a fixed keyword set, when you pitch prospects whose sites you can't access, or when building reports by hand eats billable hours. In our assessment, many small agencies reach that point at around four or five active retainers, though your service mix moves the line.

At that point, compare suites before you buy single-purpose tools one at a time. What mistakes do agencies make with free tools?

The common ones are using personal Google accounts for client properties, treating a trial as a free tool, and presenting estimates as exact traffic. Each is cheap to avoid and painful to unwind later.

  • Adding client properties to a personal Google account. Use an agency account with documented access, so access survives staff changes.
  • Building a workflow on a trial. Export everything you need before it ends.
  • Reporting Keyword Planner ranges or third-party volume estimates as precise figures.
  • Never asking whether a free tier permits client work, what you can export, and what happens to projects when you stop paying.

What else do agencies ask about free SEO tools?

Most questions are about whether free tiers allow client work and how they differ from trials. The short answers are below.

Can an agency run client SEO entirely on free tools?

Yes, for a small number of clients whose sites you can access. The gaps are competitor research, reliable daily rank tracking and polished white-label reporting.

Is Ahrefs' free access the same as a trial?

No. It is limited, ongoing access to site-level data for sites you verify, not a time-limited version of the paid plans. Check the current scope on Ahrefs' own pages.

Which free tool should a new agency set up first?

Google Search Console. Keyword discovery, reporting and technical checks all draw on the query and indexing data it provides.

Does Search Console average position replace a rank tracker?

Not fully. It averages positions across real impressions, which is useful for trends, but it can't show a fixed daily position for a chosen keyword in a chosen location.

What's the verdict?

The free stack is genuinely useful because it's built on first-party data, but it breaks on competitor research and scale. Use it to prove value on early retainers, then upgrade when workarounds cost more hours than a subscription costs money.
