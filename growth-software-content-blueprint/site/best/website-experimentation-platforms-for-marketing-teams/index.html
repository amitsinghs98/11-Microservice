Which website experimentation platform should a marketing team choose?

Choose VWO or Convert if you want a marketer-friendly visual editor with published or easily obtained pricing, and Optimizely or AB Tasty if you're running a large, governed programme across brands or regions. Use Webflow Optimize when the site is on Webflow, and GrowthBook when engineers will implement tests in code and analyse them in your data warehouse.

  • Mid-sized marketing teams: VWO or Convert.
  • Enterprise programmes with governance needs: Optimizely Web Experimentation or AB Tasty.
  • Sites built on Webflow: Webflow Optimize.
  • Product and engineering-led testing: GrowthBook.

How is marketer-controlled web testing different from product experimentation?

Marketer-controlled web tests change what visitors see on marketing pages, usually through a visual editor and a browser script, and they measure goals like form submissions or demo requests. Product experimentation changes application behaviour through feature flags in code, and measures retention, activation or revenue from product data.

The distinction matters for buying. A marketing team that buys a flag-first product tool will wait on developers for every headline test, and a product team that buys a visual editor will struggle to test pricing logic or onboarding flows. What is each platform best for, and when should you skip it?

VWO and Convert are the most practical starting points for marketers, while Optimizely and AB Tasty earn their cost at enterprise scale. Webflow Optimize and GrowthBook are excellent within narrower boundaries.

VWO: best for mid-sized marketing teams

VWO combines testing with behavioural analytics such as heatmaps and session recordings. Its pricing page shows Web Experimentation tiers priced by monthly tracked users (checked 4 November 2026), with paid figures available on request. Having research and testing in one place helps teams that don't yet run a dedicated research tool.

Limitations: you'll need a quote to model cost, and traffic growth moves you up tiers.

Skip it if you need a published, fixed price to get budget approval.

Convert: best for teams that want transparent pricing

Convert Experiences is a testing platform with visual and code editors. Its pricing page lists Growth from $299 a month billed annually for 100,000 monthly tested users, with Pro above it and custom Enterprise (checked 4 November 2026). That makes it one of the few tools here you can budget without a sales call.

Limitations: it's a testing tool rather than a research suite, so you'll pair it with a heatmap or analytics product.

Skip it if you want testing and behavioural analytics from one vendor.

Optimizely Web Experimentation: best for enterprise programmes

Optimizely offers web and feature experimentation across a broader digital experience platform. Its support documentation says cost varies by company, depending on factors such as site traffic, products chosen and implementation complexity (checked 4 November 2026), and its visual editor documentation describes a WYSIWYG editor for building variations.

Limitations: it's priced and sold for large programmes, and smaller teams may pay for governance they won't use.

Skip it if you'll run a handful of tests a quarter.

AB Tasty: best for enterprise testing with personalization

AB Tasty covers experimentation and personalization, and its pricing page asks for a custom quote (checked 4 November 2026). AB Tasty describes itself as part of Wingify alongside VWO, so expect product and packaging changes as the two are integrated.

Limitations: pricing is quote-only, and the integration adds roadmap uncertainty.

Skip it if you want a stable, published price list.

Webflow Optimize: best for sites built on Webflow

Webflow Optimize runs tests and personalization natively inside Webflow. Webflow describes A/B testing, AI-driven traffic allocation and audience personalization (checked 4 November 2026), and its pricing page lists Optimize as a paid add-on priced by page views.

Limitations: it only works on Webflow-hosted sites.

Skip it if you might leave Webflow, or test pages on other platforms too.

GrowthBook: best when engineers run the tests

GrowthBook is an open-source feature flagging and experimentation platform that analyses results from your own data warehouse. Its pricing page lists a free Starter plan for up to 3 users and Pro at $40 per seat per month (checked 4 November 2026), and its documentation describes automatic sample ratio mismatch checks on experiment results.

Limitations: it's built for developers and data teams, and marketers will depend on them for most page changes.

Skip it if nobody on your team writes code or SQL.

Do these tools really let marketers test without developers?

For copy, layout and form changes on templated pages, the visual editors are designed for that, but complex or dynamic pages usually still need code. The bigger risk, based on vendor documentation, is weak QA: broken variants and misconfigured targeting produce results that look valid but aren't.

How should you QA an experiment before and during launch?

Check every variant on real devices, confirm goals fire in both variants, and watch the traffic split in the first day for a sample ratio mismatch. Stop and fix anything unexpected before you look at conversion results.

  1. Preview each variant on desktop, mobile and the main browsers.
  2. Confirm the goal or conversion event fires in every variant, and in your analytics tool too.
  3. Check targeting rules exclude internal traffic, bots and pages the test shouldn't touch.
  4. Check for flicker and layout shift with the testing script in place.
  5. Verify the split after the first day; a sample ratio mismatch means pause and investigate.
  6. Decide the run length and stopping rule before launch, not after you see results.

What belongs in an experiment decision log?

Each entry should record the hypothesis, the primary metric, the run dates, the result and the decision taken, so anyone can see why a page looks the way it does. Without a log, teams rerun old tests and lose the reasoning behind wins.

  • Hypothesis: what you changed, for whom, and why you expected it to work.
  • Primary and guardrail metrics: for example demo requests, with bounce rate or lead quality as guardrails.
  • Setup: pages, audience, split, start and end dates.
  • QA notes: anything fixed during the run, including a sample ratio mismatch.
  • Result and decision: ship, iterate or discard, and who approved it.

What mistakes do teams make when buying an experimentation platform?

The most expensive mistake is buying an enterprise platform before you have the traffic or the people to run more than a few tests a quarter. The second is choosing a developer-first tool when marketers are the ones who'll run it.

  • Not modelling how pricing changes as traffic grows.
  • Ignoring the script's effect on page speed.
  • Testing on pages with too little traffic to reach a result.
  • Skipping the decision log, so lessons disappear when people leave.

What else do marketing teams ask about experimentation platforms?

These are the questions that usually decide whether a programme gets funded. The answers are general guidance, not vendor claims.

How much traffic do I need to run website experiments?

It depends on your baseline conversion rate and the smallest lift you care about detecting, so run a sample-size calculation before launch. Low-traffic pages often need bigger changes or longer tests to reach a reliable result.

Do client-side testing tools slow down pages?

They can, because the script has to load and apply changes before the visitor sees the page. Ask each vendor how its snippet loads, how it prevents flicker, and what it recommends for performance, then measure your own pages before and after.

Should marketing and product share one experimentation tool?

Sometimes, but the jobs differ. Marketers need a visual editor and page targeting, while product teams need feature flags and warehouse metrics, so many companies run one tool for each and share a single decision log.

What is a sample ratio mismatch?

It's when the split of visitors between variants differs from what you configured by more than chance would explain. It usually signals a bug in targeting, redirects or tracking, and results shouldn't be trusted until it's fixed.
