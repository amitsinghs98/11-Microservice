Best Corporate Blog Publishing Platforms for B2B Brands

When comparing corporate blog publishing platforms, B2B brands should weigh how well each one publishes to the web and the inbox at once, keeps the subscriber list under company control, and fits the approval process. HubSpot Content Hub, WordPress and Webflow suit a blog that lives inside the company website, while Ghost, beehiiv, Kit and Substack suit a publication that behaves more like an independent newsletter.

Which blog publishing platform should you choose?

If the blog must sit on your main domain, feed your CRM and follow brand and legal approvals, publish from your website platform: HubSpot Content Hub, WordPress or Webflow. If you're building a named publication with its own audience and an email-first rhythm, a publishing platform such as Ghost, beehiiv or Kit is usually faster and cheaper to run well.

  • Blog, email and CRM in one system with marketing automation: HubSpot Content Hub.
  • Maximum control, plugins and hosting choice: WordPress.
  • Design-led marketing site where the blog shares the brand system: Webflow.
  • Web-plus-email publication on your own domain, with memberships: Ghost.
  • Newsletter-first publication with growth and referral tools: beehiiv.
  • Email publishing with strong automations and landing pages: Kit.
  • A personal-brand publication from an executive or founder: Substack.

Should you run a company blog or an independent publication?

Run a company blog when the goal is search traffic, product education and conversions on your own site. Run an independent-style publication when the goal is a loyal reader base that opens every issue, even if that means a separate brand, domain or subdomain.

The choice changes what you optimize. A company blog is judged by organic sessions, assisted conversions and sales enablement, so SEO controls, CMS integration and CRM tracking matter most. A publication is judged by subscribers, open behavior and replies, so the email editor, list management, recommendations and archive design matter more. Many B2B teams run both: the blog for search, a newsletter for relationships, connected by signup forms.

What is each platform best for, and when should you skip it?

The website platforms win on SEO, design and CRM integration, and the newsletter-first platforms win on subscriber growth and email rhythm. Decide which half matters more, then check the weaker half is good enough.

HubSpot Content Hub: best when the blog must feed the CRM

What it does: a hosted CMS with blog, landing pages and content tools tied to HubSpot's CRM, so subscribers and readers become contact records that marketing automation can act on. See HubSpot's Content Hub pricing page.

Strengths: one record for readers, leads and customers, with approvals and roles. Limitations: check which email and automation features Content Hub includes on its own and which need other HubSpot products, because that changes the total cost. Skip it if you don't use HubSpot's CRM.

WordPress: best for control and extensibility

What it does: open-source publishing software with a block editor, roles and a large plugin and theme ecosystem; see WordPress.org. Email delivery usually comes from a plugin or a connected email platform.

Strengths: full ownership of content and data, and almost any workflow can be added. Limitations: updates, security and plugin quality are your job or your host's. Skip it if nobody owns site maintenance. Our comparison of WordPress and Webflow for marketing sites goes deeper.

Webflow: best for a design-led blog inside the marketing site

What it does: a visual site builder with hosting and CMS collections, so blog posts share the site's design system. See Webflow's pricing page.

Strengths: designers can build and change templates without developers. Limitations: it's a website platform, so plan to connect an email tool for the newsletter edition. Skip it if the email edition matters more than the web page.

Ghost: best for a web-plus-email publication on your own domain

What it does: open-source publishing that sends each post as a newsletter and supports free and paid memberships, hosted on Ghost(Pro) or self-hosted. See Ghost's pricing page.

Strengths: clean editor, custom domain and real ownership of content and members. Limitations: it isn't a full marketing-site CMS, and CRM connections need integrations. Skip it if the blog must share templates with your product site.

beehiiv: best for newsletter-first growth

What it does: newsletter publishing with a web archive, referral and recommendation tools, segmentation and monetization options gated by plan. See beehiiv's pricing page.

Strengths: tools aimed squarely at growing a subscriber base. Limitations: lighter SEO and site controls than a CMS. Skip it if organic search is the main goal.

Kit: best for email publishing with automations

What it does: formerly ConvertKit, an email platform with tagging, visual automations, landing pages and paid newsletter options. See Kit's pricing page.

Strengths: segmentation and sequences for nurturing readers. Limitations: the web archive is secondary. Skip it if you need a search-optimized blog; pair it with a CMS instead.

Substack: best for an executive's personal publication

What it does: free publishing with an app and recommendation network, funded by a share of paid subscription revenue. See Substack's site.

Strengths: very fast to start, with built-in discovery. Limitations: limited brand control, integrations and team workflow. Skip it if the publication is the company's, not a person's.

Will a company newsletter really build an owned audience?

Only if readers get something they can't get from your sales emails, on a predictable schedule. The platform makes publishing easier, but none of them makes a promotional newsletter worth subscribing to.

What operational controls matter for a B2B brand?

Prioritize roles and approvals, a custom domain, full content and subscriber export, and consent records you can show an auditor. Those decide whether the blog survives a team change, a rebrand or a platform switch.

  • Roles and approvals: contributors draft, editors approve, legal reviews where required.
  • Custom domain and sending domain: publish on your domain and authenticate email so it lands in the inbox.
  • Exportability: test exporting posts, images and subscribers with consent fields before you commit.
  • Segmentation: separate customers, prospects and partners so each gets relevant issues.
  • Analytics: connect web and email results to your CRM, not just open counts.
  • Archives: keep every issue as an indexable web page so email work also earns search value.

What mistakes do B2B teams make choosing a blog platform?

The usual mistake is picking a platform for its editor and discovering later that subscribers, SEO or CRM data are trapped. The second is splitting the blog and newsletter across tools with no shared signup or tracking.

  • Publishing on a platform subdomain: the search value builds on a domain you don't control.
  • No export test: find out how members and posts leave before you have thousands.
  • Treating monetization as a goal: most B2B brands should measure pipeline, not paid subscriptions.
  • Ignoring governance: contributors outside marketing need clear approval steps; see content approval software.

What else do teams ask about B2B blog platforms?

Teams usually ask about domains, ownership and running two tools. The answers below stay brief.

Can we run our blog and newsletter on different platforms?

Yes, and many B2B teams do: a CMS for the blog and an email tool for the newsletter. Connect them with shared signup forms and consistent tracking so readers and data don't split.

Should a corporate blog live on a subdomain or a subfolder?

Most teams choose a subfolder on the main domain so the blog and product pages are clearly one site. A subdomain can make sense for a separately branded publication.

Do we own our subscriber list on these platforms?

Generally you can export it, but confirm what the export includes, such as consent fields, tags and paid status. Test the export early rather than at migration time.

Is Substack suitable for a company publication?

It's better suited to individuals. Brands usually need more control over design, integrations, roles and data than it offers.

What's the verdict?

Publish from HubSpot Content Hub, WordPress or Webflow when the blog's job is search, conversion and CRM data, and from Ghost, beehiiv or Kit when its job is an engaged subscriber base. Keep Substack for executives' personal publications.
