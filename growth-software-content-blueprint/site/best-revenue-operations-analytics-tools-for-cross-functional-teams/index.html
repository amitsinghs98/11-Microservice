Which RevOps analytics tool should you choose?

If your data lives mostly in one CRM, start with its native reporting and add a B2B journey tool such as Dreamdata or HockeyStack when you need account-level attribution across marketing, sales and product touches. Move to a BI layer such as Looker, Power BI or Metabase on a warehouse when you must join billing, support and product data that no single application holds.

Our editorial picks, based on the product information reviewed:

  • Account-based B2B revenue attribution: Dreamdata.
  • Marketing, sales and product touches in one journey view: HockeyStack.
  • HubSpot-centred teams: HubSpot custom reporting.
  • Salesforce-centred teams: Salesforce reports, dashboards and analytics products.
  • Pipeline inspection and forecast analytics for sales leadership: Clari.
  • Governed metric definitions on a warehouse: Looker.
  • Microsoft-standard organizations: Power BI.
  • Lean teams wanting open-source BI on a warehouse: Metabase.

Why do marketing, sales and CS numbers never match?

Because each team reports from a different system with a different definition of the same word: marketing counts leads in the automation tool, sales counts opportunities in the CRM, and customer success counts renewals in billing or a CS platform. RevOps analytics is the work of reconciling those definitions and the joins between them, and software only automates the reconciliation once it's agreed.

The joins are the hard part. Contacts must map to accounts, accounts to opportunities, opportunities to subscriptions and subscriptions to product usage. If those keys are unreliable, every dashboard downstream inherits the error. Our guide to designing a revenue operations data model covers the entities and fields to fix first, and CRM data quality and deduplication tools help keep the keys clean.

What does each tool do well, and where does it fall short?

Order runs from purpose-built B2B revenue analytics to general BI. A general BI tool is more flexible but makes you build the funnel logic that the specialists ship with.

Dreamdata: best for account-based revenue attribution

Dreamdata connects CRM, marketing and ad data into account-level customer journeys, with attribution models and revenue reporting for B2B teams.

Pricing model: check the official pricing page for current tiers and any free option.

Strengths: Ships the B2B joins, contact to account to deal, so you don't build them yourself.

Limitations: You work inside its data model; check how custom stages and definitions are handled before committing.

Skip it if: you need to model billing, support and product data that it doesn't ingest.

HockeyStack: best for one journey view across marketing, sales and product

HockeyStack combines website, campaign, CRM and product signals into journey and attribution reporting for B2B go-to-market teams.

Pricing model: check the vendor's site for current packaging.

Strengths: Useful when product usage is part of the buying journey, as in product-led sales.

Limitations: Custom funnel stages and definitions need deliberate setup to match your dictionary.

Skip it if: your funnel is simple and your CRM reports already answer the question.

HubSpot custom reporting: best for HubSpot-centred teams

HubSpot reporting includes dashboards, custom report builders and attribution reporting across marketing and sales objects; how much is available depends on your subscription.

Pricing model: not confirmed for this draft; check the current plan comparison for which tier includes custom reporting and attribution.

Strengths: Lowest effort when marketing, sales and service already run in HubSpot.

Limitations: Joining external billing or product data is limited compared with a warehouse approach.

Skip it if: most of your revenue data lives outside HubSpot.

Salesforce reports, dashboards and analytics: best for Salesforce-centred teams

Salesforce offers native reports and dashboards plus analytics products for deeper exploration, built on CRM objects.

Pricing model: not confirmed for this draft; confirm current product names, packaging and which analytics need separate licences with Salesforce.

Strengths: Definitions live next to the CRM objects, which helps governance if admins own them.

Limitations: Advanced analytics need admin and modeling skill; small teams often under-use what they buy.

Skip it if: you don't have a Salesforce admin.

Clari: best for pipeline inspection and forecast analytics

Clari focuses on revenue operations for sales leadership: pipeline inspection, forecasting and deal-risk signals from CRM and activity data.

Pricing model: not confirmed for this draft; check the vendor's site.

Strengths: Strong on the sales half of the funnel and on forecast discipline.

Limitations: Not a marketing attribution tool; you'll need another source for pre-pipeline stages.

Skip it if: your main question is which campaigns create pipeline. Compare sales forecasting software if forecasting is the core need.

Looker: best for governed metric definitions on a warehouse

Looker, part of Google Cloud, uses a modeling layer so metrics such as pipeline or net revenue retention are defined once and reused across dashboards.

Pricing model: not confirmed for this draft; check Google Cloud's official pages for current editions.

Strengths: The best fit here for enforcing a single metric dictionary in code.

Limitations: Requires a warehouse and someone who owns the model.

Skip it if: you have no warehouse or no analyst time. See data warehouse platforms for small teams.

Power BI: best for Microsoft-standard organizations

Power BI is Microsoft's BI platform, with connectors to CRMs and warehouses and shared semantic models.

Pricing model: not confirmed for this draft; check Microsoft's current pricing page.

Strengths: A natural fit when your organization already standardizes on Microsoft tools.

Limitations: Funnel logic and definitions must be built and maintained by your team.

Skip it if: nobody on the team will own the semantic model.

Metabase: best for lean teams wanting open-source BI

Metabase is an open-source BI tool with a hosted cloud option, letting non-analysts ask questions of warehouse data.

Pricing model: not confirmed for this draft; check the official pricing page for self-hosted and cloud options.

Strengths: Low barrier to entry for a team with a warehouse and limited budget.

Limitations: Lighter governance and semantic-modeling features than Looker.

Skip it if: you need strict, centrally enforced metric definitions.

Will a new analytics tool end the arguments about numbers?

No tool resolves a definitional dispute on its own; it only makes the disagreement more visible. What vendors can't show you is how much of your reconciliation problem is data quality versus politics, and that ratio decides whether software is the right purchase.

How do you build a metric dictionary workflow?

Write each shared metric down once with an owner, a formula, a source system and a review date, and only publish a dashboard after its metrics are in the dictionary. Keep it in a place all three teams can read, and change it through a light approval step.

  1. List the contested metrics: usually MQL or qualified account, sales-accepted lead, opportunity created, pipeline, win rate, sales cycle, net revenue retention and churn.
  2. Assign one owner per metric and one approver from each affected team.
  3. Define each one precisely: formula, inclusion and exclusion rules, the date that counts (created, converted or closed), currency handling and source system.
  4. Record the join keys each metric relies on, such as account ID or opportunity ID, and the system of record for each.
  5. Implement once in the tool's modeling layer or saved reports, and label dashboards with the dictionary version.
  6. Review quarterly and log changes, so historical comparisons stay honest.

Source attribution belongs in the dictionary too: state which model you use and why. Our guide to marketing attribution without false certainty explains the trade-offs.

What should you check on governance, exports and alerts?

Check who can change a definition, whether changes are versioned, and whether data can leave the tool in a usable form. Alerts are only worth having if they point to an owner and an action.

  • Governance: role-based edit rights on metrics and dashboards, certified or verified dashboards, and change history.
  • Exports: scheduled exports or warehouse sync, so you aren't locked into one vendor's view.
  • Alerts: threshold or anomaly alerts on stage conversion and pipeline coverage, routed to a named owner.
  • Stage conversion: whether the tool reports cohort-based conversion (by created date) as well as period snapshots, because they answer different questions.

What mistakes do cross-functional teams make?

The most expensive mistake is buying a dashboard tool to settle a disagreement that is really about definitions or ownership. The second is building dozens of dashboards before agreeing the few metrics leadership will actually run the business on.

Also watch for mixing snapshot and cohort conversion rates in the same review, attributing revenue to campaigns without stating the model, and letting each team keep a private copy of the funnel in a spreadsheet. What else do buyers ask about revenue operations analytics?

Questions usually concern tool type, data readiness and ownership. Short answers follow.

Do we need a data warehouse for RevOps analytics?

Not at first. If most of your data is in one CRM, native reporting or a B2B journey tool can work; a warehouse becomes worthwhile when you must join billing, support and product data.

Who should own the metric dictionary?

Usually revenue operations, with an approver from marketing, sales and customer success for each shared metric. One owner per metric matters more than which team holds the document.

Is attribution the same as RevOps analytics?

No. Attribution assigns credit for revenue to touches or channels, while RevOps analytics covers the whole funnel, including conversion, velocity, forecasting and retention.

How often should funnel definitions change?

Rarely, and only through a logged review, typically once a quarter at most. Frequent changes break historical comparisons and erode trust in the numbers.

Can a small team run this without an analyst?

Yes, if it relies on CRM-native reporting or a purpose-built B2B analytics tool and keeps the metric list short. General BI tools usually need someone with modeling skills.

What's the verdict?

Write the metric dictionary first, then choose the tool type that matches where your data lives: CRM-native reporting for single-system teams, Dreamdata or HockeyStack for account-level B2B journeys, Clari for sales-side forecast analytics, and Looker, Power BI or Metabase once a warehouse holds the joins. No tool fixes definitions nobody owns.
