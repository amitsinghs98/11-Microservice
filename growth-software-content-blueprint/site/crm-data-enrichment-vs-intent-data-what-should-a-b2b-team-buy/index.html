CRM Data Enrichment vs Intent Data: What Should a B2B Team Buy?

The short answer on CRM enrichment vs intent data: enrichment completes the records you already have, while intent data tells you which accounts might be in market now. Most small B2B teams should buy enrichment first, because intent signals are hard to act on when the account and contact records underneath them are incomplete.

Which should a B2B team buy first?

Buy enrichment first if reps waste time researching companies, routing breaks on missing fields, or segmentation relies on guesswork. Add intent data only when your records are reliable, your target account list is larger than your team can work, and someone owns turning signals into a weekly action.

Our verdict by buyer type, based on the product information reviewed:

  • Founder-led or first sales hire: enrichment only, ideally inside the CRM you already use.
  • Outbound team with clean data and a large total addressable market: enrichment plus one intent source to order the list.
  • ABM programme with marketing and sales aligned on a target list: both, activated through ads, alerts and sequences.
  • Inbound-heavy team: enrichment plus first-party website signals before any third-party intent.

What job does each capability do?

Enrichment answers "who is this and what is the company like?" by filling fields such as industry, employee count, location, job title and technology used. Intent data answers "who might be looking right now?" by observing research activity and attributing it to accounts.

The two are often sold by the same vendors and shown on the same record, which blurs the distinction. Keep it sharp internally: enrichment is a data-completeness purchase judged on match rate and accuracy, while intent is a prioritization purchase judged on whether it changes which accounts you work and what results follow.

What fields and signals do you actually get?

Enrichment gives firmographic, contact and technographic fields that you can see and check on each record. Intent data gives topic-level research signals, surges or buying-stage estimates that you usually can't check one by one.

Typical enrichment fields: company domain, industry, size band, headquarters, revenue band where available, job title, seniority, department, verified email or phone, and technologies detected. Providers that work this way include ZoomInfo, Apollo, Cognism, the waterfall-style Clay, and HubSpot's Breeze Intelligence inside HubSpot.

Typical intent signals: topic surges from publisher networks such as Bombora, software-evaluation activity from G2 Buyer Intent, and blended predictive scores from platforms such as 6sense and Demandbase. First-party signals, such as companies identified on your own website, sit in between; see website visitor identification tools.

Several vendors sell both, so the label on the contract matters less than which job you are paying for. Ask each vendor to show which fields or signals you'll receive for a sample of accounts you already know.

How quickly does each go stale, and how much can you trust it?

Enrichment decays slowly but steadily as people change jobs and companies grow, so refresh it on a schedule and on key events such as a new opportunity. Intent loses relevance much faster, and its confidence depends on how activity was matched to an account.

Enrichment confidence is testable: sample a few dozen records, check titles and company details against public sources, and compute your own accuracy rate. Intent confidence is harder: ask the vendor how signals are attributed, how long a surge stays active, and whether a confidence level is exposed. Treat intent scores as bands rather than precise numbers.

Is intent data worth paying for if enrichment already works?

Sometimes, but the honest answer depends on whether your team will change what it does on a Monday morning because of a signal. No vendor page can show that for your market, so the decision should rest on a holdout pilot against your own pipeline, not on a demo dashboard.

What does each really cost?

Enrichment cost grows with how many records you enrich and how often you refresh them, so model it on volume. Intent cost is best judged per account your team can actually act on, because a signal nobody works is pure overhead.

Vendor pricing pages couldn't be fetched for this draft, so we give no figures. Model your own cost with stated assumptions (calculation from stated assumptions, hypothetical): multiply new records per month by the units your provider charges for each enrichment, add a quarterly refresh of active accounts, and compare that to the total intent cost divided by the number of accounts your team can realistically work each quarter. If the intent cost per actionable account is high and your reps are already at capacity, intent won't pay back yet.

Hidden costs apply to both: CRM storage, an integration or orchestration tool, and the time someone spends maintaining field mappings and signal routing. For the integration side, see marketing data integration platforms for lean teams.

What privacy questions should you ask?

Enrichment involves personal data on named people, so ask about the lawful basis, opt-out handling and data sources, especially for EU and UK contacts. Intent data is mostly behavioural and often account-level, but ask how it is collected, whether consent is involved and whether it is ever resolved to individuals.

Review each vendor's privacy and data-processing documentation with your legal adviser before signing. Also decide internally which fields reps may see and which stay in marketing systems only.

When is one enough, and when are both justified?

One is enough when your bottleneck is clearly on one side: enrichment when records are incomplete, intent only in the rare case where records are clean and the target list far exceeds capacity. Both are justified when you run account-based programmes that need complete records to act on and timely signals to decide where to act.

  • Enrichment alone works for: inbound routing, territory assignment, segmentation, personalization and lead scoring inputs.
  • Intent adds value for: ordering a large outbound list, timing ad spend on target accounts, and alerting account owners to renewed activity.
  • Both together: intent picks the account, enrichment supplies the right contacts and context, and the CRM records what happened.

If you'll run both, compare orchestration-style tools carefully; our Clay vs Apollo comparison covers two common enrichment workflows.

How hard is it to add or change providers later?

Changing enrichment providers is mostly a field-mapping and overwrite-rules exercise, and the main risk is the new provider overwriting good data with worse data. Changing intent providers resets your history, so trend comparisons break and reps lose familiarity with signal meaning.

Before signing either, store the provider name and date on enriched fields, keep raw intent signals separate from your own scores, and check contract length and renewal terms. That keeps a later switch manageable.

What else do buyers ask about enrichment and intent data?

Most questions are about overlap, sequencing and measurement. Short answers follow.

Can one vendor provide both enrichment and intent data?

Yes, several vendors sell both, but buying them together doesn't change the fact that they do different jobs. Evaluate enrichment on accuracy and intent on whether it changes outcomes in a pilot.

Is website visitor identification intent data?

It is first-party intent: it shows companies visiting your own site. It is usually more explainable than third-party intent but only covers accounts that already know you.

How often should we re-enrich CRM records?

Refresh active accounts and open opportunities on a regular schedule, such as quarterly, and enrich on creation for new records. Avoid blanket overwrites that replace data reps have corrected by hand.

How do we measure whether intent data works?

Run a holdout test: act on signals for one matched group of target accounts and not for another, then compare meetings or opportunities after a fixed window agreed in advance.

Does a small team need intent data at all?

Usually not at first. A small team often has more known accounts than it can work, so enrichment and first-party signals give more value per dollar until capacity grows.

What's the verdict?

Buy enrichment first, because it fixes the records every other workflow depends on and its quality is easy to check. Add intent data once records are reliable, your target list exceeds your capacity, and a named owner will turn signals into actions you can measure against a holdout.
