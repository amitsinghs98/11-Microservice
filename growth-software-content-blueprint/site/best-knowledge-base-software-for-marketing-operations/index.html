Best Knowledge Base Software for Marketing Operations

The right knowledge base software for marketing teams holds playbooks, campaign processes, brand guidance, research and SOPs in one searchable place, with owners and review dates so pages don't quietly go stale. Notion and Confluence suit most teams as a general wiki, Guru and Tettra suit teams that need verified answers, and Slab, Coda and SharePoint fill narrower roles.

Which knowledge base should a marketing team choose?

Choose by where your team already works and by how much you need to prove a page is current. A team living in Jira picks differently from a team living in Slack or Microsoft 365, and a team whose sales reps quote positioning needs verification more than layout.

  • Flexible all-in-one wiki for a small or mid-sized team: Notion.
  • Marketing alongside engineering or product teams already on Atlassian: Confluence.
  • Verified answers that sales and support pull into their daily tools: Guru.
  • Lightweight, Slack-centered Q&A: Tettra.
  • A clean, readable wiki: Slab.
  • Playbooks that double as working tools (tables, trackers, calculators): Coda.
  • Organizations standardised on Microsoft 365 with strict IT controls: SharePoint.

What does a marketing operations knowledge base actually need to hold?

It needs to hold the decisions and processes your team repeats: how a campaign gets launched, what the brand allows, which research is current and who owns each answer. A customer help center answers buyers' questions; a marketing operations knowledge base answers your own team's questions.

That boundary matters when you shortlist. Tools built for public self-service focus on article SEO, ticket deflection and widgets, which you can read about in our guide to customer knowledge base and self-service portal software. Internal tools care more about permissions, ownership, page history and how fast someone can find the current UTM convention mid-launch.

A useful marketing operations architecture has five top-level spaces:

  1. Brand and messaging: voice, positioning, approved claims, boilerplates and links to your asset library.
  2. Campaign playbooks: launch checklists, channel playbooks, naming conventions and post-campaign review templates.
  3. Processes and SOPs: intake, briefs, approvals, publishing and reporting, each with one named owner.
  4. Research: personas, interview notes, win/loss summaries and competitor notes, each with a "valid until" date.
  5. Tools and access: which tool does what, who administers it and how to request access.

Assets themselves belong in a DAM rather than the wiki; see our digital asset management roundup if brand files are scattered.

What does each knowledge base do well, and where does it fall short?

Notion and Confluence are the safest defaults; Guru and Tettra win when trust in the answer matters more than page design. The others solve specific problems well and are worth choosing only if that problem is yours.

Notion: best for flexible all-in-one team wikis

Notion is positioned as a workspace of pages and databases, so a campaign playbook could sit next to a tracker of launches by owner and status. Confirm which templates, page-history and permission controls come with the plan you'd buy. Limitation: the flexibility that makes it easy to start makes it easy to sprawl, so you'll need a space owner and enforced templates. Skip it if IT requires deep retention and compliance controls you can't confirm on your plan. See Notion's official site.

Confluence: best when marketing shares a stack with product and engineering

Confluence is Atlassian's team wiki, positioned for teams that also plan work in Jira. For marketing teams that ship launches with product, linking a launch plan to the tickets behind it is the likely benefit; confirm how spaces, templates, page history and permissions work on your plan. Limitation: the interface and permission model can feel heavy for a five-person team. Skip it if nobody else in your company uses Atlassian. See Confluence's official page.

Guru: best for verified answers in sales and support workflows

Guru is positioned around verified knowledge, aimed at keeping answers current for teams such as sales and support. That suits positioning, pricing talk tracks and competitor answers that marketing owns but sales uses; confirm in a trial how verification and review reminders work and which tools Guru can surface answers in. Limitation: an answer-focused format may fit short answers better than long, multi-step SOPs, so test one of yours. Skip it if the knowledge base is only for the marketing team itself. See Guru's official site.

Tettra: best for lightweight, Slack-centered knowledge

Tettra is positioned as a lightweight internal knowledge base and Q&A tool for teams that work in Slack. Confirm how page ownership, review reminders and the Slack connection work before you commit. Limitation: it's deliberately simple, so complex databases and interactive playbooks may need to live elsewhere. Skip it if your team doesn't use Slack. See Tettra's official site.

Slab: best for a clean, readable wiki

Slab is positioned as a clean, readable team wiki; check on its site whether its search reaches into the other tools you use. It's a good fit to evaluate if your main problem is that nobody reads the current wiki. Limitation: expect fewer built-in workflow and database features than all-in-one tools, and confirm that against your needs. Skip it if you want task tracking and documentation in one tool. See Slab's official site.

Coda: best for playbooks that are also tools

Coda is positioned as a doc that can hold tables and interactive elements alongside text, so a campaign playbook could include a live checklist or a budget table. Limitation: heavily built docs can be hard for occasional readers to navigate, and you should confirm on Coda's official pricing page how billable users are counted before you budget. Skip it if you want a plain reference wiki. See Coda's official site.

SharePoint: best for Microsoft 365 organizations

SharePoint is Microsoft's site and content platform within Microsoft 365, so it can draw on the permissions and compliance setup your IT team may already manage; confirm with IT which controls apply to your sites. Limitation: authoring and navigation feel less fluid than purpose-built wikis, and marketing rarely controls the configuration. Skip it if you're a small team without IT support. See Microsoft's SharePoint page.

Which features separate a usable marketing knowledge base from a dumping ground?

Ownership, review dates and findability separate them; editor polish barely matters. A page without an owner and a "last reviewed" date will be wrong within a quarter, whatever tool it's in.

  • Page structure: can you enforce templates for SOPs, playbooks and research summaries?
  • Search: does it rank recent, verified pages above old drafts, and can it search attachments?
  • Permissions: can agencies, freelancers or sales see only what they need?
  • Approvals and verification: can a page be marked as reviewed by an owner, with a reminder to re-check?
  • Version history: can you see who changed the approved claims page and restore the old version?
  • Integrations: does it connect to chat, your project tool and your editorial calendar?
  • Exports: can you export everything in a usable format if you switch tools?

Will the team actually use a new knowledge base?

Adoption depends on habits more than features: a knowledge base gets used when people are sent to it for answers and when owners keep it current. The vendor documentation reviewed describes the features, but none of it can show whether your team will change its behavior.

Which knowledge base fits your team's situation?

Match the tool to the team shape and stack you have now, not the one you plan to have. Migrating a wiki is tedious, so the cheapest mistake is the one you avoid at the start.

  • Three to ten marketers, no IT team: Notion or Slab, with a single owner for structure.
  • Marketing inside a product company on Jira: Confluence, so launch docs link to engineering work.
  • Product marketing feeding a sales team: Guru for verified talk tracks, plus a wiki for internal SOPs.
  • Agency documenting delivery processes across clients: start with our guide to SOP and process documentation software for agencies.
  • Enterprise with Microsoft 365 and retention policies: SharePoint, with marketing-owned templates on top.

What mistakes do marketing teams make with knowledge bases?

The common mistakes are importing everything, assigning no owners and treating search as optional. Each one turns a knowledge base into another place people check before asking a colleague anyway.

  • Bulk-importing old drives. Migrate only pages someone is willing to own; archive the rest.
  • No review cadence. Give research pages a "valid until" date and brand pages a quarterly review.
  • Mixing internal and public content. Keep customer help articles in a help center with its own workflow.
  • Over-permissioning freelancers. Share a dedicated space instead of the whole wiki.
  • Ignoring exports. Confirm before you commit that you can get your pages out in a standard format.

What else do buyers ask about knowledge base software for marketing?

Most questions are about overlap with tools you already own and about keeping pages current. The short answers are below; confirm plan-specific details on each vendor's official pages.

Can we use our project management tool as a knowledge base?

You can for a small team, but project tools are built around tasks that close, while knowledge pages need owners and review dates that persist. Most teams end up linking tasks to a separate wiki.

Should the brand guidelines live in the knowledge base or the DAM?

Keep the written rules and approved claims in the knowledge base and the files themselves in the DAM or brand portal, with links both ways. That way one source holds the rule and one holds the asset.

How often should marketing knowledge pages be reviewed?

A workable default is quarterly for brand and messaging pages, after every campaign for playbooks, and at a set expiry date for research. Tools with verification reminders make this easier to enforce.

Is AI search in knowledge bases reliable?

AI answers are only as good as the pages underneath, so stale or conflicting pages produce confident wrong answers. Check whether the tool cites the source page and respects permissions before you rely on it.

What's the verdict?

Start with Notion for a flexible marketing wiki or Confluence if you share Atlassian with product teams, and add Guru or Tettra where answers must be verified. Choose Slab, Coda or SharePoint only when readability, interactive playbooks or Microsoft 365 governance is the deciding need.
