Which CDN and web security platform should a marketing team choose?

Choose Cloudflare if you want CDN, DNS, TLS and a WAF in one dashboard a marketer can learn, or Amazon CloudFront's flat-rate plans if your site already runs on AWS. Pick Fastly or Akamai only when engineers will own the edge configuration, Bunny.net when cheap static and media delivery is the main job, and Sucuri when the real worry is a hacked WordPress site.

  • One dashboard for a small team: Cloudflare.
  • Sites already hosted on AWS: Amazon CloudFront with its flat-rate plans.
  • Developer-owned, programmable edge: Fastly.
  • Large, regulated or global estates with procurement: Akamai.
  • Low-cost asset and video delivery: Bunny.net.
  • WordPress sites that need malware cleanup alongside a firewall: Sucuri.

None of these tools replaces good hosting, patching or access control. They reduce load and filter bad traffic in front of your origin, which is valuable, but a misconfigured rule can also break forms, tracking or crawling, so whoever owns the account needs time to learn it.

What does a CDN and edge security layer actually do for a marketing site?

A CDN caches your pages and assets on servers close to visitors, so pages load faster and your origin server handles less traffic. The security layer sits in the same place and filters requests before they reach your site, using a web application firewall (WAF), DDoS mitigation, bot controls and rate limits.

For a marketing team, the practical jobs are these:

  • Global delivery and cache rules: decide which pages are cached, for how long, and how to purge them when a campaign page changes.
  • TLS and DNS: issue certificates and manage the records behind your domain, subdomains and landing pages.
  • WAF and DDoS protection: block common exploit patterns and absorb traffic floods.
  • Bot management: separate search crawlers and real buyers from scrapers, credential stuffers and form spam.
  • Logs: show what was blocked and why, which matters when a lead says your form wouldn't submit.

The technical complexity is real. Edge platforms were built for engineers, and the most powerful features (custom rules, edge code, log streaming) assume someone who reads HTTP headers comfortably. If nobody on your team does, weight ease of use and support heavily.

What is each platform best for, and when should you skip it?

Cloudflare is the easiest all-in-one starting point, and CloudFront is the natural choice inside AWS. The other four are better treated as specialist picks for a specific team or problem.

Cloudflare: best for small teams that want one dashboard

Cloudflare combines CDN, DNS, TLS, WAF and bot features in a single account. Its pricing page lists a free plan alongside paid Pro and Business plans and custom-priced Enterprise, and its CDN pricing table sets out what each tier costs and includes; check current monthly and annual prices there before you budget.

Limitations: the WAF managed rules documentation explains which managed rulesets are available on which plan, so confirm the free plan covers what you need. The breadth of the product also means there are many settings that can interfere with caching, analytics scripts or forms.

Skip it if your hosting provider already runs its own edge layer that you can't proxy through another CDN, or you need contractual support terms that only an enterprise agreement provides.

Amazon CloudFront: best for sites already hosted on AWS

CloudFront is AWS's CDN, and AWS now sells it in bundles. Its announcement of flat-rate plans describes tiered bundles that combine CDN delivery with security features, as an alternative to pay-as-you-go billing. Check each tier's allowances in the flat-rate plan documentation before you choose.

Limitations: setup lives in the AWS console, which assumes familiarity with origins, behaviours, certificates and IAM. Confirm in AWS's documentation that the flat-rate plans are available for your account.

Skip it if nobody on the team works in AWS already. Learning the console just for a CDN is rarely worth it for a marketing site.

Fastly: best for engineering-owned, programmable delivery

Fastly is an edge cloud platform with CDN, security and compute products. Its pricing page sets out usage-based and packaged options. Fastly is strongest where developers want fine-grained cache logic, instant purging and edge code.

Limitations: the product assumes engineering ownership, and its pricing is worth modelling carefully against your actual traffic.

Skip it if the people changing cache rules will be marketers rather than developers.

Akamai: best for large, global or regulated estates

Akamai sells delivery and security to large organisations through a sales-led process. Its blog on web application security costs discusses how pricing is structured, but there's no self-serve price list, so expect a quote.

Limitations: procurement and onboarding take longer, and you'll usually work through an account team or partner.

Skip it if you need to go live this month or your budget is a credit card, not a contract.

Bunny.net: best for low-cost static and media delivery

Bunny.net is a usage-priced CDN with storage, video and DNS products. Its CDN pricing page lists usage-based rates that vary by region; check the current per-GB figures there.

Limitations: in our editorial assessment it's positioned more for asset and media acceleration than full edge protection, so confirm its current security features before relying on it.

Skip it if bot management and a managed WAF are your main reasons for buying.

Sucuri: best for WordPress sites that need cleanup as well as a firewall

Sucuri combines a cloud WAF and CDN with malware scanning and removal. Its signup page lists annual plans for a single site, and it also has a website firewall page; check which features each plan includes.

Limitations: it isn't a general DNS or edge compute platform, so confirm the current price and inclusions at checkout.

Skip it if your site runs on a managed builder where you don't control the server or plugins.

What goes wrong when marketing teams run edge security themselves?

Based on the documentation reviewed, the common failure isn't a missed attack but an over-eager setting that blocks real visitors, breaks a form or slows search crawlers. Where a platform offers a log-only or simulate mode, the safer pattern is to log first and enforce later.

Which platform fits a startup, an agency or an in-house team?

A startup on a hosted builder often needs nothing beyond the builder's own CDN until it controls its DNS and wants a WAF. An agency managing many client sites benefits from one platform it can standardise, and an in-house team should match the platform to whoever already owns hosting.

  • Startup on a hosted builder: start with the builder's delivery, then add Cloudflare in front if you need DNS control and custom rules.
  • Agency with many WordPress clients: standardise on one platform, keep each client as the account owner, and use delegated access. Sucuri fits if cleanup is part of your care plan.
  • In-house team on AWS: CloudFront keeps everything in one bill and one set of permissions.
  • Enterprise with a security team: Akamai or Fastly, owned by engineering, with marketing given request rights rather than admin rights.

What should you ask a vendor before you commit?

Ask how the platform handles your specific traffic, who can change rules, and what you'll see when something is blocked. The answers decide operational effort more than the feature list does.

  1. Which WAF rulesets and bot features are included in the plan we're buying, and which are add-ons?
  2. How are verified search engine crawlers and monitoring tools treated by bot rules?
  3. How long are request logs retained, and can we export them to our analytics or log tool?
  4. What are support response times on this plan during an attack?
  5. How do cache purges work for a single campaign page, and how quickly do they propagate?
  6. Can we restrict roles so a marketer can purge cache but not change DNS?
  7. What are the overage terms, and is there a spend cap?
  8. How do we leave: can we export DNS zones and rule configurations?

What else do marketing teams ask about CDNs and web security?

Most follow-up questions are about who owns the configuration and whether a website host already covers the basics. The short answers are below.

Does my website builder already include a CDN?

Most hosted builders serve pages through a CDN as part of hosting, so you may not need a separate one for delivery. You may still want a separate edge security layer if you need custom WAF rules, bot controls or DNS you manage yourself.

Is a free CDN plan safe for a business website?

A free plan can cover TLS and basic DDoS protection for a small marketing site. Check what the free tier excludes, such as full managed WAF rulesets, support response times and log access, before you rely on it for a revenue-critical site.

Can a WAF block legitimate leads or crawlers?

Yes. An aggressive rule or bot setting can challenge real visitors, form submissions or search engine crawlers, so test changes in log or simulate mode first and watch form completions and crawl stats after each change.

Who should own the CDN account?

The company should own the account, not an agency or freelancer, with at least two named admins. Agencies can be granted delegated access, but losing access to DNS and edge settings during an incident is a serious and avoidable risk.

What's the verdict on CDN and web security for marketing sites?

For most marketing teams, Cloudflare is the most practical default and CloudFront's flat-rate plans are the obvious choice inside AWS. Fastly and Akamai earn their place when engineers own the edge, while Bunny.net and Sucuri solve narrower problems well.
