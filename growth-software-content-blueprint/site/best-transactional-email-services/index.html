Which transactional email service should you choose?

Pick the service whose trade-off matches your team: Postmark if you want a focused transactional product and support, Amazon SES if you'll do more setup to pay less, and SendGrid or Mailgun if you need both transactional and marketing sending at volume. Resend and MailerSend are worth a look for developer experience and simpler onboarding.

  • Transactional-first focus and clear stream separation: Postmark.
  • Lowest infrastructure cost, already on AWS: Amazon SES.
  • One vendor for transactional and marketing email at scale: SendGrid or Mailgun.
  • Modern developer workflow with React-based templates: Resend.
  • Template editing by non-developers plus an API: MailerSend.
  • Small team that also wants marketing campaigns in the same account: Brevo.

What counts as transactional, and why keep it separate?

Transactional email is a message a user triggers and expects, such as a sign-up confirmation, password reset, invoice or usage alert. Keep it apart from promotional email, ideally on separate streams or subdomains, so a marketing campaign that draws complaints can't slow down your password resets.

The line blurs with onboarding tips and product announcements. A useful test: would the user be confused or locked out if the message didn't arrive? If yes, it's transactional. If it's persuasion, it's marketing, belongs in your lifecycle email platform and must honor unsubscribes. Don't slip promotional content into receipts, because that blurs the classification and can create compliance problems.

What does each service do well, and where does it fall short?

Specialists give you focus and support, infrastructure gives you price, and the combined platforms give you one bill. Each block below says who should skip it.

Postmark: best for transactional-first focus

Postmark, owned by ActiveCampaign, built its product around transactional email and separates transactional and broadcast mail into distinct message streams. It offers an API and SMTP, templates, searchable activity logs, webhooks and a public status page. See the Postmark pricing page for volume tiers and retention options.

Skip it if you send very high volumes of low-stakes notifications and price per message is the deciding factor.

Amazon SES: best for low-cost infrastructure

Amazon SES is AWS's sending service, with an API and SMTP, configuration sets for event publishing and an optional Virtual Deliverability Manager. New accounts start in a sandbox and must request production access. Pricing is pay-as-you-go; see the Amazon SES pricing page.

Skip it if nobody on the team will own bounce handling, suppression and monitoring; SES gives you the pieces, not the dashboard.

SendGrid: best for one vendor at scale

SendGrid, part of Twilio, offers an email API and SMTP relay alongside a separate marketing campaigns product. It provides event webhooks, templates, subuser management and dedicated IPs on higher tiers. See the SendGrid pricing page.

Skip it if you want a small vendor's hands-on support; check the support level included in your tier.

Mailgun: best for developers who live in the logs

Mailgun, owned by Sinch, offers API and SMTP sending, inbound routing, detailed logs and webhooks, plus email validation and inbox placement tools in its wider product family. See the Mailgun pricing page.

Skip it if you need long log retention on an entry plan; confirm retention by tier.

Resend: best for developer experience

Resend is a newer API-first service from the team behind React Email, with SDKs for common languages, webhooks and templates written as code. It suits teams that want email to live in their codebase. See the Resend pricing page.

Skip it if procurement needs a long operating history or extensive compliance documentation; ask for what you need before committing.

MailerSend: best when non-developers edit templates

MailerSend, from the company behind MailerLite, combines an API and SMTP with drag-and-drop and HTML template editors, inbound routing and analytics. It suits teams where support or marketing staff maintain transactional copy. See the MailerSend pricing page.

Skip it if you need a large partner ecosystem or enterprise contract terms.

Brevo: best for small teams wanting one account

Brevo (formerly Sendinblue) offers marketing campaigns and a transactional API and SMTP in one platform. It's convenient for a small team that doesn't want two vendors. See the Brevo pricing page.

Skip it if transactional reliability is critical enough to justify a specialist.

What will 100,000 and 1 million messages a month cost?

At 100,000 messages a month, most services land on an entry or mid volume tier, and the difference between vendors is usually smaller than the cost of an engineer's time to integrate. At 1 million, per-message rates, dedicated IPs and log retention start to dominate, and quote-based discounts become worth asking for.

We couldn't confirm current rate cards on 4 November 2026, so here's how to build the comparison yourself from each pricing page:

  • Base cost: the tier that covers your monthly volume, or for pay-per-message services, volume times the per-1,000 rate.
  • Overage: the rate beyond your tier, since password resets spike after incidents and launches.
  • Dedicated IP: at 1 million messages a month a dedicated IP becomes a real option; check its monthly price and whether warm-up is managed.
  • Retention and logs: longer message history is often a paid add-on, and support teams depend on it.
  • Extras: validation, inbox placement testing, extra users and higher support tiers.

Price both scenarios at today's volume and at three times it. A cheap per-message rate that requires a dedicated IP and extra engineering may cost more than a tier that includes them.

What should a developer integration checklist include?

Cover authentication, failure handling, event webhooks and observability before the first production send. Most transactional email problems trace back to a missing retry, an unhandled bounce or a template change nobody tested.

  1. Authenticate the sending subdomain with SPF, DKIM and DMARC, separate from your marketing subdomain.
  2. Use the API with idempotency or de-duplication so retries don't send twice.
  3. Queue sends and retry on timeouts with backoff, and alert on sustained failures.
  4. Consume bounce, complaint and delivery webhooks, and write hard bounces to your own suppression list.
  5. Store message IDs against user records so support can trace a "never got the email" ticket.
  6. Version templates and test them before release with an email testing tool.
  7. Separate staging from production, and never send to real users from staging.
  8. Document a fallback: a second provider or a manual process if the primary goes down.

Questions to put to each vendor: Where is your status page and incident history? How long are logs retained by default? What does support look like on our tier? Can we get a dedicated IP, and who manages warm-up? Which security and privacy certifications can you share, and is a data processing agreement available?

How much do vendor reliability claims tell you?

Less than they appear to, because uptime and delivery-speed figures are vendor-reported and measured in ways that may not match your traffic. A public incident history and your own monitoring tell you more than any headline number.

What else do SaaS teams ask about transactional email?

Most questions concern SMTP, shared versus dedicated IPs and splitting vendors. The short answers follow.

Should we use the API or SMTP?

Use the API where you can, because it gives clearer errors, metadata and idempotency, and keep SMTP for legacy systems or tools that only support it.

Do we need a dedicated IP for transactional email?

Not at low volume, where a well-managed shared pool is usually fine. Dedicated IPs make sense at steady high volume, when you can keep them warm.

Can one provider handle both marketing and transactional email?

Yes, if it separates streams or IP pools, but many SaaS teams use a specialist for transactional mail so a marketing problem can't affect sign-ins.

How do we measure transactional email performance?

Track delivery rate, bounce and complaint rates and time from trigger to delivery, and join message events to product data so you can see whether confirmations and resets complete.
