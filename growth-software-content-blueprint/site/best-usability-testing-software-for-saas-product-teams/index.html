Best Usability Testing Software for SaaS Product Teams

The right usability testing software depends on whether you're testing a prototype before build or a live product after release, and whether you need moderated or unmoderated sessions. Maze and Lyssna suit fast unmoderated prototype tests, UserTesting and Userlytics suit recorded think-aloud sessions with a recruited panel, and Lookback suits moderated sessions.

Which usability testing software should you choose?

For quick unmoderated tests on Figma prototypes, choose Maze or Lyssna; for recorded think-aloud sessions on live products with a recruited panel, choose UserTesting or Userlytics. Choose Lookback for moderated sessions you run yourself, and Optimal for information architecture work such as card sorting and tree testing.

  • Maze: a good fit for design teams testing prototypes often.
  • Lyssna: a good fit for small teams that want quick tests (first click, preference, five-second) plus a pay-per-use panel.
  • UserTesting: a good fit for larger organizations that want video think-aloud sessions and a managed panel.
  • Userlytics: a good fit for teams that want moderated and unmoderated testing with flexible plan and credit options.
  • Lookback: a good fit for researchers who moderate sessions live with observers.
  • Optimal: a good fit for navigation and labeling decisions before you redesign menus.

Who is this for, and what problem does usability testing solve?

This guide is for SaaS product, design and growth teams that need to find out why users struggle in onboarding or core workflows. Usability testing shows you the confusion behind a drop-off, which analytics alone can't explain.

Your product analytics may show that many trial users never finish setup, and session replay may show them stalling on one screen. A usability test puts a participant in front of that screen with a task and asks them to think aloud, so you hear that the label means something different to them than it does to you.

If your aim is broader discovery conversations rather than task-based testing, see our guide to user research interview platforms. For website and marketing-page testing with remote panels, see remote user testing platforms.

How do the platforms handle tasks, capture and analysis?

The main split is between metric-driven unmoderated tools and video-first platforms built around think-aloud sessions. Check each block's limitations against the scenario you actually need to test.

Maze: best for unmoderated prototype testing

Maze is built for unmoderated tests on design prototypes, including Figma prototypes, per the Maze website. Panel responses are bought with credits, as the vendor's panel credits article explains; check current credit costs and study limits there.

Limitations: best on prototypes; long live-product sessions fit other tools better. Skip it if you need moderated think-aloud sessions.

Lyssna: best for quick tests on a small budget

Lyssna (formerly UsabilityHub) offers quick design tests such as first-click, five-second and preference tests, plus prototype tests and surveys, with panel responses paid per use, per its help center overview.

Limitations: plan limits couldn't be confirmed during drafting, so check them before you commit. Skip it if you need long, recorded sessions on a live SaaS app.

UserTesting: best for think-aloud video at scale

UserTesting records participants' screens and voices as they complete tasks, with a managed panel and highlight tools. Plan options are on its platform plans page; confirm them there, since they couldn't be fetched during drafting.

Limitations: it's built for research at volume, so costs are likely to rise with usage. Skip it if you run a few tests a quarter.

Userlytics: best for flexible moderated and unmoderated plans

Userlytics supports moderated, unmoderated and quantitative studies, with subscription plans, project-based purchasing and panel credits, per its pricing and plans page.

Limitations: pricing has several moving parts to model. Skip it if you want a simple self-serve subscription.

Lookback: best for moderated sessions

Lookback is built for live moderated sessions that colleagues can observe, and it supports unmoderated sessions too; check plans on the Lookback pricing page.

Limitations: it's focused on moderated sessions rather than quantitative metrics. Skip it if you need large unmoderated samples quickly.

Optimal: best for information architecture

Optimal (from Optimal Workshop) is positioned for information architecture research such as card sorting and tree testing. Feature and plan detail couldn't be checked on official pages during drafting; confirm on the Optimal website.

Limitations: not a workflow-testing tool. Skip it if your problem is a broken onboarding flow rather than navigation.

Do unmoderated tests miss problems a moderator would catch?

Often, yes: unmoderated tests are good at showing where people fail, but weaker at revealing why, because nobody can ask a follow-up. The documentation reviewed covers capture and metrics; it can't tell you how well a task is written.

How should you design tests for onboarding and core workflows?

Write tasks around goals a real user would have, test onboarding with people who've never seen the product, and test core workflows with people who match your active users. Record success, time, errors and the participant's own explanation.

Onboarding scenario. Recruit people who match your ideal customer but haven't used the product. Task: sign up and get to the first meaningful result, such as importing data or inviting a colleague. Watch for setup steps that need knowledge they don't have. Our guide to product tour and onboarding software covers fixes once you've found the friction.

Core workflow scenario. Recruit current users or close matches. Task: complete a frequent job end to end, for example building and sharing a report. Watch for workarounds, repeated backtracking and moments where they leave the product to check something.

For both, check that participants include people using keyboard navigation or assistive technology, export clips and notes so findings reach the backlog, and re-test after changes. Confirm impact afterwards with an A/B test where traffic allows.

Which platform fits your team and stage?

Match the tool to your stage and research maturity. Early-stage teams usually get more from cheap, frequent tests than from a large enterprise contract.

  • Designer-led startup testing Figma prototypes: Maze or Lyssna.
  • Product team testing a live app with hard-to-recruit users: UserTesting or Userlytics.
  • Researcher running weekly moderated sessions: Lookback.
  • Redesigning navigation: Optimal first, then a prototype test.

What mistakes do teams make with usability testing tools?

The biggest mistake is testing with the wrong participants, such as colleagues or friendly power users. The second is running a study and never turning findings into backlog items.

  • Writing leading tasks that use the interface's own labels.
  • Ignoring panel costs when comparing subscriptions.
  • Testing only prototypes and never the live product's real data states.
  • Failing to export or tag clips, so evidence disappears after the presentation.

What else do SaaS teams ask about usability testing tools?

These answers cover participant numbers, accessibility, prototypes versus live products and analytics. They reflect general research practice and documented features rather than tests we ran.

How many participants does a usability test need?

For finding qualitative problems in one flow, small rounds repeated after each fix usually teach more than one large round. For metrics such as success rate or time on task, you need larger samples, and unmoderated tools make that cheaper.

Should we test prototypes or the live product?

Test prototypes when changes are cheap and you want to compare directions, and test the live product to find problems real data and real edge cases expose. Most SaaS teams need both at different stages.

Can usability testing software check accessibility?

It can include participants who use assistive technology if you recruit them deliberately, but it isn't an accessibility audit. Pair sessions with a standards-based audit and automated checks.

Is session replay a substitute for usability testing?

No. Replay shows what users did at scale, but not what they were trying to do or why they hesitated. Use replay to find where to test, and usability sessions to understand the cause.

Who should watch usability sessions?

Designers, product managers and engineers who own the flow. Short highlight reels shared after each round help stakeholders who can't attend live.

What's the verdict on usability testing software?

Maze or Lyssna is the practical start for most SaaS teams, with UserTesting or Userlytics added when you need recorded think-aloud sessions from a panel at volume. Lookback and Optimal are specialist tools worth adding for moderated research and navigation work.
