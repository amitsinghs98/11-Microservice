How to Run a Monthly Paid Media Account Review for Clients

A monthly PPC account review should end with decisions, not screenshots of ROAS and CPC. Use it to check results against the client's objective, surface budget and tracking risk, explain what changed and agree the next experiments, with priorities that differ for lead generation and ecommerce accounts.

Why do screenshot-led monthly reviews fail?

Screenshots of ROAS and CPC show what the platform measured, not whether the client's business moved, and they invite the client to optimise for a metric. A review built on objectives, risk and decisions gives the client a reason to keep the retainer and the team a plan for next month.

The other problem is that a screenshot review hides the work. If the client never sees the search terms cut, the tracking fixed or the test retired, they judge the agency by one number that the agency only partly controls.

Which data should you pull before the meeting?

Pull platform data, the client's own records and your change log for the same calendar month, in the client's time zone and currency. Reconcile them before the call so the meeting isn't spent arguing about whose number is right.

  • Spend, clicks, conversions and conversion value by campaign, from each platform.
  • CRM or store outcomes for the same period, by paid source.
  • Search term and placement reports with spend and conversions.
  • Policy status: disapproved or limited ads and assets.
  • Call data, if phones matter; call tracking software fills a gap many lead gen reviews miss.
  • The previous month's decisions, to report on what was done.

A recurring check with a PPC audit tool speeds up items 3 and 4, but read its flags against client context before presenting them.

How should you keep the change log?

Record every material change with the date, account, what changed, why, the expected effect and the date you'll review it. The log turns "performance dropped" into "performance dropped after we narrowed match types on the 12th", which is a conversation you can have.

Keep it in a shared sheet or your management tool, one row per change. Include changes the client made, such as site launches, price changes and promotions, because they explain more variance than most bid changes do. Platform change history fills gaps, but it doesn't record intent.

How do you run the budget-risk review?

Compare spend to date against the monthly plan, project the month-end total at the current daily rate, and flag any campaign that's budget-limited while performing or overspending while weak. Agree the reallocation in the meeting, not by email afterwards.

Watch for automated bidding changes that raise daily spend, seasonal peaks and promotions the client hasn't told you about. If you manage many accounts, the budget pacing and spend forecasting tools can raise alerts between reviews.

What goes in the experiment register?

Each test gets a hypothesis, the metric that decides it, the start date, the planned end date and a result line. Close tests on schedule, including the ones that didn't work, so the client sees learning rather than activity.

A hypothetical example: "Moving the demo form to a two-step layout will raise the qualified-lead rate without lowering volume; decide on sales-accepted rate after four weeks." Keep no more than two or three active tests per account so each has enough data.

What do clients actually want from a monthly review?

Most want to know whether their goal is on track, what's at risk and what you'll do next, in that order. This page can't tell you how your clients react to a particular format, so treat the agenda as a starting point to adapt.

What should the client summary say?

Write a one-page summary with five parts: result against objective, the main reason, the main risk, what changed, and the decisions with owners and dates. Send it within a day of the meeting.

  1. Result: the business outcome versus target, stating which source it comes from.
  2. Why: one or two causes, linked to the change log.
  3. Risk: budget, tracking or quality issues to watch.
  4. Work done: the material changes, briefly.
  5. Next: experiments and decisions, each with an owner.

Platform ROAS and CPC can go in an appendix. They belong there because they explain the result rather than being the result.

How do lead gen and ecommerce reviews differ?

Lead gen reviews spend most time on lead quality and CRM handoff, because the platform can't see what happened after the form. Ecommerce reviews spend more time on revenue quality, margin, product feeds and promotions, because the platform sees the sale but not its profit.

In lead gen, bring sales feedback and stage conversion rates, and push to import qualified outcomes. In ecommerce, bring margin by category, new versus returning customers and stock constraints. Either way, set the review against the measurement plan agreed before launch.

What goes wrong with monthly account reviews?

Reviews drift into reporting history instead of making decisions, and they skip tracking health until a client spots the problem first. A fixed agenda, a reconciled data pack and recorded decisions prevent most of it.

Watch the cost too. Reviews that take a day to prepare for a small retainer erode margin, which our guide to agency profitability reporting tools helps you measure. Automating the data pull with your PPC management software keeps preparation time predictable.

What else do agencies ask about monthly PPC reviews?

These questions come up when agencies redesign their review process. The answers are short.

How long should a monthly PPC account review take?

Plan for 45 to 60 minutes with the client, plus preparation time that's predictable because the data pack is the same each month. Smaller accounts can run a shorter version of the same agenda.

Should the client see the search term report?

Show a summary of what was cut and why, not the full report. That proves the work without asking the client to read hundreds of rows.

What if platform conversions and the CRM disagree?

Show both, label which is the business record, and explain the known causes such as attribution windows, duplicates or offline steps. Unexplained gaps go on the tracking-health agenda item.

Is a monthly review enough for high-spend accounts?

Usually not on its own. Pair it with weekly pacing and tracking checks, and keep the monthly meeting for decisions and experiments.

What should you do next?

Copy the agenda table into your review template, start a change log and experiment register for each client this month, and send your first one-page summary after the next meeting.
