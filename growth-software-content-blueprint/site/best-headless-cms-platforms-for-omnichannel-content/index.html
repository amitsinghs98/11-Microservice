Best Headless CMS Platforms for Omnichannel Content

Headless CMS platforms earn their keep when one set of structured content has to power a website, an app and product screens at the same time; for a single marketing site they usually add cost and developer dependence. This guide compares seven options on the trade-off that decides most purchases: developer flexibility against marketer autonomy.

Which headless CMS should you choose?

Choose Storyblok or Prismic if marketers need a visual, page-building experience on top of headless delivery, Contentful or Contentstack if a large organisation needs governed content across many channels, and Sanity or Hygraph if developers want maximum control over the content model and queries. Strapi suits teams that want to self-host open-source software and own the infrastructure.

  • Visual editing for marketers: Storyblok.
  • Slice-based page building for marketing sites: Prismic.
  • Enterprise content reuse with established governance: Contentful.
  • Enterprise programs with complex workflows and many brands: Contentstack.
  • A highly customisable editing studio and flexible queries: Sanity.
  • GraphQL-first delivery and federating content from other sources: Hygraph.
  • Self-hosted, open-source control: Strapi.

When does structured content need a headless CMS?

You need headless when the same product descriptions, help articles, offers or legal copy must appear consistently in several places, such as a website, a mobile app, in-product messages and partner portals. If everything you publish lives on one website, a traditional or hosted CMS will usually be faster and cheaper to run.

The benefit comes from modelling content as reusable pieces (a product, a feature, a testimonial) rather than as pages. The cost is that someone has to build every front end that consumes it, and marketers lose the "what you see is what you get" editing they may be used to unless the platform and your developers restore it through preview and visual editing.

What does each headless CMS do well, and where does it fall short?

The blocks below are ordered from most marketer-friendly to most developer-centred within our criteria. Confirm product details on each linked vendor page; fit is our editorial assessment.

Storyblok: best for marketers who want to see what they're editing

Storyblok pairs a component-based content model with a visual editor that shows changes on a live preview of the page. Developers define the components; marketers assemble and edit them. Plans are on the Storyblok pricing page.

Strengths: on paper, the most marketer-oriented editing model in this group. Limitations: the visual editor only works as well as the front-end integration your developers build. Skip it if your content is mostly data for apps rather than pages.

Prismic: best for marketing sites built from reusable sections

Prismic organises pages into reusable sections, which developers code once and marketers combine into new landing pages. It's aimed at website teams more than at multi-app content programs. Plans are on the Prismic pricing page.

Strengths: quick landing-page creation within a design system. Limitations: less suited to complex, deeply related content models. Skip it if you need the same content in apps and product interfaces.

Contentful: best for governed reuse across many channels

Contentful is an API-first platform with structured content types, environments for safe changes, roles and an app framework for extensions. It's aimed at organisations reusing content across sites and apps. See the Contentful pricing page for tiers and usage limits.

Strengths: mature modelling, environments and ecosystem. Limitations: usage limits and locale or seat needs can push a growing team to higher tiers sooner than expected. Skip it if a single website is your only channel.

Contentstack: best for enterprise programs across brands and regions

Contentstack targets enterprise teams with workflows, publishing rules, localization and multi-brand setups. Start from the Contentstack pricing page.

Strengths: governance features for large, distributed teams. Limitations: scale and price are aimed at large organisations. Skip it if you're a mid-sized team with one brand.

Sanity: best for teams that want to shape the editing experience

Sanity stores content in a hosted content lake and lets developers configure the editing studio in code, with flexible querying for front ends. Real-time collaboration and custom inputs are part of its appeal. Plans are on the Sanity pricing page.

Strengths: very flexible models and a studio tailored to your team. Limitations: without developer time, editors get a generic experience. Skip it if nobody will own studio configuration.

Hygraph: best for GraphQL-first teams pulling content from many sources

Hygraph, formerly GraphCMS, is built around GraphQL and promotes content federation, bringing data from other systems into one API alongside managed content. Plans are on the Hygraph pricing page.

Strengths: a single query layer for content and external data. Limitations: best suited to teams comfortable with GraphQL. Skip it if your developers prefer REST and simple models.

Strapi: best for teams that want to self-host open source

Strapi is an open-source headless CMS you can host yourself, with a paid cloud option and enterprise features for teams that want them. Developers can customise the admin and APIs directly. Options are on the Strapi pricing page.

Strengths: ownership of code and data, no vendor lock-in on hosting. Limitations: you carry hosting, upgrades, backups and security. Skip it if you have no one to run infrastructure.

Can marketers really work without developers on a headless CMS?

Day-to-day editing can be largely developer-free once components, preview and workflows are built, but new page types, integrations and model changes still need developers. Vendor material shows what editors can do; it doesn't show how long the initial build takes or how often marketers hit its limits.

How do you decide with or without developers on the team?

If you have no developers and one website, don't go headless; choose a hosted or traditional CMS. If you have developers, the deciding questions are how many channels consume the content and how much visual control marketers need.

  1. Do you have, or will you fund, developers for the long term? No: choose a hosted CMS such as Webflow or HubSpot, or a managed WordPress setup. Yes: continue.
  2. Does content need to appear in more than one website, app or product surface? No: a traditional CMS is still likely simpler; headless is optional. Yes: continue.
  3. Do marketers need to build and arrange pages visually? Yes: shortlist Storyblok or Prismic. No: continue.
  4. Is this an enterprise program with many brands, regions or approval layers? Yes: shortlist Contentful or Contentstack. No: continue.
  5. Do developers want full control of hosting or the editing studio? Hosting: Strapi. Studio and queries: Sanity. GraphQL and federation: Hygraph.

What should you test during a trial?

Model one real content type end to end, connect preview, and have a marketer publish a change without help. That single exercise reveals more than any feature list.

  • Content modelling: can you represent references, reusable blocks and variants cleanly?
  • Preview: can editors see a change in context before publishing?
  • Roles and workflow: can you restrict who publishes, and require review?
  • Localization: are locales field-level or entry-level, and how do fallbacks work? Our guide to translation management software covers connecting translators.
  • Environments: can developers test model changes without touching live content?
  • Assets: is image transformation built in, or do you need a digital asset management platform?
  • Webhooks and APIs: do publish events trigger builds, search indexing and cache purges reliably?

What pushes headless CMS pricing up?

Headless pricing usually rises with seats, locales, environments, content records and API or bandwidth usage, and the enterprise features many organisations want, such as SSO and advanced workflows, can sit on higher tiers. Model your expected usage for each of these before comparing plans.

Add the costs outside the CMS: front-end hosting, developer time to build and maintain integrations, and any separate search or asset tools. For a single site, those extras are often what makes headless more expensive than a traditional CMS.

What else do teams ask about headless CMS platforms?

These answers cover SEO, migration and the difference between headless and composable. They reflect our editorial assessment, so check vendor documentation for specifics.

Is a headless CMS bad for SEO?

Not inherently, but SEO controls such as metadata, redirects, sitemaps and rendering move to your front end. If developers don't build them, marketers lose control they'd have in a traditional CMS.

What's the difference between headless and composable?

Headless describes a CMS that delivers content by API without its own front end. Composable describes a wider architecture where the CMS, commerce, search and other services are chosen separately and connected.

How hard is it to migrate into a headless CMS?

The hard part is remodelling page-based content into structured types, not the import itself. Budget time for mapping fields, cleaning content and rebuilding templates in the front end.

Do headless CMS platforms have free plans?

Availability varies by vendor, and open-source options such as Strapi can be self-hosted without a licence fee. Where a free tier exists, check its seat, locale and usage limits on the vendor's pricing page against your real needs.

What's the verdict?

Choose a headless CMS only when content genuinely has to serve several channels and you can fund developers; then pick Storyblok or Prismic for marketer autonomy, Contentful or Contentstack for enterprise governance, and Sanity, Hygraph or Strapi for developer control. If you have one site and no developers, a traditional CMS remains the better buy.
