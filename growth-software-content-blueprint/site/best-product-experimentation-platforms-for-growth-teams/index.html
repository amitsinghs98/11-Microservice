Which product experimentation platform should a growth team choose?

Choose a warehouse-native platform such as GrowthBook, Statsig or Eppo if your metrics live in a data warehouse and you want analysts to trust the numbers. Choose Amplitude, LaunchDarkly or PostHog if you'd rather run experiments inside the analytics or flag tool you already pay for.

  • High test volume with rigorous statistics: Statsig or Optimizely Feature Experimentation.
  • Warehouse-first data team: GrowthBook or Eppo.
  • Already on Amplitude Analytics: Amplitude Experiment.
  • Already on LaunchDarkly for releases: its Experimentation add-on.
  • Small engineering-led team: PostHog.

How are product experiments different from web A/B tests?

Web A/B tests change marketing pages through a browser script and usually measure a conversion goal like a signup. Product experiments change application logic through feature flags in code, randomise identified users or accounts, and measure outcomes such as activation, retention or expansion revenue.

That makes product experiments slower to set up but able to test things a visual editor can't touch: pricing logic, onboarding sequences, recommendation rules or a redesigned workflow. What is each platform best for, and when should you skip it?

The standalone engines suit teams that run many tests and care about method, while the bundled options suit teams that run a few tests a quarter. Each block names the fit and when to pass.

Statsig: best for high-volume product testing

Statsig combines flags, experiments and analytics with variance reduction and a warehouse-native option. Its pricing page starts teams on a free developer tier, with metered paid plans above it (checked 5 November 2026). Its ownership has changed, as its acquisition announcement explains. Skip it if you need a long, settled roadmap commitment before signing; ask who owns and supports the product today.

Optimizely Feature Experimentation: best for enterprise programmes

Optimizely's product-side offering supports targeted deliveries, A/B tests and feature rollouts, according to its feature rollouts documentation (checked 5 November 2026). Its plan options article describes custom-quoted pricing (checked 5 November 2026). Skip it if you're a small team that needs to start this week.

Eppo: best for warehouse-native teams already on Datadog

Eppo built a reputation for warehouse-native statistics, and Datadog announced its acquisition of Eppo in 2025 (checked 5 November 2026). Strengths: experiment results next to observability guardrails. Skip it if you don't use Datadog and want a vendor-neutral tool.

GrowthBook: best for open, warehouse-native testing

GrowthBook queries metrics in your warehouse, supports Bayesian and frequentist analysis, and documents sample ratio mismatch checks on experiment results (checked 5 November 2026). Its pricing page lists a free Starter plan and a per-seat Pro plan (checked 5 November 2026). Skip it if you have no warehouse or analyst to define metrics.

Amplitude Experiment: best for teams already analysing in Amplitude

Amplitude includes experimentation in the same platform as its analytics; check the Amplitude pricing page for which plans include it. Strengths: no separate event pipeline. Skip it if your source of truth is a warehouse rather than Amplitude.

LaunchDarkly Experimentation: best if LaunchDarkly already runs releases

LaunchDarkly adds experiments on top of its flags, and its billing documentation explains how usage is metered. Skip it if you don't need its release governance, because you'd be paying for both.

PostHog: best for small engineering-led teams

PostHog runs experiments on its own flags and analytics events, with usage-based pricing and a monthly free allowance (checked 5 November 2026). Skip it if you need formal review workflows and a central experiment repository.

Do growth teams get trustworthy results from these platforms in practice?

The documentation reviewed shows each platform can compute sound statistics, but results are only as good as assignment, event quality and the decision rules set before launch. Most bad calls come from peeking, underpowered tests and broken randomisation, not from the tool.

What should you decide before you launch a product experiment?

Write down the hypothesis, the primary metric, guardrail metrics, the unit of randomisation, the minimum effect you care about and the planned duration before the test starts. Locking these in stops you from moving the goalposts after seeing early numbers.

  • Unit of randomisation: randomise by account in B2B products, or teammates in one company may see different versions.
  • Sample size: run a power calculation from your baseline rate and minimum detectable effect. Low-traffic products often can't detect small lifts at all, so test bigger changes.
  • Peeking: don't stop a fixed-horizon test early on a good day. If you need to monitor continuously, use a sequential method your platform supports.
  • Guardrails: track latency, errors, support tickets or churn so a "winner" doesn't quietly hurt something else.
  • Sample ratio mismatch: if the split differs from what you set, fix the bug before trusting results.

Keep the plan and outcome in a shared log; our roundup of experiment tracking and repository platforms covers tools for that.

Which platform fits your experiment programme?

Match the platform to where your metrics live and how many tests you'll run. Here are the common cases.

  • Onboarding tests in a B2B product: any platform with account-level randomisation and retention metrics.
  • Pricing and packaging tests: a flag-based platform with server-side assignment, plus legal review of what each user is shown.
  • Feature release with measurement: combine flags and experiments, as covered in our feature flag management platforms guide.
  • Metrics defined in product analytics, not a warehouse: Amplitude, PostHog or Statsig, alongside your product analytics tool.

What else do growth teams ask about product experimentation?

These questions usually follow the shortlist. Answers are based on product information reviewed, not testing.

Do we need a data warehouse to run product experiments?

No. Several platforms compute results from their own event data. A warehouse-native tool is worth it when your trusted revenue and retention metrics already live in the warehouse.

Can we run pricing experiments safely?

Yes, with care. Assign users server-side, keep each user in one variant, and check legal and billing implications before showing different prices to similar customers.

What is a holdout group?

It's a small share of users kept out of new features or experiments for a period. Comparing them with everyone else shows the cumulative effect of many changes.

How long should a product experiment run?

Long enough to reach the sample size you planned and to cover at least one full weekly cycle. Ending early because results look good inflates false positives.

What's the verdict on product experimentation platforms?

GrowthBook and Eppo suit warehouse-first teams, Statsig and Optimizely suit high-volume or enterprise programmes, and Amplitude, LaunchDarkly and PostHog are sensible when you already use them. Check ownership changes and pricing meters carefully, because this category has consolidated quickly.
