Clay vs Apollo: Data Enrichment and Prospecting Workflows

Clay vs Apollo isn't really a like-for-like choice. Apollo is a contact database with sequences built in, while Clay is a workflow tool that chains many data sources, including databases like Apollo. Pick Apollo to start prospecting this week, and Clay when one source can't give you the fields you need.

Should you choose Clay or Apollo?

Choose Apollo if you want one tool to find contacts, reveal emails and send sequences with little setup. Choose Clay if you need custom research fields, several data sources chained together, or a team member who enjoys building workflows.

  • Founder or first SDR starting outbound: Apollo, because search, reveal and send live in one place.
  • Agency building lists for many clients: Clay, because each client's criteria can become a reusable table. See our sales prospecting tools for agencies for the wider field.
  • RevOps team fixing CRM coverage: Clay for enrichment, often with Apollo as one of its sources.
  • SDR team that mainly needs volume: Apollo, or a dedicated sender with a database feeding it.

How are Clay and Apollo positioned?

Apollo positions itself as an all-in-one sales platform: a large B2B contact database, enrichment, sequences and CRM sync. Clay positions itself as a data enrichment and workflow platform, a spreadsheet-like workspace where each column can call a data provider, an AI step or an integration.

The difference is where the data comes from. Apollo mainly sells its own database. Clay orchestrates many providers and lets you fall back from one to the next when a lookup comes up empty, which is usually called waterfall enrichment.

In our editorial assessment, Apollo is a product you use, and Clay is a product you build with.

How do Clay and Apollo compare at a glance?

Both publish self-serve, credit-based plans, but credits mean different things. In Apollo, a credit mostly pays for revealing data from its own database; in Clay, credits pay for each enrichment step, which can include calls to several providers.

How does the same prospecting workflow run in each?

In Apollo, the workflow is filter, save, reveal and enroll, which a rep can do in an afternoon. In Clay, the same workflow becomes a table of chained steps, which takes longer to build but can add sources and custom fields Apollo doesn't have.

Hypothetical brief: find the head of marketing at 300 Series A to Series B SaaS companies in the UK that use HubSpot, get a verified email, write a personalized first line, and push the contact to a sequence and the CRM.

In Apollo

  1. Filter companies by location, industry, headcount and funding, and filter people by title.
  2. Save the list and reveal emails, using credits for each contact.
  3. Check the technology filter for HubSpot, if your plan and coverage support it, or check it by hand.
  4. Write the first line yourself or with Apollo's AI assistance, then enroll contacts in a sequence.
  5. Sync contacts and activity to your CRM.

In Clay

  1. Import or source the 300 accounts, from Clay's own sources, a CSV or a connected database.
  2. Add a column to find the right person at each company.
  3. Add an email waterfall: try provider A, and if it returns nothing, try provider B, then C.
  4. Add verification, a technology lookup for HubSpot and an AI column that drafts the first line from public signals.
  5. Push the finished rows to your sequencer and CRM through an integration.

The Clay version produces richer rows, but every column is something you designed and must keep working.

How many credits does that workflow consume?

Apollo's credit use is roughly one reveal per contact, so it scales in a straight line with the list. Clay's credit use is the sum of every step on every row, so a four-step table can use several times the credits of a single lookup.

To show the shape, give every step an illustrative weight of one credit unit. These are teaching numbers, not either vendor's actual rates, which you'll find on the pricing pages linked above.

  • Apollo: 300 contacts multiplied by one reveal is 300 units, plus extra if you also reveal phone numbers.
  • Clay: per row, one unit to find the person, about 1.4 units for the email waterfall (assuming the first provider finds 60% and the second finds half of the rest), one for verification and one for the AI first line. That's about 4.4 units a row, or roughly 1,320 units for 300 rows.

The comparison that matters is cost per usable contact. If Clay's waterfall finds valid emails for far more of the 300 than a single database does, its higher per-row cost may still produce a lower cost per contact you can actually email.

How long do setup and maintenance take?

Apollo needs very little setup beyond connecting the CRM and mailboxes, so a rep can be sending the same week. Clay needs someone to design, test and maintain each table, and that upkeep continues as providers, fields and integrations change.

Budget Clay time as an ongoing operations task, not a one-off project. A table that breaks quietly, for example when a provider returns a new field format, can push bad data into the CRM for weeks before anyone notices.

How does each hand contacts to outbound and the CRM?

Apollo can send sequences itself and sync to the CRM, so the handoff happens inside one product. Clay mostly hands off to other tools, pushing enriched rows into a sequencer, a CRM or a webhook.

That makes Clay dependent on your sending tool. Our cold email software roundup covers the senders teams usually pair it with. Whichever route you take, decide which system owns the contact record, so enrichment from Clay doesn't overwrite fields your reps have corrected.

Is Clay's flexibility worth the build time?

It's worth it when you repeat the same research many times, such as for an agency's client lists or a recurring account-scoring job. For a one-off list, the build time often costs more than the extra coverage returns.

Where does each fall short?

Apollo's main limit is that you're relying on one database, whose coverage is uneven across regions and segments. Clay's main limit is the skill and time it needs, plus credit costs that grow with every step you add.

Apollo

  • One primary data source, so gaps in your niche stay gaps.
  • Credits can run out before the month does on high-volume plans.
  • Sequencing is solid for small teams but lighter than dedicated engagement platforms.

Clay

  • Steep learning curve for people who don't think in tables and logic.
  • Credit consumption is harder to forecast.
  • No native sending at the depth of a sequencer, so you'll need another tool.

When should you use Clay and Apollo together?

Use both when Apollo's database covers most of your market but not all of it. Apollo can be the first source in a Clay waterfall or the sending layer at the end of it, while Clay fills the gaps and adds custom fields.

A common split: reps search and send from Apollo, while an operations owner runs Clay tables that enrich CRM records and score accounts. Our lead enrichment software guide and B2B contact database comparison cover the other tools that fit either role.

What else do buyers ask about Clay vs Apollo?

Most questions are about whether one can replace the other and how credits work. Check both vendors' official pricing pages for current allowances.

Can Clay replace Apollo?

For enrichment and list building, often yes, because Clay can pull from many sources. For sending sequences, usually not on its own, so you'd pair it with a sender.

Can Apollo replace Clay?

For a simple find-reveal-send workflow, yes. For multi-source enrichment, custom research columns or complex scoring, Apollo isn't designed to do what Clay does.

Which is cheaper, Clay or Apollo?

Apollo is usually cheaper per contact for straightforward reveals. Clay can be cheaper per usable contact when its waterfall finds data that one database misses, so model both on the same list.

Do I need technical skills to use Clay?

You don't need to code, but you do need to think in tables, conditions and fallbacks. Plan for a learning period and give one person ownership of the tables.

What's the verdict?

Apollo is the faster, simpler choice for teams that want to prospect and send from one tool. Clay is the more powerful choice for teams that repeat complex research and have someone to own it, and the two often work best together.
