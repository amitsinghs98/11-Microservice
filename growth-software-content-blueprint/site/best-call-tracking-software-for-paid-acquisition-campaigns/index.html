Which call tracking tool should you choose?

Start with Google's own call conversion tracking if you only advertise on Google Ads and just need to count calls, then move to a dedicated platform once you need number pools across several channels, recordings, routing or CRM sync. Among dedicated tools, the deciding factors are call volume, whether you run a contact center, and whether you need to send call outcomes back to the ad platforms.

  • Local-service businesses and the agencies that run their ads: CallRail.
  • Agencies that want calls, forms and other leads in one lead log: WhatConverts.
  • Teams that need call tracking plus queues, IVR and agent tools: CallTrackingMetrics.
  • Enterprises with high call volume and conversation analytics needs: Invoca.
  • Pay-per-call publishers and lead buyers routing calls between partners: Ringba.
  • Google-only advertisers with modest needs: Google Ads call conversion tracking.

Who needs call tracking for paid campaigns?

You need it when a meaningful share of paid leads arrive by phone, because the ad platforms can't see a call that happens after someone reads your number on a landing page. Without it, bidding algorithms optimize toward form fills and treat your best phone leads as if they never happened.

Two scenarios show the gap. A local plumbing or legal firm may get most of its booked jobs from calls made on a phone, often within minutes of clicking an ad. A B2B company selling a complex service may see fewer calls, but each one is a sales conversation worth far more than a whitepaper download. In both cases the paid team is flying blind if calls don't reach the reporting.

Call tracking closes the gap in three steps. It shows a trackable number to each visitor, records which source and session that number was shown to, and then passes the call (and ideally its outcome) to your analytics, CRM and ad accounts. How do dynamic number insertion, number pools and offline imports work?

Dynamic number insertion (DNI) is a script on your site that swaps your displayed phone number for a tracking number based on how the visitor arrived. A number pool assigns a unique number to each active visitor so a call can be tied to a specific session, keyword and click ID rather than just a channel.

There are two levels of detail. Source-level tracking gives each channel its own number: one for Google Ads, one for Meta, one for the print ad. It's cheap and simple but can't tell you which keyword drove the call. Session-level tracking uses a pool of numbers, rotating them among visitors, so each call inherits the visitor's landing page, UTM values and click ID. Pool size has to match your concurrent traffic, or numbers get reused while a previous visitor might still call, which muddies attribution.

Offline conversion imports are the step most teams skip. Once a call is tied to a click ID, the tracking platform (or your CRM) can send that conversion, and later its qualified or sold status, back to Google Ads and other platforms so bidding learns which clicks produce real customers. Google documents both a website call tracking setup using Google forwarding numbers and a route to import call conversions from another system; check the current requirements in your account before you build the integration, because import options change.

What is each call tracking tool best for, and when should you skip it?

CallRail and WhatConverts cover most small and mid-sized advertisers, while CallTrackingMetrics, Invoca and Ringba each serve a narrower operating model. The blocks below separate what the vendors document from our editorial assessment.

CallRail: best for local-service advertisers and their agencies

What it does: CallRail is a call tracking and lead attribution platform built around dynamic number insertion and call recording. Its pricing page sets out the current plans; check there what each includes and how extra numbers and minutes are charged.

Strengths: In our assessment, its focus on number-level attribution for local leads suits agencies serving local businesses; confirm the ad platform and CRM integrations you need on its site.

Limitations: Usage-based costs can grow as you add numbers or minutes, so price a large session-level pool before you commit. Skip it if you need a full contact center with queues and agent scripting.

WhatConverts: best for agencies that report every lead type in one place

What it does: WhatConverts positions itself around a single lead log that brings calls, forms and other lead types together with their marketing source. Plans are tiered; see the official pricing page for current allowances.

Strengths: The lead-centric model suits client reporting, because a client can see "leads by campaign" rather than separate call and form reports.

Limitations: Routing and agent tooling are lighter than a contact-center platform. Skip it if call handling, not attribution, is your bottleneck.

CallTrackingMetrics: best for teams that also run the phones

What it does: CallTrackingMetrics pairs call attribution with contact-center features such as call routing and agent tools. Check what each plan includes on the CTM pricing page.

Strengths: One system for tracking and answering calls avoids passing data between a tracker and a separate phone system.

Limitations: More configuration than a pure tracker, and more than a small advertiser needs. Skip it if you just need call counts by campaign.

Invoca: best for enterprises with high call volume

What it does: Invoca focuses on conversation intelligence for large brands, analyzing calls to classify outcomes and pushing those signals into ad and analytics platforms. Expect a sales-led buying process; see the official site.

Strengths: In our assessment, suited to high-volume programs where call outcomes need classifying at scale.

Limitations: Longer procurement and implementation. Skip it if you handle a few hundred calls a month; the overhead won't pay back.

Ringba: best for pay-per-call operations

What it does: Ringba is built around routing and distributing calls between publishers and buyers; see its official site for current features.

Strengths: Strong fit for lead generation businesses that sell or buy calls.

Limitations: It's not designed around a single brand's marketing reporting. Skip it if you only answer your own calls.

Google Ads call conversion tracking: best for Google-only advertisers

What it does: Google's phone call conversion tracking can count calls from call assets and calls to a Google forwarding number shown on your site after an ad click. No separate subscription is needed.

Strengths: No extra subscription, and conversions feed Smart Bidding directly.

Limitations: It doesn't attribute calls from Microsoft Ads, Meta, organic search or offline media, and it isn't a call recording or call-analysis platform. Skip it if you run more than one paid channel or need call quality data.

Does call tracking attribution hold up once real calls come in?

Documented features describe the ideal path; in practice, attribution quality depends on pool sizing, how often people call back from a saved number, and whether call outcomes get recorded at all. Treat early reports as directional until you've reconciled tracked calls against what your phone system logged.

Which tool fits a local-service or B2B scenario?

Local-service advertisers usually need high-volume, session-level tracking with spam filtering and simple outcome tagging. B2B teams usually need fewer numbers but tighter CRM sync, so a call becomes an opportunity with the original campaign attached.

Local-service scenario (hypothetical): a multi-location home-services company runs Google and Microsoft search ads plus local service listings. It needs a number pool sized to peak concurrent visitors, a separate number per location, recordings for coaching, and a way to mark booked jobs so they can be sent back as conversions. CallRail or WhatConverts fits; CTM fits if the company also staffs a central call team.

B2B scenario (hypothetical): a software or professional-services firm gets a handful of demo calls a week from paid search and LinkedIn. Here the priority is pushing each call into the CRM as an activity on the right contact and routing it to the right rep. Pair the tracker with your CRM (see our guide to CRM software for small businesses) and a routing layer such as those in our roundup of lead routing and scheduling tools.

What should you check before you buy?

Check recording consent, spam handling and total number cost before features, because those are where call tracking projects go wrong. Then confirm the integrations you need actually pass click IDs and call outcomes, not just call counts.

  • Recording controls: can you play a consent message, disable recording by number or location, and redact sensitive data such as card details? Recording laws differ by state and country; confirm requirements with your own counsel.
  • Spam detection: how does the tool flag robocalls and repeat spam numbers, and can you exclude them from conversions sent to ad platforms?
  • Routing: time-of-day rules, location routing, round-robin and voicemail fallbacks.
  • Transcription and analysis: is it included, an add-on, or charged per call?
  • Number costs: local versus toll-free numbers, porting your existing number, and the cost of a pool large enough for your traffic.
  • Reports: calls by keyword, first-time versus repeat callers, missed calls and outcome tagging, ideally fed into the same view as your cross-channel paid media reporting rather than a separate dashboard.
  • Ad platform sync: which platforms receive conversions, and can you send qualified or revenue values later?

Phone spam and bot form fills often show up together; if lead quality is your worry, compare these controls with the tools in our guide to ad fraud detection software.

What else do buyers ask about call tracking for paid campaigns?

Most questions come down to accuracy, number portability and whether native ad platform tracking is enough. The short answers are below.

How many tracking numbers do I need for keyword-level tracking?

Enough to give each concurrent visitor their own number for the length of a typical session. Vendors usually size pools from your traffic; too few numbers means calls get attributed to the wrong visitor.

Can I keep my existing business phone number?

Usually yes. Your main number stays on your site for non-paid visitors and in listings, while tracking numbers forward to it. Some vendors also let you port an existing number into the platform.

Is Google Ads call tracking enough on its own?

It can be if Google Ads is your only paid channel and you only need call counts. Once you run several channels or need recordings and outcomes, a dedicated tool gives a fuller picture.

Does call tracking hurt local SEO?

It can if tracking numbers replace your main number inconsistently in listings. Keep your primary number as the listed number and use tracking numbers carefully where the vendor's guidance supports it.

Is call recording legal?

Recording rules depend on where callers and your business are located, and some places require all parties to consent. Use the platform's consent messages and confirm requirements with legal counsel; this guide isn't legal advice.

What's the verdict on call tracking software for PPC?

For most advertisers whose paid leads arrive by phone, CallRail or WhatConverts is the sensible shortlist, with CallTrackingMetrics stepping in when you also need contact-center features. Invoca is for large programs, and Google's native tracking is a reasonable start only if Google Ads is your single paid channel.

Whichever you choose, the value comes from closing the loop: tie calls to clicks, tag outcomes, and send them back to bidding. A tracker that only counts calls leaves bidding unable to tell a sales call from a wrong number.
