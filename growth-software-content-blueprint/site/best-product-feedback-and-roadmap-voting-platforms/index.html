Which product feedback and voting tool should you choose?

Choose Canny if you want mature voting, duplicate merging and customer segments in one product, or Featurebase if you want feedback, roadmap and changelog bundled per seat. Choose Productboard when votes are only one input into a wider prioritization process, and Frill or Nolt when you need a clean public board without much administration.

  • Canny: a good fit for B2B SaaS teams that need to see which accounts sit behind each request.
  • Featurebase: a good fit for teams that also want in-app widgets and a changelog without stitching tools together.
  • Productboard: a good fit for product teams that link feedback notes to features and score them against strategy.
  • Frill: a good fit for small teams that want ideas, roadmap and announcements on a flat plan.
  • Nolt: a good fit for teams that want an unfussy board with little administration.
  • UserVoice: a good fit for larger organizations that run formal feedback programs across many product lines.

Who needs roadmap voting software, and what problem does it solve?

You need dedicated software once feature requests arrive through support tickets, sales calls, Slack and email faster than one person can track them. The real problem isn't collection; it's that duplicates hide true demand and customers never hear back.

A spreadsheet works until two people log the same request under different names. After that, counts drift, the loudest customer wins, and nobody can tell a requester that their idea shipped. A voting board fixes the visibility problem and creates a new one: votes become the only prioritization signal. A feature with hundreds of votes from free users can matter less than one with six votes from the accounts that carry most of your revenue.

This page is for product managers and founders at SaaS companies choosing a first dedicated tool, or replacing a board that has become a graveyard of unanswered posts. What does each platform do well, and where does it fall short?

Each platform below collects ideas and supports voting; the differences lie in deduplication, customer context and how they close the loop. Read the limitations and skip-it-if notes as closely as the strengths.

Canny: best for B2B teams that weigh votes by account

Canny runs feedback boards, voting, a roadmap and a changelog, and staff can capture feedback on behalf of customers. Canny's help center describes an Autopilot feature for spotting likely duplicates and a merge option that keeps voters attached; confirm the current behavior there. The vendor also describes customer segments for filtering ideas by account attributes.

Pricing: Canny's pricing scales with tracked users, and no figures are quoted here because the page couldn't be fetched during drafting, so check the current tiers directly.

Limitations: because cost follows tracked users, a product with a large free user base can climb tiers quickly, so check which plan includes segmentation. Skip it if you're a consumer app with many low-value voters and no CRM data to attach.

Featurebase: best for an all-in-one board, roadmap and changelog

Featurebase positions itself as one product for feedback boards, voting, roadmaps, changelogs and in-app widgets. The vendor's pricing explainer describes per-seat plans; confirm what each plan includes there.

Limitations: plan inclusions couldn't be confirmed during drafting, so a company with several product lines should check how boards and seats map to plans before buying. Skip it if you already run a changelog and in-app widgets elsewhere and only need voting; you'd be paying for a bundle you won't use.

Productboard: best when votes are one input among many

Productboard is built around feedback notes that product managers link to features and weigh against objectives. That design answers the risk of votes becoming the only signal, because a feature's evidence is a set of linked customer notes rather than a raw count.

Pricing is tiered by maker seats; confirm current plans on Productboard's pricing page.

Limitations: it's a product management system, not a lightweight board, so it needs an owner who keeps the taxonomy clean. Skip it if you only need a public idea board; see our roadmap prioritization software guide for the planning side.

Frill: best for small teams wanting a polished public board

Frill bundles ideas, a public roadmap and announcements, per the Frill website. Check the current plans and trial terms there, since they couldn't be fetched during drafting.

Limitations: plan limits couldn't be confirmed, so check them before you commit. Skip it if you need to weigh votes by account revenue.

Nolt: best for a simple, low-admin board

Nolt provides feedback boards, voting, statuses and a roadmap. Plan structure and which integrations each tier includes are listed on Nolt's pricing page; confirm them there, since the page couldn't be fetched during drafting.

Limitations: it's positioned as a simple board rather than a prioritization system. Skip it if you need revenue-weighted prioritization or deep CRM sync.

UserVoice: best for enterprise feedback programs

UserVoice is a feedback management product aimed at larger organizations that gather requests from customers, support and sales across several products. Pricing is quote-based; start on the UserVoice website.

Limitations: it's priced and scoped for larger programs, and the published feature detail couldn't be checked during drafting. Skip it if you're a startup with one product and one product manager.

Do voting boards actually change what gets built?

Only if someone owns triage and the board feeds a prioritization meeting; otherwise a voting board becomes a public list of ignored requests. The product documentation reviewed shows every tool can collect and count, but none can make a team respond.

What governance rules stop votes becoming the only prioritization signal?

Write down who triages, how duplicates are merged, and what other evidence a request needs before it reaches the roadmap. Then publish your statuses so customers know a vote is input, not a commitment.

  1. Triage owner and cadence. One named person reviews new posts at least weekly, merges duplicates and tags each post to a product area.
  2. Merge, don't delete. Merging keeps every voter attached, so they're notified later. Deleting duplicates throws away demand.
  3. Attach account context. Pass plan, company and revenue from your CRM so you can see who's asking, not just how many.
  4. Require a second signal. Before an idea moves to Planned, check usage data in your product analytics tool, interview evidence or strategic fit.
  5. Define public statuses. Under review, Planned, In progress, Shipped and Not planned. Avoid dates on the public view.
  6. Review the board quarterly. Close or archive anything untouched for two quarters, with a reason.

How should you close the loop with customers who voted?

Notify voters automatically at each status change, and add a personal message from the account owner for high-value accounts. Closing the loop on Not planned matters as much as announcing a launch.

Use the tool's built-in notifications for the broad audience, then ask customer success to follow up with the named accounts that drove the decision. Pair the launch with a changelog entry and, where adoption matters, an in-app announcement; our guide to feature adoption analytics covers how to check whether requesters actually use what shipped. Which platform fits your scenario?

Match the tool to who submits feedback and how you decide. A self-serve product with many users needs strong deduplication and cost control; a sales-led product needs account context and internal capture.

  • Early-stage startup, one product: Nolt or Frill, then move up when you need segmentation.
  • B2B SaaS with a CRM and account managers: Canny or Productboard, so votes carry company and plan data.
  • Team that also wants a changelog and help widget: Featurebase.
  • Several product lines and formal governance: UserVoice or Productboard.

What else do buyers ask about feedback and voting tools?

Most remaining questions are about public visibility, internal capture and how votes should feed prioritization. The short answers below cover the points that come up during shortlisting.

Should our roadmap be public or private?

Public roadmaps build trust with customers who asked for a feature, but they turn every status into a commitment. Many teams publish a coarse public view (Planned, In progress, Shipped) and keep dates, scoring and internal context private.

Can support and sales log votes for customers?

Most tools in this category describe a way for staff to submit or vote on behalf of a customer, which matters because many B2B buyers never visit a public board. Check that the vote is attributed to the customer's account, not to the staff member.

How many votes make an idea worth building?

There's no threshold that works across products. Treat votes as one demand signal and weigh them against revenue at stake, strategic fit, effort and the evidence from product analytics.

Do we still need a feedback tool if we use Jira or Linear?

An issue tracker stores work the team has committed to; a feedback tool stores demand that hasn't been accepted yet. Keeping them separate stops the backlog filling with unvetted requests, and integrations sync status between the two.

What should we do with ideas we'll never build?

Close them with a short, honest reason and notify the voters. A visible Not planned status costs less goodwill than leaving a request open for two years.

What mistakes do buyers make with voting tools?

The most common mistake is ranking the roadmap by vote count. The second is launching a public board without the staff time to triage and respond.

  • Letting staff vote as themselves instead of on behalf of the customer, which loses attribution.
  • Choosing a tracked-user pricing model without estimating how many end users will interact with the board.
  • Skipping the integration with the issue tracker, so statuses drift out of sync.
  • Never using Not planned, so the board fills with requests that will never move.

What's the verdict on product feedback and voting platforms?

For most B2B SaaS teams, Canny or Featurebase is the practical starting point, and Productboard is the better choice once feedback has to feed a structured prioritization process. Whatever you pick, governance matters more than features.
