Which composable CMS should an enterprise marketing team choose?

Choose Contentful or Contentstack when you need a mature, governed platform for many brands, regions and teams, and Storyblok when marketers' visual editing is the deciding factor. Sanity suits teams with strong developers who want a fully customized editing experience, Hygraph suits API-heavy architectures that pull content from several sources, Sitecore XM Cloud suits existing Sitecore estates, and Strapi suits teams that must self-host.

  • Global brands with many regions, roles and approval steps: Contentful or Contentstack.
  • Marketing-led teams that need in-context visual editing: Storyblok.
  • Engineering-strong teams that want to shape the editor itself: Sanity.
  • Architectures that federate content from several back-end systems: Hygraph.
  • Organizations moving off a legacy Sitecore platform: Sitecore XM Cloud.
  • Teams with self-hosting, data-residency or open-source requirements: Strapi.

Is a composable CMS the same decision as a visual website builder?

No. A composable CMS stores structured content and serves it through APIs to any front end, while a visual builder such as Webflow bundles content, design and hosting into one product. Enterprise teams usually need the CMS decision first and a separate decision about the front end and page-building experience on top.

That split is the point of "composable": the CMS, front-end framework, search, personalization, DAM and commerce engine are separate services joined by APIs. You gain the freedom to swap any one of them and reuse the same content across sites, apps and regions. You also take on an integration project and a front-end team to maintain it. If one marketing site with a handful of editors is the whole requirement, a builder is often the better answer, as our B2B marketing website builder guide explains.

What is each platform best for, and when should you skip it?

Contentful and Contentstack are the safest enterprise defaults, Storyblok is the most marketer-friendly, and Sanity, Hygraph, Sitecore XM Cloud and Strapi each win when a specific technical or organizational constraint dominates. Product descriptions come from vendor materials; fit judgments are our editorial assessment.

Contentful: best for governed, multi-region content

What it does: Contentful is an API-first content platform with content modeling, roles and permissions, localization, environments for safe changes, and an app framework for extending the editor. Plan tiers and enterprise terms are on the Contentful pricing page.

Strengths: Mature governance and a large partner and integration ecosystem make it a common enterprise standard for many brands and regions.

Limitations: Marketers often find pure form-based editing abstract without a preview or visual layer configured. Costs scale with spaces, environments and usage. Skip it if you have one site, a small team and no front-end developers.

Contentstack: best for an enterprise digital experience stack

What it does: Contentstack positions itself as a composable digital experience platform, combining a headless CMS with workflows, live preview, automation and personalization options. Pricing is quote-based.

Strengths: Strong workflow and approval controls suit regulated or multi-team publishing, and the platform covers more of the experience stack than a pure CMS.

Limitations: Breadth means more to license and configure, and some capabilities are separate products. Skip it if you only need structured content and prefer to pick best-of-breed tools around it.

Storyblok: best for marketers who need visual editing

What it does: Storyblok is a headless CMS built around reusable components and a visual editor that shows content in the context of the live page, with localization and workflow features. See Storyblok pricing for tiers.

Strengths: It narrows the gap between a composable architecture and the editing experience marketers expect from a builder.

Limitations: Visual freedom can encourage one-off layouts, so the component library needs ownership. Very complex content relationships may fit other platforms better. Skip it if your content is mostly structured data for apps rather than pages.

Sanity: best for teams that want to shape the editor

What it does: Sanity pairs a hosted content store with Sanity Studio, an open-source editing environment that developers configure in code, plus real-time collaboration and a query language for content. Plans are listed on the Sanity pricing page.

Strengths: Almost any content model or editorial workflow can be built, and real-time collaboration suits busy teams.

Limitations: The flexibility is developer-delivered, so editor quality depends on your engineering investment. Skip it if you lack ongoing developer capacity for the CMS itself.

Hygraph: best for federating content from many sources

What it does: Hygraph is a GraphQL-native headless CMS with content federation, which lets one API combine CMS content with data from other systems. See Hygraph pricing for plans.

Strengths: A good fit when product, commerce or PIM data must appear alongside marketing content without duplication.

Limitations: Most valuable to GraphQL-first teams; marketers gain less from federation than developers do. Skip it if your architecture doesn't use GraphQL or need federated sources.

Sitecore XM Cloud: best for existing Sitecore estates

What it does: Sitecore XM Cloud is Sitecore's SaaS, headless content management product, with a page-building experience and integration with Sitecore's wider marketing suite. Pricing is quote-based.

Strengths: A natural path for organizations already invested in Sitecore skills, partners and content.

Limitations: Implementation typically needs a systems integrator, so budget for partner fees on top of the licence. Skip it if you're not on Sitecore today and have no reason to join that ecosystem.

Strapi: best for self-hosting and open-source control

What it does: Strapi is an open-source headless CMS you can self-host, with a managed cloud and paid enterprise features; see Strapi pricing.

Strengths: Full control over hosting, data location and code, without per-seat licensing for the open-source edition.

Limitations: You own upgrades, scaling, backups and security, and enterprise governance features sit on paid tiers. Skip it if your team doesn't want to operate infrastructure.

Do composable CMS projects really deliver faster publishing for marketers?

The vendors document faster, more flexible publishing, but marketers only feel it once the front end, preview and component library are in place, which can take months. Many teams experience a slower period first, while editors adapt to structured content and developers finish the presentation layer.

What requirements should be on your composable CMS checklist?

Write requirements for the content model, workflow, localization, preview, SEO governance, integrations and exit before you see a demo. Vendors demo strengths; a written checklist forces them to show your hard cases.

  1. Structured content: can you model pages, modules, products, authors and campaigns as reusable types with references and validation?
  2. Roles and workflow: custom roles by brand, region and content type; multi-step approvals; scheduled publishing; audit history.
  3. Localization: field-level translation, fallback locales, and a connector to your translation tools. Our comparison of translation management systems and localization platforms covers that layer.
  4. APIs and environments: REST or GraphQL, rate limits, webhooks, and sandbox environments for schema changes.
  5. Preview and page building: how marketers see a draft in context, and whether page assembly happens in the CMS or the front end.
  6. SEO governance: required fields for titles, descriptions, canonicals and redirects, enforced by validation rather than goodwill.
  7. Maintenance: who owns the front end, upgrades, monitoring and editor support after launch.
  8. Migration and exit: import tooling, content export formats and the effort to leave.

What mistakes do enterprise teams make with composable CMS projects?

The biggest mistake is buying a composable CMS for its flexibility and then rebuilding page-centric templates inside it, which throws away content reuse. The second is underestimating the front-end and integration work, which can rival the licence in cost.

Other common errors: migrating every legacy page instead of pruning first; skipping preview because it seems like a developer detail; and leaving SEO redirects and metadata until the last sprint. What else do enterprise teams ask about composable CMS platforms?

Most questions are about terminology, migration time and marketer control. Short answers follow.

What does composable CMS mean?

It means the CMS is one replaceable service in a stack joined by APIs, alongside separate front-end, search, commerce and personalization tools. Content is stored as structured data and delivered to any channel.

Is headless CMS the same as composable CMS?

Headless describes the CMS itself, which has no built-in front end. Composable describes the wider architecture, where a headless CMS is combined with other independent services.

How long does a composable CMS migration take?

It depends on content volume, the number of sites and the front-end build, so there's no reliable general figure. Plan for content modeling, front-end development, migration and SEO redirect work as separate phases.

Do marketers lose control with a composable CMS?

They can, if no preview or visual layer is configured. Platforms such as Storyblok, and preview features on the others, exist to restore that control.

What's the verdict on composable CMS platforms for enterprise marketing?

Contentful and Contentstack are the strongest fits for large, governed, multi-region programs, and Storyblok is the best choice when marketer editing experience decides the purchase. Sanity, Hygraph, Sitecore XM Cloud and Strapi are strong in narrower situations defined by engineering capacity, architecture, legacy estate or hosting rules.
