Which content workflow tool should an agency choose?

Choose Teamwork.com or Wrike if you want one system for client projects, tasks and approvals, and add Filestage or Ziflow when review rounds on documents, designs and video are the real bottleneck. Planable fits agencies whose content is mostly social, and Content Workflow by Bynder fits website and product-copy projects that need structured templates.

  • Client work, time and delivery in one place: Teamwork.com.
  • Larger agencies with intake forms and cross-team workflows: Wrike.
  • Social and short-form content approved by clients: Planable.
  • Version-by-version review of files with external reviewers: Filestage.
  • Automated multi-stage proofing for creative-heavy work: Ziflow.
  • Structured copy for site builds and migrations: Content Workflow by Bynder.

What makes agency content workflow different from in-house?

An agency runs several approval chains at once, each with its own client stakeholders, brand rules and deadlines, and a single misrouted draft can expose one client's work to another. In-house teams can live with a shared calendar; agencies need hard separation, guest approvers and a revision trail they can show a client when scope is questioned.

Revision traceability is commercial, not just tidy. If a contract includes two rounds of changes, you need a record of what was requested, by whom and when. That pushes agencies towards tools with version history and per-reviewer comments rather than the lighter editorial calendar tools built for in-house authors.

What does each tool do well, and where does it fall short?

Read each block for the agency type it suits and the one it doesn't. Product descriptions should be confirmed on the linked vendor pages; fit is our editorial assessment.

Teamwork.com: best for agencies that run content as billable client projects

Teamwork.com positions itself around client work, with projects, tasks, time tracking and budget visibility in one system and client-facing access described on its site; confirm what client users can see and approve. For a content agency, the draw is that a brief, the writing task and the hours spent all sit in the same record. Tiers are on the Teamwork.com pricing page.

Strengths: profitability and delivery in one view, and client-facing access. Limitations: annotating a PDF or video is less developed than in Filestage or Ziflow. Skip it if you don't track time or budgets per client.

Wrike: best for larger agencies with intake forms and many departments

Wrike combines request forms, custom workflows, dashboards and proofing, so a brief can arrive through a form, follow a defined path and gather approvals, including from outside reviewers where the plan allows. It suits agencies with separate content, design and paid teams that share clients. See the Wrike pricing page for which features each tier carries.

Strengths: deep workflow configuration and reporting. Limitations: it takes real setup time and a dedicated owner. Skip it if you're a team of five who need to start this week.

Planable: best for social-first agencies that need client sign-off

Planable shows posts as they will appear on each network and lets clients comment and approve in context, with separate workspaces per client. That removes the screenshot-in-email loop for social calendars. Plans are on the Planable pricing page.

Strengths: intuitive for non-technical client approvers. Limitations: less suited to long-form articles, design files and complex task dependencies. Skip it if social is a small share of your output.

Filestage: best for reviewing files with clients and outside reviewers

Filestage focuses on the review step: reviewers comment on documents, images and video, each version is kept, and review stages can be set per project. That gives agencies a clean record of what each client asked to change. Plans are listed on the Filestage pricing page.

Strengths: clear version history and simple reviewer experience. Limitations: it doesn't assign, schedule or track the work around the file. Skip it if you want one tool for everything; pair it with a project tool instead.

Ziflow: best for creative-heavy agencies with multi-stage proofing

Ziflow is an online proofing tool with automated review stages, so a proof can pass from internal reviewers to the client to legal in sequence. It suits agencies producing design, video and campaign assets alongside copy. See the Ziflow pricing page for tiers.

Strengths: structured approval routing for complex assets. Limitations: more than a writing-only agency needs. Skip it if your deliverables are mainly blog posts. Our list of creative proofing software for agency client approvals goes deeper on this step.

Content Workflow by Bynder: best for structured site and product copy

Content Workflow, formerly GatherContent, lets you build templates that mirror the CMS fields, collect copy against them from writers and client subject experts, and move items through workflow stages. It fits agencies delivering website rebuilds and migrations. Details are on the Bynder Content Workflow page.

Strengths: consistent structure across hundreds of pages. Limitations: it doesn't handle time, billing or social scheduling. Skip it if you rarely run templated page projects.

Clients may fall back to email unless approving in the tool is genuinely easier, which is why reviewer experience and guest access matter more than internal features. Vendor pages describe the approval flow, but they can't tell you how a busy client stakeholder will behave.

Where does work jam when five clients are live at once?

With five concurrent clients, work usually jams at client approval and at the editor, not at drafting. The model below is hypothetical: five clients, each taking four articles a month, served by three writers, one editor and one designer.

  • Twenty drafts a month, one editor: if every draft needs an edit pass and a revision check, the editor touches each piece at least twice. Batch edits per client and set a cut-off day.
  • Five sets of approvers: each client may have a marketer plus a subject expert. Without a fixed response window, one slow approver delays the whole month's schedule.
  • Design requests: header images and diagrams queue behind campaign work. Track them as linked tasks with their own due dates.
  • Brief quality: vague briefs create extra revision rounds. Standardise them; our list of content brief and assignment tools helps.
  • Client separation: one workspace or project per client, with guests limited to their own items, prevents cross-client leaks.

The fix is rarely a bigger tool. It's a review window in the contract, a cap on revision rounds and a tool that makes those limits visible.

Should you run one tool or a project tool plus a proofing tool?

Small, writing-led agencies can usually run on one project tool with basic approvals, while design- and video-heavy agencies get more from a project tool paired with a dedicated proofing tool. The trade-off is one subscription and one login versus a better client review experience.

If you pair tools, decide which one is the source of truth for status and connect them, or the two systems will disagree. Our roundups of project management software for digital marketing agencies and content approval software cover each half in detail.

What should you check before signing?

Check how guests and client reviewers are billed, whether client workspaces are truly separate, and whether you can export projects and version history if you leave. Those three answers decide both the real cost and the switching risk.

  • Are client reviewers free guests or paid seats, and is there a cap?
  • Can you brand the client view, and on which tier?
  • Is version history kept for every round, and for how long?
  • Can you export comments and approvals as evidence of sign-off?
  • Does the tool connect to your CMS and chat tool, or will someone copy-paste?

What else do agencies ask about content workflow tools?

These answers cover white-labelling, freelancers and the overlap with general project tools. Confirm vendor-specific details on official pages before relying on them.

Do agency workflow tools offer white-label client portals?

Some may offer custom branding or client-facing views, and where they do, it can depend on the plan. Ask the vendor exactly what can be branded, because "white-label" ranges from a logo to a fully branded domain.

How should freelancers be set up across several clients?

Give each freelancer access only to the client projects they work on. Check whether freelancers count as full seats, since that can change the cost more than the base plan.

Can one tool handle both content and paid media workflows?

A general project tool such as Wrike or Teamwork.com can, if each service line has its own workflow. Specialist tools like Planable or Filestage cover one step and need a project tool around them.

How many revision rounds should an agency allow?

That's a commercial decision rather than a software one, but a common approach is to write a fixed number of rounds into the contract. Whatever you agree, choose a tool that records each round so the limit can be enforced.

What's the verdict?

For most content-led agencies, a client-work tool such as Teamwork.com, or Wrike at larger scale, is the backbone, with Filestage or Ziflow added only when file review is the bottleneck. Social-first agencies should look at Planable first, and site-build specialists at Content Workflow by Bynder.
