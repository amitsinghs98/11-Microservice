Which event networking software should you choose?

Pick a dedicated matchmaking tool when booked meetings are the main reason people buy a ticket, and pick an all-in-one platform when networking is a supporting feature. That single question, meetings as the product or meetings as a perk, removes most of the shortlist before you look at features.

  • Partnering events and investor or buyer-supplier meetings: Brella or Grip, because scheduled one-to-one meetings are their core workflow.
  • Trade shows where networking feeds exhibitor leads: Swapcard, which ties matchmaking to exhibitor and sponsor tools.
  • Enterprise conferences already run on Cvent: Cvent's attendee app, so profiles and appointments sit on the registration record you already own.
  • Mid-sized conferences and associations on a lean team: Whova, where networking lives inside a broad event app.
  • Marketing-led conference programmes: Bizzabo, if you want networking from the same vendor as your wider event platform.
  • Virtual or hybrid audiences who need informal mingling: Airmeet, which positions itself around virtual and hybrid events where informal mingling matters more than a meeting grid.

What problem does event networking software actually solve?

It solves the discovery and logistics problem: helping the right two people find each other, agree a time and meet at a known table or video room. Without it, most conference networking is left to chance in the coffee queue, and the attendees who came specifically to meet buyers, partners or investors leave disappointed.

The organizer benefits too. Meeting data shows which attendee segments are in demand, which sponsors generated conversations and whether the event delivered on its networking promise. That evidence matters when you are pricing next year's sponsorship packages.

If your event is mostly content and networking is a nice extra, you may not need a separate tool. Your virtual event platform or registration system may already include attendee chat and a directory that is good enough.

What does each networking tool do well, and where does it fall short?

Brella and Grip are the specialists, Swapcard and Cvent are platforms with strong networking modules, and Whova, Bizzabo and Airmeet treat networking as one part of a broader event product. Read the skip-it-if lines first, because the limitations separate these tools more clearly than the feature lists do.

Brella: best for conferences sold on meetings

What it does: Brella positions itself around attendee matchmaking and scheduled one-to-one meetings at in-person, virtual and hybrid events. Confirm on Brella's own pages how matches are generated and how meeting slots are set up.

Strengths: the meeting workflow is the product, so organizers get control over slots, tables and who can meet whom. That suits partnering events; ask whether you can set rules on who may request meetings with whom.

Limitations: you will likely still need a separate registration and ticketing system, so check the integration and how attendee data flows in. Prices were not confirmed for this draft, so check Brella's site for current terms.

Skip it if networking is a minor part of your agenda and attendees mainly come for sessions.

Grip: best for large trade-show meeting programmes

What it does: Grip markets matchmaking and meeting scheduling for trade shows and conferences; confirm on Grip's own pages how recommendations are generated.

Strengths: it is built for scale and for exhibitor-visitor matching, which makes it a natural fit for organizers who sell hosted-buyer or meeting packages.

Limitations: AI matching is only as good as the profile and registration data behind it. If your registration form captures little more than name and company, recommendations will be generic. Confirm what data the model uses and whether attendees can see why a match was suggested.

Skip it if you run a single-track conference of a few hundred people, where a simple directory and meeting request feature will do.

Swapcard: best when networking and exhibitor leads must connect

What it does: Swapcard markets hybrid event software with attendee networking, alongside exhibitor and sponsor tools aimed at lead capture; confirm which lead sources and meeting formats your package covers.

Strengths: meeting activity becomes lead data for exhibitors, which strengthens your sponsorship story. Ask whether its wider platform could replace any tools you already run.

Limitations: a broad platform takes longer to configure and train exhibitors on. Budget onboarding time for exhibitor staff, not just your own team.

Skip it if you only need a meeting scheduler and have no exhibitor programme.

Cvent: best for teams already on Cvent registration

What it does: Cvent sells event management and registration software; if you already run registration there, ask which attendee app and networking features your package includes and whether they use the same attendee record.

Strengths: one data record across registration, check-in, sessions and networking reduces export-import work and keeps consent choices in one place.

Limitations: the value depends on the rest of the suite; buying Cvent just for networking is rarely the efficient choice. Prices were not confirmed for this draft, so confirm terms and included networking features with Cvent.

Skip it if you are not on Cvent and don't plan to move registration there.

Whova: best for mid-sized conferences on lean teams

What it does: Whova markets an event app and management platform in which networking is one part of a broader feature set; check its site for the current networking features.

Strengths: attendees get one app for the agenda and networking, and small teams avoid integrating a separate tool.

Limitations: networking is one module, so formal meeting-slot management is not as central as in Brella or Grip. Prices were not confirmed for this draft, so check Whova's site for current terms.

Skip it if pre-scheduled one-to-one meetings are what your sponsors are paying for.

Bizzabo: best for marketing-led conference programmes

What it does: Bizzabo sells an event platform covering in-person, virtual and hybrid formats, with networking as one part of the wider platform.

Strengths: it suits marketing teams that would rather buy a broad event platform from one vendor than stitch networking onto a separate stack; confirm which modules your package includes.

Limitations: if networking is your only gap, a full platform migration is a lot of change for one feature.

Skip it if you are happy with your registration stack and need only matchmaking.

Airmeet: best for virtual and hybrid mingling

What it does: Airmeet describes virtual and hybrid event formats; confirm on its own pages which informal networking features, such as virtual tables, are available.

Strengths: informal, table-based networking feels closer to a real coffee break than a meeting request grid, which helps remote attendees.

Limitations: for formal in-person meeting programmes with physical tables and slot capacity, a matchmaking specialist is the better fit.

Skip it if your event is entirely in person.

Which features matter most when you compare networking platforms?

Matching logic, meeting capacity and calendar sync decide whether meetings happen; moderation, privacy and accessibility decide whether attendees trust the tool enough to use it. Ask every vendor the same questions so you can compare answers side by side.

Matching logic

Ask what inputs drive recommendations: declared interests, job role, company type, session choices or behaviour in the app. Ask whether organizers can set rules, such as buyers-only requests, and whether attendees see why someone was suggested. Opaque matching erodes trust quickly.

Meeting capacity and locations

Confirm how you define meeting slots, how many tables or rooms you can assign and what happens when someone double-books or no-shows. For hybrid events, check whether a meeting can be in person for one party and video for the other.

Calendar sync and the mobile app

Accepted meetings should land in attendees' own calendars, and the app should send reminders with the table number. Test the app on older phones and poor venue Wi-Fi before you commit.

Moderation, privacy and consent

Check whether attendees can hide their profile, block someone, report messages and choose whether contact details are shared. Make the consent question explicit at registration, especially if meeting data will be passed to exhibitors, and keep that choice with the attendee record.

Accessibility

Ask for the vendor's accessibility conformance documentation, and check screen-reader support, text scaling and captioning for any video meetings. Offer a non-app route, such as a help desk that books meetings, for attendees who can't use the app.

Integrations and analytics

Registration data should flow in without manual uploads, and meeting outcomes should flow out to your CRM. Meeting requests, acceptance rates and attended meetings by segment are the organizer analytics worth asking for.

Do attendees really use conference networking apps?

Adoption is the biggest risk with any networking tool, because matches and meeting slots are worthless if attendees never open the app. The documentation we reviewed explains features well but cannot tell you how many of your attendees will complete a profile, so plan promotion and onsite help as part of the rollout.

Which networking setup fits your event?

Match the tool to the event's format and commercial model rather than to its size alone. Three common setups cover most business conferences.

A hosted-buyer or partnering event: a matchmaking specialist like Brella or Grip, with strict request rules and defined meeting tables. Connect it to your event registration software so profile data arrives without re-entry.

A trade show with an exhibitor programme: Swapcard, or a platform paired with dedicated lead capture apps for exhibitors, so booth scans and meetings both become follow-up data.

A hybrid conference: make sure remote attendees can network with in-person attendees, not just with each other. Our guide to hybrid event management platforms covers the platform side.

What mistakes do organizers make with networking software?

The most common mistake is launching the networking module too late, so attendees arrive with empty profiles and no booked meetings. The second is collecting too little profile data to make matching meaningful.

  • Unclear consent: attendees discover after the event that their details went to exhibitors. State the data sharing plainly at registration.
  • No meeting logistics: meetings are booked but tables are unnumbered or too close to stage noise.
  • No follow-up path: meeting data stays in the event tool. Route it to your CRM, and use a lead routing tool if sales owns follow-up.
  • Ignoring no-shows: without reminders and easy cancellation, slots are wasted.

What else do buyers ask about event networking software?

Most questions concern adoption, data sharing and whether a separate tool is needed at all. Short answers follow.

Do I need separate networking software if my event app has a directory?

Not always. A directory and messaging are enough for content-led events, but meeting-led events usually need slot management, request rules and table assignment.

Can attendees opt out of being matched?

Privacy controls vary by platform, so confirm with each vendor whether attendees can hide their profile or limit who can contact them before launch.

How early should the networking module open?

Open it once registration is steady and enough profiles exist to make matches useful, so attendees can book meetings before they travel.

Should exhibitors see attendee meeting data?

Only with clear attendee consent captured at registration. Tell attendees exactly what is shared and with whom.

Does AI matchmaking replace good profile data?

No. Recommendations depend on what attendees tell you, so design registration questions that make matching meaningful.
