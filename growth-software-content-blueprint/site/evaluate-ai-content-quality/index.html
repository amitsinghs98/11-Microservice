How to Evaluate AI Content Quality for SEO, Accuracy, and Brand Safety

To evaluate AI content quality consistently, score every draft on the same seven dimensions and make factuality and rights hard gates that no overall score can offset. This guide gives you the scorecard, a repeatable sample test, the red flags that fail a draft outright and the thresholds for publishing.

What should an AI content review measure?

Seven things: factuality, usefulness, originality, search intent fit, brand voice, source quality, and rights and safety. Together they cover whether a piece is true, worth reading, distinctly yours and safe to publish.

Readability and keyword coverage matter too, but they are easy to optimize and say little about whether a reader can trust the page. Keep them as secondary checks, not the score.

How do you score each dimension?

Give each dimension 0 (fail), 1 (revise) or 2 (pass) against a written test, for a maximum of 14. Our editorial recommendation is to publish only at 11 or more, with no zeros and a 2 on both factuality and rights.

How do you run a repeatable sample test?

Score a fixed sample with two reviewers working independently, then compare. Where they differ by more than one point on a dimension, rewrite that dimension's test until they agree.

  1. Pick five recent pieces, or five drafts from a tool you're evaluating, all written to real briefs.
  2. Extract ten factual claims from each and check every one against its source.
  3. Have two reviewers score all seven dimensions without conferring.
  4. Compare scores and refine any test that produced disagreement.
  5. When evaluating a tool, run the same brief more than once. Anthropic's documentation describes comparing outputs of the same prompt, since inconsistencies can indicate hallucinations (checked 4 November 2026).
  6. Repeat the sample each quarter, or whenever you change tools or prompts.

Which red flags fail a draft immediately?

Anything invented: statistics without a source, quotes nobody said, testing nobody did, or links that go nowhere. These fail the piece regardless of its total score.

  • Precise numbers with no citation, or citations that don't contain the number.
  • Links to pages that don't exist, or that don't say what the draft claims. Open every one.
  • Claims that a tool did something it can't do. Anthropic's help center warns that Claude can sometimes hallucinate its capabilities (checked 4 November 2026).
  • First-person experience the writer never had.
  • Out-of-date product details stated as current.
  • Equal praise for every option, which signals no real assessment.
  • Images or likenesses with unknown rights.

Would this scorecard catch the errors that reach publication?

It catches the errors it samples, which is why the claim sample is the most important part of the test. A ten-claim sample can miss a wrong figure, so high-risk pages such as pricing guides deserve a full claim check rather than a sample.

Can an AI detector prove who wrote a draft?

No. Detector scores are probabilistic guesses that can flag human writing and miss edited machine text, so they are not evidence of authorship and shouldn't decide a contractor dispute.

The scorecard measures what matters to readers and search engines alike: whether the page is accurate, useful and original. If authorship matters contractually, ask for drafts, version history and the source pack instead.

Where do SEO tools fit in the evaluation?

They inform the intent-fit dimension and nothing else. A content score shows term coverage against ranking pages, not whether the claims are true or the advice is good.

What does a scored draft look like?

In this hypothetical example, a comparison draft scores 2 on intent, voice and source quality, 1 on usefulness and originality, and 2 on rights, so it would reach 12 of 14 with full marks for factuality. But one sampled claim states a plan limit that the vendor page no longer lists.

Factuality therefore scores 0, bringing the total to 10, and the draft fails on the hard gate whatever its total. The editor removes the limit, checks the remaining claims in full rather than by sample, and rescores before approval.

What else do editors ask about AI content quality?

Questions cluster around thresholds, frequency and who should score. Short answers follow.

Why make factuality a hard gate instead of weighting it?

Because a weighted average lets a well-written page with one invented figure pass. A single false claim can cost reader trust that no amount of polish recovers.

How often should we re-run the sample test?

Quarterly, and whenever you change tools, prompts or writers. Drift shows up first in the claim sample.

Should the writer score their own draft?

They can self-check, but the recorded score should come from someone else. Independent scoring is the point of the exercise.

What should you do next?

Copy the scorecard into your style guide, run the five-piece sample with two reviewers this month, and fix any test that produces disagreement. Then attach the score to each piece's approval record.
