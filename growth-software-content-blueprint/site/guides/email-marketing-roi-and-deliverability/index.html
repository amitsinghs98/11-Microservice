How to Measure Email Marketing ROI and Deliverability Together

Measure email marketing ROI and deliverability on one dashboard, because revenue only arrives when mail reaches the inbox. Pair each revenue figure with complaint, bounce and unsubscribe rates for the same sends, and test incrementality with holdouts rather than trusting attributed revenue alone.

Why measure ROI and deliverability together?

Because a campaign can look profitable while it erodes the sender reputation that every future campaign depends on. Revenue reports show this week's return; deliverability signals show whether next quarter's revenue is at risk.

Sending more email to more people usually raises attributed revenue in the short term. If it also lifts complaints and unsubscribes, inbox placement can slip and later sends earn less. Looking at the two side by side stops you rewarding the behavior that causes the decline.

Which metrics matter, and what are their caveats?

Track delivery, bounces, spam complaints, unsubscribes, clicks, conversions and revenue per recipient, and treat opens as a rough signal at best. Each metric has a known distortion, so read them together rather than alone.

  • Opens: Apple's Mail Privacy Protection downloads remote content privately in the background and stops senders learning when or how often a message was viewed (checked 4 November 2026), so open rates overstate real reading for affected recipients.
  • Clicks: more reliable, because Apple says Mail Privacy Protection doesn't extend to links, but security scanners can still click links automatically. Klaviyo, for example, turns on bot-click exclusion by default for accounts created on or after 27 August 2024 (checked 4 November 2026).
  • Spam complaints: the metric mailbox providers watch most closely. Gmail's guidance is to stay below a 0.1% user-reported spam rate and never reach 0.3% (checked 4 November 2026).
  • Bounces and unsubscribes: rising hard bounces point to list decay or poor sources; rising unsubscribes point to frequency or relevance problems.
  • Revenue per recipient: more useful than total revenue, because it doesn't rise just because you sent more.

How should you calculate email ROI?

Email ROI is incremental gross profit from email, minus the full cost of the program, divided by that cost. Use margin rather than revenue, and include labor, creative and discounts, not just the platform fee.

Email ROI = (incremental revenue × gross margin − program cost) ÷ program cost.

Program cost covers the platform, any deliverability or testing tools, staff time, agency fees and the cost of discounts you offer only by email. If you can't yet measure incremental revenue, calculate the same formula with attributed revenue, but label it as an upper estimate.

Why isn't attributed revenue the same as incremental revenue?

Attributed revenue counts purchases that happen after an email interaction, whether or not the email caused them. Incremental revenue is only the extra revenue that wouldn't have happened without the email, and you measure it with a holdout group.

Attribution rules are set by your platform. Klaviyo's default, for instance, credits a conversion to email within five days of an email open or click, using last-touch attribution, and the window can be changed (checked 4 November 2026). Because privacy features can register opens that never happened, open-based windows can credit email with purchases it didn't influence.

To estimate incrementality, hold back a random share of a segment from a campaign or flow, then compare revenue per person between the two groups over the same period. Run it long enough for the result to be stable, and don't change the holdout mid-test. How do you build cohort-based measurement?

Group subscribers by the month and source they joined, then track each cohort's revenue, engagement, unsubscribes and complaints at fixed intervals. Cohorts show whether new subscribers are worth less than old ones, which campaign totals hide.

  1. Tag every subscriber with signup month and acquisition source.
  2. For each cohort, record revenue per subscriber, click rate, unsubscribe rate and complaint rate at 30, 60 and 90 days.
  3. Compare cohorts at the same age, not on the same calendar date.
  4. Flag sources whose cohorts complain or go inactive faster than average, and fix or cut them.
  5. Feed inactive subscribers into a re-engagement flow, then a sunset rule.

Which deliverability signals should you watch?

Watch mailbox-provider dashboards, authentication results, bounces, complaints and blocklists, and read them as trends rather than single readings. A sudden drop in clicks from one provider's domains is often the first visible sign.

  • Google Postmaster Tools: the Compliance status dashboard reports against Gmail's sender requirements, and the Spam rate dashboard shows the share of mail users mark as spam; Google says data is typically updated within 24 hours and may be missing for low-volume senders (checked 4 November 2026). Google says the Domain and IP Reputation dashboards are being retired in the new version.
  • Microsoft: domains sending 5,000 or more messages to Microsoft consumer services must pass SPF and DKIM and publish an aligned DMARC record (checked 4 November 2026). Watch for rejections in bounce logs.
  • Gmail bulk-sender rules: Google's FAQ requires one-click unsubscribe for marketing messages from bulk senders and treats failing to honor unsubscribes within 48 hours as a compliance failure (checked 4 November 2026).
  • Your own data: bounce rate by provider, DMARC aggregate reports, blocklist checks and clicks by mailbox provider.

What should weekly and monthly dashboards show?

The weekly view should catch deliverability problems early, and the monthly view should judge commercial return and list health. Keep both on one page so nobody reads revenue without the risk signals next to it.

What does a warning sign look like in practice?

The classic pattern is revenue holding steady while revenue per recipient falls and complaints creep up. That usually means the team is sending more to keep totals flat, which works only until inbox placement drops.

Consider a hypothetical store that doubles its campaign frequency. Attributed revenue rises in the first month, but its newest cohort unsubscribes faster than earlier ones and Gmail clicks fall. On the combined dashboard, the right move is to reduce frequency for low-engagement segments, not to celebrate the revenue line.

The question a skeptical reader should ask is whether holdouts and cohorts are worth the effort for a small list. The documentation explains the signals, but not how noisy they are at small volumes.

What else do teams ask about email measurement?

Most follow-up questions are about open rates, benchmarks and holdout size. The short answers follow.

Should I stop reporting open rates?

Not entirely, but treat them as a rough trend signal rather than a success metric, because privacy features can register opens that didn't happen. Use clicks, conversions and revenue per recipient for decisions.

Is there a good benchmark for email ROI?

Published benchmarks rarely match your margins, costs or attribution rules. Compare your own cohorts and holdout results over time instead.

How big should a holdout group be?

Big enough for the difference between groups to be stable over repeated periods. Small lists may need longer tests or a larger share held back.
