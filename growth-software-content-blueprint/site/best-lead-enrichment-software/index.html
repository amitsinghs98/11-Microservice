Which enrichment tool should you choose?

Pick Clay if someone on your team will own multi-provider waterfalls and per-field rules, and pick Apollo.io if you're a small sales team that wants enrichment, a contact database and sequencing on one bill. Choose a specialist (ZoomInfo, Cognism, People Data Labs) only when its coverage or delivery model matches a gap you can name.

For HubSpot-only stacks, HubSpot's native Breeze Intelligence is the simplest route. Lusha suits individual reps who want quick lookups, and FullEnrich suits teams that want waterfall contact finding without building it.

When does enrichment help, and when is it wasted?

Enrichment earns its cost when a downstream process needs fields that humans won't type reliably: routing, fit scoring, territories or outbound lists that need a verified email. It's wasted when the CRM has no agreed account model, when records are enriched once and never refreshed, or when nobody uses the fields.

Separate the layers before you shop. Firmographic data describes the company (headcount, industry, location), technographic data lists the software it appears to run, and person-level data covers role, email and phone. A waterfall tries several providers in turn for one field and stops at the first acceptable answer. Which tools are worth shortlisting?

Most teams should shortlist two: one orchestration or bundled tool and one specialist that covers their weakest segment. The blocks below give best-for and skip-it-if guidance so you can cut the list quickly.

Clay: best for teams that want to control every source

Clay works like a spreadsheet where each column can call a data provider, a formula or an AI research step, so you decide which provider fills each field and in what order.

Pricing model: tiered platform plans with usage credits; the Clay pricing page sets out what each plan meters and which plans include CRM sync. Strengths: flexible waterfalls and the option to bring your own provider API keys. Limitations: usage is harder to forecast than a flat seat price. Skip it if nobody will own the logic after launch. Our Clay vs Apollo breakdown goes deeper on the trade-off.

Apollo.io: best for small teams that want one bill

Apollo pairs its own people and company database with enrichment of CSV files, CRM records and API calls, plus sequencing.

Pricing model: per seat with credit allowances and a free tier; see the Apollo pricing page for current allowances and how many credits an email or phone lookup costs. Strengths: low entry cost and one place to find, enrich and contact. Limitations: mobile numbers consume allowances faster than emails, so phone-heavy teams outgrow entry plans quickly. Skip it if you already pay for a sequencer and only need enrichment. Our roundup of Apollo alternatives covers other routes.

ZoomInfo: best for depth from a single vendor

ZoomInfo is a broad sales intelligence platform covering firmographic, technographic, intent and org-chart data, with tools for keeping CRM records updated and enriching forms.

Pricing model: quote-based, usually on annual contracts; request figures through ZoomInfo's pricing page. Strengths: company-level breadth in one relationship. Limitations: a single database can't fall back to another source on a miss, and the contract suits larger teams. Skip it if you have a handful of reps and no admin; our list of ZoomInfo alternatives for SMB teams is the better starting point.

HubSpot Breeze Intelligence: best for HubSpot-only stacks

HubSpot acquired Clearbit and folded its company and contact enrichment into HubSpot under the Breeze Intelligence name. Enriched fields are available to forms, workflows and scoring without middleware.

Pricing model: tied to HubSpot subscriptions and HubSpot's credit system, which has been repackaged since the acquisition; check the Breeze Intelligence product page for what your tier includes today. Strengths: nothing to integrate. Limitations: it helps only inside HubSpot and offers no second source. Skip it if your CRM is Salesforce, Pipedrive or anything else.

Cognism: best for calling into Europe

Cognism sells person-level contact data with an emphasis on mobile numbers and European markets, delivered through a browser extension, web app and CRM integrations.

Pricing model: quote-based; see Cognism's pricing page. Strengths: a clear regional focus. Limitations: less suited to very small budgets, and coverage in other regions should be judged on your own sample. Skip it if nearly all your buyers are in North America and email is your only channel.

Lusha: best for quick rep lookups

Lusha provides emails, phone numbers and basic company details through a browser extension, a web app and CRM integrations.

Pricing model: credit tiers with a free plan; see Lusha pricing. Strengths: speed to value. Limitations: lighter on technographic depth and scheduled bulk enrichment than platform tools. Skip it if you need rules-based enrichment across tens of thousands of CRM records.

People Data Labs: best for builders

People Data Labs is a developer-first provider of person and company records through an API or bulk files.

Pricing model: usage tiers, including a free developer tier; see People Data Labs pricing. Strengths: warehouse-friendly and flexible. Limitations: writeback, deduplication and governance are entirely your job. Skip it if you don't have engineering time.

FullEnrich: best for waterfall contact finding with little setup

FullEnrich positions itself as an aggregator that queries several contact-data providers in sequence, giving you waterfall behaviour without building it.

Pricing model: credits; check FullEnrich pricing for how unsuccessful lookups are billed. Strengths: coverage with minimal configuration. Limitations: contact fields only, and you depend on upstream providers you don't contract with directly. Skip it if routing and scoring need company-level fields.

How many credits do 1,000 and 10,000 records use?

Under the assumptions below, a 1,000-record inbound job needs about 1,800 email-equivalent units and a 10,000-record outbound job between 16,000 and 26,000, depending almost entirely on how many phone numbers you request. Multiply by your vendor's real unit price to get a budget.

This is a hypothetical model, not vendor data. Assume an email lookup costs one unit and a mobile lookup eight (an illustrative ratio; check your vendor's), and every record gets an email lookup.

  • 1,000 inbound leads, phones for the top 10%: 1,000 email units plus 100 × 8 phone units = 1,800 units. Restricting phones to fit-qualified leads keeps it there; requesting phones for everyone would make it 9,000.
  • 10,000 outbound records, phones for 20%: 10,000 plus 2,000 × 8 = 26,000 units. Dropping phones to the 7.5% of records in tier-one accounts brings it to 16,000.

Two lessons hold whatever the vendor: request phones only for records that pass your fit rules, and ask whether a lookup that returns nothing is charged, because on a cold list that term can move the bill more than the headline price.

How accurate is enriched data in practice?

Accuracy varies by country, seniority, company size and industry, so a vendor's headline coverage claim tells you little about your own segment. The only reliable measure is a sample: run a couple of hundred records you already know are correct through each shortlisted tool and score fill rate and accuracy field by field.

How do you stop enrichment from corrupting the CRM?

Write a short field rulebook before you switch on automated writeback: who wins for each field, when an overwrite is allowed, and which fields enrichment may never touch. Then deduplicate before you enrich, because enriching duplicates doubles the bill and makes merges harder.

  • Source of truth per field. Decide whether rep, form or vendor data wins for title, headcount and industry.
  • Overwrite policy. Fill-blanks-only, overwrite-if-stale or never-overwrite, per field. Owner, lifecycle stage and opt-out status are never enrichment's to change.
  • Provenance. Store the source and a last-enriched date beside key fields.
  • Match keys. Match companies on normalised domain rather than name, and people on email first.
  • Quarantine. Send low-confidence results to a review view instead of live fields.

What mistakes should you avoid, and what should you ask vendors?

The common mistakes are enriching every record instead of fit-qualified ones, buying phone allowances nobody dials, and letting two tools write to the same field. Each one is cheaper to prevent with a contract question than to fix later.

  • Are we charged when a lookup returns nothing, or a result we reject?
  • Which fields can we lock against overwrite, and is there a change log?
  • Which sub-processors supply the data, and how are opt-out requests passed on?
  • What may we keep in our CRM after the contract ends?

What else do buyers ask about lead enrichment?

Most remaining questions concern overlap with contact databases, refresh cadence and compliance. Short answers follow.

Is lead enrichment the same as a contact database?

They overlap but do different jobs. A contact database is where you search for new people, while enrichment fills gaps in records you already hold, and many vendors sell both.

How often should records be re-enriched?

There's no universal interval. A sensible default is to refresh open-pipeline accounts quarterly, the rest of the database once or twice a year, and any record on demand when it enters a sequence.

Do small teams need a waterfall?

Only if one provider leaves important gaps in your segment. If a single source fills most fields correctly in your sample, a waterfall adds cost for little gain.

Is enriched data automatically GDPR-compliant?

No. You remain responsible for your lawful basis, transparency and handling objections, so review each vendor's data processing terms with your own counsel.

What's the verdict?

In our editorial assessment, Clay wins when someone will own the logic, and Apollo.io is the practical default for small teams that also need outreach.
