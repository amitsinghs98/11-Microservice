Which CRO tool should you choose?

Start with free analytics and a free heatmap tool, then add a specialist only where the evidence points. Buy an experimentation platform last, when you have both a written hypothesis and enough visitors to finish a test in weeks rather than months.

  • Finding which page leaks: Google Analytics 4, if your conversion events are set up properly.
  • Seeing behavior with no budget: Microsoft Clarity.
  • Watching behavior and asking visitors why: Hotjar.
  • Lead-gen pages where the form is the conversion: Zuko.
  • Low-traffic pages that need message testing: Lyssna.
  • Paid-media teams building and testing their own pages: Unbounce.
  • Research and testing under one contract: VWO.
  • Enterprise experimentation and personalization: Optimizely.

How does the CRO workflow run from problem to test?

Conversion work moves through four stages: find the leak with analytics, understand it with qualitative research, write a hypothesis, then test or ship the fix. Each stage needs a different kind of tool, and buying for the wrong stage is where most CRO budgets disappear.

  1. Find the leak. Analytics shows which landing pages, sources and devices underperform. Without it you are guessing where to look.
  2. Understand why. Heatmaps, session replay, form analytics and on-page surveys show what visitors do and say on that page. Our guide to heatmap and session replay software for conversion research goes deeper on this step.
  3. Write the hypothesis. A workable format is: "Because we saw X, we believe changing Y for audience Z will move metric M."
  4. Test or ship. Run a controlled experiment when traffic allows. When it doesn't, ship the change, watch it, and treat the result as directional.

Testing and analytics are different jobs. Analytics describes what happened; an experiment isolates the effect of one change by randomly splitting visitors. If statistics, server-side tests or feature flags are your main question, our comparison of A/B testing platforms for SaaS and ecommerce covers that ground, and this page stays focused on the landing page itself.

One last stage is easy to skip: judge the result downstream. A lift in form fills that produces weaker leads isn't a win, which is why a realistic approach to attribution for small marketing teams belongs in the plan from the start.

What does each tool do well, and where does it fall short?

Each tool below earns its place at one stage of the workflow and is weaker outside it. Read the "skip it if" line first, because that's usually where the buying decision is made.

Google Analytics 4: best for finding which page leaks

Best for: any team that needs landing-page conversion rates by source, campaign and device. Skip it if: you want a lightweight, cookie-light setup, in which case our roundup of privacy-conscious web analytics is the better route.

GA4 is event-based, so landing-page conversion rates are only as good as your key-event setup and UTM discipline. Explorations can isolate a single page's funnel. The CRO limitation is that it reports what happened without showing why. Google offers a free standard version and a paid enterprise product; see the official Google Analytics site for current terms.

Microsoft Clarity: best free heatmaps and replay

Best for: teams and agencies that want click maps, scroll maps and recordings on every page without a new budget line. Skip it if: you need on-page surveys, marketer-friendly funnels or formal support commitments.

Based on Microsoft's product information, Clarity provides heatmaps, session recordings and automatic flags for behaviors such as rage clicks and dead clicks, and it can connect to GA4. You still own masking and consent configuration. Review the Microsoft Clarity site and its privacy documentation before installing it on pages that collect personal data.

Hotjar: best for behavior plus visitor feedback

Best for: marketers who want heatmaps, recordings and on-page surveys from one vendor. Skip it if: heatmaps are all you need, because a free tool may cover that.

Hotjar, now part of Contentsquare, pairs observation with asking visitors directly, which helps when a replay shows hesitation but not the reason. Paid plans scale with sessions captured, so busy pages may be sampled unless you upgrade; check allowances on the Hotjar pricing page. If you're already weighing a move, see our Hotjar alternatives breakdown.

Zuko: best for lead-gen forms

Best for: B2B and lead-gen pages where the form is the conversion. Skip it if: your page converts on a single button or an email-only field.

Zuko specializes in field-level form behavior: which field people abandon on, how long they spend in each, and which fields trigger corrections. It does one job, so you still need analytics and a way to test changes. Plans are on the Zuko website.

Lyssna: best for pre-testing messages on low traffic

Best for: low-traffic pages choosing between headlines, hero layouts or value propositions. Skip it if: you can run live tests quickly, or your buyers are too niche for a general panel.

Lyssna (formerly UsabilityHub) runs unmoderated research such as five-second, first-click and preference tests. It measures panel reactions, not real visitor behavior, so use it to filter ideas rather than prove a lift. Panel recruitment is generally priced separately from the subscription; see the Lyssna pricing page.

Unbounce: best for marketers who build and test their own pages

Best for: paid-media teams launching many campaign pages with built-in A/B testing. Skip it if: your landing pages must live inside your main CMS or store theme.

Unbounce combines a drag-and-drop builder with A/B testing and Smart Traffic, an AI feature that routes each visitor to the variant predicted to suit them. That makes it a light form of personalization, but it optimizes allocation rather than giving you a clean test readout, and testing only applies to pages Unbounce hosts. Plans are tiered; see the Unbounce pricing page, or compare builders in our guide to landing page builders for paid campaigns.

VWO: best for research and testing under one contract

Best for: mid-market teams that want heatmaps, recordings, surveys, form analytics and testing in one platform. Skip it if: you'd run a handful of tests a year and use few of the modules.

VWO sells testing, behavior insights, personalization and server-side experimentation as separate products, so you can start with one. Its visual editor handles simple variants, while complex changes still need a developer. Pricing is modular and tied to traffic, so model your monthly visitors before a sales call using the VWO pricing page.

Optimizely: best for enterprise experimentation and personalization

Best for: large organizations running many concurrent experiments with dedicated developers and analysts. Skip it if: you're a small team running a few landing-page tests a quarter.

Optimizely covers web experimentation, feature experimentation and personalization, with a statistics engine built for monitoring results as they arrive. It's sold through sales, typically on annual contracts. Start at the Optimizely website.

How much traffic does each kind of tool need?

Research tools are useful on modest traffic, because a few hundred recordings of one page can expose obvious friction. Testing tools need far more, since detecting a real difference requires a large sample per variant.

As illustrative arithmetic using the standard two-proportion sample-size formula (95% confidence, 80% power), a page converting at 4% needs roughly 6,700 visitors per variant to detect a lift to 5%. A page with 3,000 visitors a month would need about four and a half months to finish a two-variant test. That's a calculation, not vendor data, and your testing tool's own calculator should take precedence.

Pricing follows the same split. Analytics and replay tools tend to price by sessions or pageviews, testing platforms by monthly tracked users or visitors, page builders by plan tier, and research panels per participant. A fair budget comparison counts the traffic you'll actually send through each tool, and our marketing analytics software shortlist covers the reporting layer that sits above them.

What should you fix first on a landing page?

Fix tracking, message match and form friction before you test anything, because they're cheap to repair and often matter more than any variant. Work down the list in order and only commit traffic to an experiment at the end.

  1. Tracking works. Each conversion fires once, and UTMs follow one naming convention.
  2. Message match. The headline repeats the promise of the ad or email that sent the visitor. If you draft variants with AI, our review of AI copywriting software for ads and landing pages covers the editing they need.
  3. Speed and mobile layout. The offer and call to action load fast and are visible on a phone.
  4. One clear action. Competing links and secondary offers are removed or demoted.
  5. Form friction. Every field is justified, errors are specific, and abandonment is measured by field.
  6. Proof near the decision. Genuine, permissioned testimonials or logos sit close to the call to action.
  7. Objections answered. Price, commitment and data-use questions are handled on the page.
  8. Behavior reviewed. Heatmaps and replays checked for rage clicks, dead clicks and drop-off.
  9. Visitors asked. A short on-page or exit survey asks what stopped them.
  10. Biggest hypothesis tested. Only now does the page get a live experiment.

What mistakes do CRO buyers make?

The three common mistakes are buying a testing platform for a page without enough traffic, testing on pages with broken tracking, and declaring winners on form fills without checking lead quality. Each wastes months, and all three are avoidable with the checklist above.

Before you sign, ask each vendor these questions:

  • What counts toward the usage limit: sessions, pageviews, tracked users or tested visitors?
  • What happens at the limit: sampling, overage charges or a forced upgrade?
  • How are form inputs and personal data masked by default?
  • Does the testing script cause flicker, and how is it prevented?
  • Can results flow to our analytics and CRM?

What else do buyers ask about landing-page CRO?

These are the questions that come up once a team has a shortlist. The short answers favor research before testing, and privacy settings before installation.

Do I need an A/B testing tool to do CRO?

No. Many of the most valuable fixes come from repairing tracking, tightening message match and removing form friction, all found through research. Testing earns its cost once you have hypotheses and enough traffic to measure them.

Is a free heatmap tool good enough for landing pages?

Often, yes, for observing behavior on a handful of pages. Paid tools add surveys, richer filtering, integrations and support, which matter more as the number of pages and stakeholders grows.

Can I run CRO on a page with very low traffic?

Yes, but lean on research and pre-testing instead of live experiments. Make larger, evidence-based changes, monitor conversion over time and treat the outcome as directional rather than proven.

How do privacy rules affect CRO tools?

Replay and heatmap scripts can capture personal data. Configure masking, respect your consent banner, exclude sensitive pages and review each vendor's data-processing terms with your legal adviser.

What's the verdict?

Our editorial assessment: buy for the stage you're stuck on, not the stage that sounds most advanced. Most landing-page teams get further with GA4, a replay tool and a disciplined checklist than with an enterprise testing contract.

Add Zuko or Lyssna when forms or messaging are the bottleneck, and move to Unbounce, VWO or Optimizely once traffic and team capacity can support real experiments.
