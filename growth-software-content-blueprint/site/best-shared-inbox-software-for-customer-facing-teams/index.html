Which shared inbox should a customer-facing team choose?

Front is a strong candidate for teams that run many aliases across support, sales and operations with rules and SLAs, while Hiver suits teams that want to stay inside Gmail. Help Scout fits support-led teams that may want a knowledge base and chat later, Missive fits small teams that also want shared chat, and a native Google or Microsoft shared mailbox is enough only at low volume.

  • Many aliases, rules and SLAs across departments: Front.
  • Teams who want to work in Gmail without a new app: Hiver.
  • Support-led teams wanting docs and chat on the same platform: Help Scout.
  • Small teams mixing shared email and team chat: Missive.
  • Sales and service working from the same CRM record: HubSpot's inbox.
  • Very small teams on Google Workspace with low volume: Google Groups collaborative inbox.
  • Very small teams on Microsoft 365 with low volume: Outlook shared mailbox.

How is a shared inbox different from a ticketing suite?

Shared inbox software lets a team work a common email address as a group, typically with assignment, internal notes, collision warnings and tags, while the customer still sees an ordinary email conversation. A ticketing suite adds structured tickets, customer portals, complex routing and deep reporting, which help larger support teams but add setup and cost.

That distinction matters because shared inboxes serve more than support. Sales teams work inbound@ and partner aliases, finance works billing@ and accounts payable, and operations works vendor and logistics threads. A full help desk can feel heavy for those teams, and it often bills every member as a support agent.

The line blurs as tools grow, with some shared inboxes adding SLAs and reporting and some help desks adding inbox-style views. Which shared inbox features matter most when you shortlist?

Assignment, collision detection and internal notes are the minimum; SLAs, audit trails and integrations decide whether the tool scales past a handful of people. Templates and tags are table stakes in every dedicated tool.

  • Assignment: manual, round-robin and rule-based, with a clear owner on every open conversation.
  • Collision detection: a visible warning when a teammate is viewing or drafting a reply.
  • Internal notes and mentions: discussion on the email thread itself, not in a separate chat.
  • Tags: consistent categories for reporting, ideally applied by rules.
  • SLAs: targets and breach alerts by inbox, customer tier or tag.
  • Templates: shared, owned and versioned, with variables for names and account details.
  • Integrations: CRM, billing and project tools, so context appears beside the email.
  • Audit trails: who was assigned, who replied and when, especially for finance aliases.
  • Per-user pricing: how the vendor treats occasional users and whether every alias needs its own seat.

Will a shared inbox really stop duplicate and missed replies?

Dedicated tools are designed around assignment and visibility features meant to prevent both, but they only work if every message has an owner and the team agrees on when to assign. We can't measure how much any tool reduces errors from public materials alone.

How should you route several aliases in one shared inbox?

Give each alias its own inbox with a named owning team, route by rules on alias, sender and keywords, and keep one triage view for anything the rules can't place. Review the rules monthly, because a routing rule nobody owns becomes a hiding place for missed email.

A hypothetical design for a 15-person company with five aliases:

  1. support@ goes to the support inbox, round-robin among on-shift agents, with an SLA by customer tier taken from the CRM.
  2. sales@ and partners@ go to the sales inbox; known contacts route to the account owner, new senders to a rotating rep.
  3. billing@ goes to a restricted finance inbox, visible only to finance and the support lead, with every action in the audit trail.
  4. ops@ goes to operations, tagged by vendor, with no SLA but a weekly unresolved-thread review.
  5. Catch-all triage: anything matching no rule lands in a shared view the support lead clears daily.

When a conversation crosses teams, reassign it rather than forwarding, so the history stays in one thread. What else do buyers ask about shared inbox software?

The usual questions are about native mailboxes, occasional users, departments sharing one tool and migrating old email. Short answers follow; confirm current details with each vendor.

Is a Google Group or Microsoft 365 shared mailbox enough?

For two or three people handling low volume, often yes. You'll miss reliable assignment, collision warnings, internal notes on the email itself and response-time reporting, which is usually the point at which teams move to a dedicated tool.

Do customers see that we're using a shared inbox?

Generally no. Replies go out from the shared address, such as support@ or billing@, and internal notes and assignments stay internal, but check how each tool handles signatures and reply-from settings.

Can one shared inbox tool serve sales, support and finance?

Yes, if it supports separate inboxes or teams with their own permissions and rules. Keep finance and HR aliases restricted to the people who need them, and check that the audit trail shows who viewed and replied.

How do shared inbox tools charge for occasional users?

Seat rules differ by vendor, and we couldn't confirm them for this draft. Check each vendor's definition of a billable user carefully, because a manager who sometimes replies may need a full seat.

What should we do with old email when we switch?

Decide whether you need history in the new tool or only going forward. Ask each vendor whether and how far back it can import mail from a connected account, and whether tags and notes carry over, before you commit.

What's the verdict on shared inbox software?

Front is the one to check first for multi-department teams that need rules, SLAs and audit trails across many aliases, and Hiver for teams who want shared-inbox features without leaving Gmail. Help Scout suits support-led teams, Missive suits small teams who talk through email together, and HubSpot's inbox makes sense when HubSpot is already your CRM.

Start with a native shared mailbox only if volume is low and nobody asks for response-time reports, and move up once missed or duplicated replies appear.
