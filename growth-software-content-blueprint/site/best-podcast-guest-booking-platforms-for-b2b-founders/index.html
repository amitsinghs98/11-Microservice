Which podcast guest booking platform should a B2B founder choose?

If you need to find shows, start with a podcast database such as Rephonic, Listen Notes or Podchaser; if you want hosts who are already looking for guests, try a matching marketplace such as PodMatch or Matchmaker.fm. Then use a scheduler like Calendly or SavvyCal for bookings and a simple CRM to track every host relationship.

No single product does all three jobs well for a founder. Treat booking as a small pipeline: research, pitch, schedule, record, follow up.

  • Researching shows and audiences: Rephonic
  • Searching a large podcast index or API: Listen Notes
  • Show credits and guest histories: Podchaser
  • Two-sided guest and host matching: PodMatch
  • Browsing host requests for guests: Matchmaker.fm
  • Guest-request newsletters: PodcastGuests.com
  • Booking the recording slot: Calendly or SavvyCal

Do guest marketplaces actually get founders onto shows their buyers hear?

Sometimes, but marketplaces tend to concentrate on shows that actively seek guests, which isn't the same as shows with a large or relevant audience. The product information we reviewed explains how matching works, not how often a match turns into a qualified listener, so treat every match as a lead to research rather than a booking to accept.

What does each platform do well, and where does it fall short?

The research tools are strongest when you already know your buyer and want a defensible target list. The marketplaces are strongest when you want speed and are willing to filter hard.

Rephonic: best for researching shows before you pitch

Rephonic is a podcast research database built around show profiles, audience estimates and contact details for hosts. It suits founders who want a ranked, researched list before writing a single pitch. Pricing is a subscription; check the current tiers and what each includes on its pricing page. The limitation is that audience numbers on any third-party database are estimates, so treat them as directional. Skip it if you'll only pitch a handful of shows you already know.

Listen Notes: best for searching the long tail of shows

Listen Notes is a podcast search engine with an API, useful for finding episodes by topic, keyword or past guest name. Searching for a competitor's founder, for example, surfaces shows that already cover your space. It's a search tool first, so outreach details and CRM features are thin. Skip it if you want booking help rather than research.

Podchaser: best for guest and host credit histories

Podchaser catalogs podcasts with credits for hosts and guests, which helps you see who interviewed people like you. It's useful for spotting shows that regularly book B2B operators. Check which audience and contact data each plan includes before relying on it. Skip it if you need a matching service rather than a database.

PodMatch: best for two-sided matching with opted-in hosts

PodMatch matches guest profiles with host profiles, so the hosts you're shown have chosen to receive guests. That cuts cold outreach. The trade-off is that the pool favors shows actively looking for guests, which can skew toward newer or smaller programs. Skip it if your buyers listen to a short list of established shows that don't use marketplaces.

Matchmaker.fm: best for browsing open guest requests

Matchmaker.fm is a marketplace where hosts post what they're looking for and guests respond, which suits founders who prefer to answer briefs rather than pitch cold. Quality control falls to you, because shows vary widely in size and consistency. Skip it if you don't have time to vet each request.

PodcastGuests.com: best for low-effort inbound opportunities

PodcastGuests.com circulates host requests and guest listings, which can produce opportunities with little setup. Expect volume over precision; few requests will match a specific B2B niche. Skip it if you want a curated shortlist.

Calendly and SavvyCal: best for booking the recording itself

Calendly and SavvyCal send hosts a booking link instead of trading times by email. Use intake questions to collect the show name, recording platform and topic, and buffers to avoid stacking interviews. Check each vendor's pricing page for the current plans, and confirm which plan includes the custom questions or routing you need. Neither helps you find shows. How do you handle recording handoff and host relationships?

Let the host choose the recording platform, and send a one-page guest kit with your bio, headshot, topics and microphone setup before the call. Log every show, contact, recording date, publish date and follow-up in one CRM so relationships outlast the episode.

Many hosts already use a remote recorder such as Riverside or Zencastr, so you rarely need to buy one as a guest; just test your audio on their link. A free CRM is enough to start, and our shortlist of CRM software for small businesses compares entry-level options. Track three stages: pitched, booked and published, plus whether the episode earned a mention in a sales conversation.

After the episode goes live, share it, thank the host and offer an introduction to another guest. That turns a one-off booking into a referral source, which no marketplace can automate.

How do you vet relevance and avoid pay-to-play traps?

Vet every show on audience fit, publishing consistency and past guests before you accept, and treat any request for payment as sponsorship rather than an interview. Pay-to-play isn't automatically bad, but it should be a conscious paid media decision with its own budget and evidence.

  • Audience fit: do past guests and topics match your buyers' job titles and problems?
  • Consistency: has the show published regularly over recent months, or is it stalling?
  • Distribution: does the host promote episodes on LinkedIn, a newsletter or YouTube, or only the feed?
  • Payment requests: if a fee is required, ask for listener evidence and disclose the arrangement internally.
  • Rights: confirm you can reuse clips and transcripts in your own content.

What mistakes do founders make with podcast guesting?

The common mistakes are pitching on audience size alone, sending the same generic pitch everywhere, and never following up after the episode airs. Each one wastes the slot you worked to book.

  • Buying a database and a marketplace and a booking agency at once, then using none of them consistently.
  • Pitching a product demo instead of a story or a contrarian lesson the host's audience can use.
  • Letting bookings live in an inbox, so nobody knows which shows were promised a follow-up.
  • Measuring downloads instead of whether prospects mention the episode.

What else do founders ask about booking podcast guest spots?

Most questions come down to fit, cost and time. The short answers below assume a B2B founder pitching shows, not a host booking guests.

Should a founder pay to appear on a podcast?

Paying a show to appear is a sponsorship, not an interview, and it should be judged like one. Ask for audience evidence, label it internally as paid media, and expect a weaker editorial endorsement than an earned booking.

How many podcast pitches should a founder send each month?

There's no reliable benchmark, so start with a small batch of well-researched pitches and track replies. Volume without research tends to land on shows that don't fit and burns your name with hosts.

Is a guest marketplace better than cold email to hosts?

A marketplace gives you hosts who have opted in to receive guests, which may make replies more likely. Cold email reaches shows that aren't on any marketplace, which often includes the niche B2B programs your buyers listen to.

Can a podcast booking agency replace these tools?

An agency can handle research, pitching and scheduling for you, usually on a retainer. Ask which shows they've placed clients on recently, and keep the relationship records in your own CRM so you don't lose them when the contract ends.

What's the verdict on podcast guest booking platforms?

For most B2B founders, one research database plus a free scheduler and CRM is enough; add a matching marketplace only if you need volume and can vet hard. The marketplaces save time on outreach, but research decides whether the shows you land actually reach buyers.
