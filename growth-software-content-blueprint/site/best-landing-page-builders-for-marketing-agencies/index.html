Which landing page builder should an agency choose?

Agencies that sell conversion work on paid traffic usually do best with Unbounce or Instapage, while agencies producing many smaller client pages on tight margins should look at Landingi or Swipe Pages. Duda suits agencies that want to resell white-labeled websites, Webflow suits design-led agencies that hand over complete sites, and Leadpages suits small shops serving local businesses.

  • Performance agencies running constant tests for paid clients: Unbounce.
  • Agencies needing approvals, personalization and team governance: Instapage.
  • High page volume across many client domains: Landingi.
  • Mobile-first paid social work: Swipe Pages.
  • Agencies reselling websites under their own brand: Duda.
  • Design-led agencies that hand over full sites: Webflow.
  • Small agencies serving owner-run businesses: Leadpages.

What does an agency need that an in-house team doesn't?

An agency needs to separate clients cleanly, reuse its own templates across them, give each client the right level of access, and hand pages or accounts over without rebuilding them. Pricing also has to work across many clients, so per-client domain and page allowances matter more than editor features.

In practice that means checking six things: how clients are separated (sub-accounts, workspaces or separate accounts), whether templates can be copied between clients, how many custom domains each plan allows, whether clients can view or approve without editing, whether the product can be white-labeled, and how a page or account transfers to the client when the engagement ends. These details are rarely on the headline pricing table, so ask for them in writing.

What is each builder best for, and when should you skip it?

Unbounce, Instapage and Landingi are the strongest pure landing page tools for agencies, Duda and Webflow are better when the deliverable is a whole site, and Swipe Pages and Leadpages fit narrower client types. Product descriptions come from vendor materials; fit judgments are our editorial assessment.

Unbounce: best for performance agencies that test constantly

What it does: Unbounce offers a drag-and-drop builder, A/B testing, AI-assisted features, popups and sticky bars; ask how it separates client accounts. Plans are tiered by usage; see Unbounce pricing.

Strengths: A strong fit when the agency sells conversion improvements and needs to show test results to each client.

Limitations: Usage-based tiers mean costs climb with client traffic, so price the roster, not one client. Skip it if your clients rarely test or you mostly build simple brochure pages.

Instapage: best for governed agency teams

What it does: Instapage provides ad-to-page personalization, experimentation, heatmaps, and collaboration with comments and approvals; ask how client workspaces are structured. Check the Instapage plans page.

Strengths: Commenting and approval flows suit agencies where account managers, designers and clients all review pages.

Limitations: Higher tiers are sales-led, which makes the cost of a growing roster harder to predict. Skip it if you're a small agency with a few low-traffic clients.

Landingi: best for many pages and client domains

What it does: Landingi combines a page builder with A/B tests and popups, with plan allowances for traffic and domains; see Landingi pricing and ask about agency options.

Strengths: Plan structure is built around traffic and domain allowances, which suits agencies publishing many pages across many client domains if the allowances fit your roster.

Limitations: Allowances vary widely by plan, so model your full client roster before choosing a tier. Skip it if you need deep enterprise governance or advanced personalization.

Swipe Pages: best for mobile-first paid social

What it does: Swipe Pages focuses on fast, mobile-oriented pages and A/B testing; see Swipe Pages pricing for agency options.

Strengths: Suits agencies whose clients buy paid social, where most clicks arrive on phones.

Limitations: Smaller ecosystem and fewer advanced features. Skip it if most client traffic is desktop B2B search.

Duda: best for white-labeled client websites

What it does: Duda is a website builder built for agencies and SaaS platforms, with white-label options, client management, permissions and templates; see Duda pricing.

Strengths: Agencies can resell sites under their own brand and control exactly what each client may edit.

Limitations: It's website-first, so testing-heavy campaign work fits better in a dedicated landing page tool. Skip it if your service is paid campaign pages rather than client websites.

Webflow: best for design-led agencies

What it does: Webflow is a visual builder with CMS, components and hosting, priced by site plans and workspace seats, with workspace features for agencies and client handover.

Strengths: Agencies can build fully custom, on-brand pages on the client's main site and transfer the site at handover.

Limitations: Testing may need an extra product or separate tool, and the editor is a learning curve for clients. Skip it if you need dozens of cheap campaign variants per client per month.

Leadpages: best for small agencies and local clients

What it does: Leadpages offers templates, a drag-and-drop editor, forms, popups, alert bars and simple sites; see Leadpages pricing.

Strengths: Simple enough that some clients can edit their own pages after handover.

Limitations: Client management and testing are lighter than the other campaign tools here. Skip it if you run structured experimentation programs for clients.

Do agency features really save production time across clients?

Vendors document sub-accounts, templates and approvals, but the time saved depends on whether the agency has a standard template library, a naming convention and an approval process before the tool arrives. A builder speeds up a defined workflow; it won't create one.

What does a repeatable agency landing page workflow look like?

A repeatable workflow moves every page through the same steps: brief, template, build, tracking, client approval, launch, reporting and handover. Each step should have an owner and a checklist, so quality doesn't depend on who built the page.

  1. Brief: offer, audience, ad source, primary conversion and the CRM or inbox that receives leads.
  2. Template: start from the agency's tested template library, not a blank page.
  3. Build and tracking: connect the form to the client's CRM, add consent, and set UTM and conversion tracking.
  4. Client approval: share a preview link and capture approval in one place; tools from our guide to creative proofing software for client approvals help when builders lack it.
  5. Launch QA: test lead delivery, mobile speed and the custom domain before ad spend starts.
  6. Reporting: report conversions in the client's dashboard; see our list of white-label reporting platforms.
  7. Handover: agree in the contract whether pages, domains and data transfer to the client or stay with the agency.

Many agencies also centralize briefs, approvals and files in one of the tools from our guide to client portal software for agencies.

What does landing page software cost an agency at 5 and 20 clients?

Exact figures couldn't be verified for this draft, but the cost drivers are consistent: the number of client accounts or workspaces, custom domains, published pages, and traffic or conversion caps. Model all four at 5 and 20 clients, because a plan that is cheap at five clients can need a higher tier or agency plan at twenty.

  • At 5 clients: a mid-tier plan on most builders may cover the roster; check domain count and whether each client needs a separate account.
  • At 20 clients: agency, enterprise or per-client add-on pricing usually applies, and traffic caps start to bind on paid clients.
  • Pass-through or bundled: decide whether clients pay for their own account or the cost is built into your retainer, and price the handover either way.

What else do agencies ask about landing page software?

Most questions are about white-labeling, ownership and moving between tools. Short answers follow.

Can I white-label a landing page builder for clients?

Some builders offer white-label options, and website platforms such as Duda are built around them. Confirm exactly what is rebranded, such as the editor, login and emails, and on which plan.

Should clients own their landing page builder account?

Client ownership makes handover simple and keeps data with the client. Agency ownership gives you control and simpler billing, but agree in the contract what transfers when the engagement ends.

How do agencies keep client pages separate?

Use sub-accounts or workspaces per client where the builder offers them, with separate domains, forms and integrations. Avoid sharing one CRM connection or tracking ID across clients.

Can I move pages between builders?

Rarely cleanly. Most builders don't export pages in a format another builder can import, so plan to rebuild, and keep copy, images and tracking details in your own files.
