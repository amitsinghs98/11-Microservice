Best Client Communication Platforms for Agency Teams

Client communication software for agencies works when every message, approval and decision lands somewhere searchable that the whole account team can see. Which client communication platform should an agency choose?

Match the platform to where your clients already communicate, then add a project record that captures approvals and decisions. The wrong choice is a tool your clients never open.

  • Clients who already use Slack and want fast back-and-forth: Slack with Slack Connect channels.
  • Corporate clients standardised on Microsoft 365: Microsoft Teams with guest access or shared channels.
  • Calm, project-centred communication for small agencies: Basecamp.
  • Email-first clients where the team must share one inbox: Front or Missive.
  • Agencies that want messages tied to tasks, time and budgets: Teamwork.com.
  • Replacing status meetings with short recorded walkthroughs: Loom, alongside one of the above.

What problem does client communication software solve?

It stops client knowledge living in one account manager's inbox. When messages, approvals and status sit in a shared, searchable place, anyone on the team can pick up the account and nobody re-asks a question already answered.

Agency communication typically fragments across email, chat, comments in a project tool, feedback on documents and calls with no notes. Each is fine alone. The cost appears when a client says "we approved the other version" and the evidence sits in a personal inbox, or a key contact leaves and their history goes with them.

Communication tools solve the routing problem, not the discipline problem. You still need rules about where approvals go and who owns each thread, which the charter later on this page covers.

What does each platform do well, and where does it fall short?

Each tool is strong in one mode of communication and weak in the others. Choose the primary channel first, then decide how decisions get recorded outside it.

Slack: best for real-time shared channels

Slack positions Slack Connect as a way for your organisation and a client's to share a channel while each keeps its own workspace; check which plans include it. That suits clients who expect quick answers and already use Slack internally. Fast chat buries decisions, so pin approvals or log them elsewhere. Skip it if your clients are email-first and would treat chat as an always-on hotline. See the Slack pricing page.

Microsoft Teams: best for Microsoft 365 clients

Teams offers external collaboration options such as guest access, which makes it the natural choice when a corporate client runs on Microsoft 365. The catch is that the client's IT policies often decide what external users can do. Skip it if your agency and clients mostly use other tools. Check plans on the Microsoft Teams plans page.

Basecamp: best for calm, project-centred communication

Basecamp is positioned around organising messages, to-dos and files by project, with client access to what you choose to share. It encourages fewer, longer updates instead of constant pings. Check that it connects to the other tools you rely on. Skip it if clients want instant messaging. See the Basecamp pricing page.

Front: best for accountable client email

Front positions itself around turning shared email addresses into a team inbox where each conversation can be assigned, so every client email has an owner. It suits account teams where email is still the client's main channel. Clients see ordinary email, which keeps adoption friction low. Skip it if most client talk happens in chat. See the Front pricing page.

Missive: best for small teams that share inboxes and chat

Missive positions itself around shared email with team chat alongside conversations, which helps small agencies discuss a client email without forwarding it. Confirm its reporting and rules features if you track response times closely. Skip it if your team doesn't share client inboxes. See the Missive pricing page.

Teamwork.com: best for tying messages to delivery

Teamwork.com is positioned as agency project management in which clients can be invited to collaborate inside your project tool, close to delivery tracking. Communication sits next to the work it's about. Clients have to log in to your tool, which some won't. Skip it if a client refuses another login. See the Teamwork.com pricing page.

Loom: best for replacing status meetings

Loom is positioned around recording screen and camera walkthroughs that clients watch in their own time. It's useful for report reviews and creative feedback. It isn't a system of record, so link each video from the relevant task or decision. Skip it if clients prefer written updates. See the Loom pricing page.

What should a client communication charter say?

A charter is a one-page agreement, signed off at kickoff, that says which channel is used for what, how fast you respond and where decisions are recorded. It turns vague expectations into rules you can point to.

  1. Channels: chat for quick questions, email or the project tool for requests, the project tool for approvals.
  2. Response times: target reply windows for normal and urgent messages, and working hours.
  3. Owners: a named account lead and a named client approver, with deputies.
  4. Approvals: what counts as approval and where it must be recorded.
  5. Decision log: what's logged, who logs it and where it lives.
  6. Status updates: cadence and format, for example a weekly written update and a monthly report.
  7. Escalation: who to contact when something is late or wrong.

Won't clients just ignore the channel rules?

Some will, especially senior stakeholders who email whoever they know. Based on the product information reviewed, no tool enforces behaviour, so the account lead has to redirect messages politely and log decisions wherever they arrive.

Which integrations and controls matter most?

Prioritise links between your communication tool and your project tool, so a client request becomes a task without copy-and-paste. Then check notification controls and searchable history, because those decide whether the tool stays usable after six months.

What's the verdict?

Use Slack or Teams when clients live in chat, Front or Missive when they live in email, and Basecamp or Teamwork.com when you want conversation tied to the project. Whichever you choose, record approvals and decisions in one place the whole team can search.

What else do agencies ask about client communication tools?

Most follow-up questions concern channel sprawl, after-hours expectations and keeping records. These answers are general, so confirm current features on each vendor's official pages.

Should we use the client's chat tool or ours?

Use whichever the client's decision-makers actually read, then mirror decisions into your own project record. If you join a client's workspace, you don't control retention or access, so keep your own copy of approvals.

How do we stop clients expecting instant replies in chat?

Put response times and working hours in a written communication charter agreed at kickoff, and set channel notifications accordingly. Then keep to those times consistently, including when you could reply faster.

Is email still acceptable for client approvals?

Yes, if the approval is specific, dated and stored where the team can find it. A shared inbox or a project tool that captures email makes this far more reliable than individual inboxes.

What is a decision log?

It's a running list of what was decided, by whom, when and where the evidence lives. It settles scope disputes quickly because nobody has to search old threads.

Do we need a separate tool for video updates?

Not necessarily, but short recorded walkthroughs can replace meetings for report reviews and creative feedback. Store the links with the related task or decision so they stay findable.
