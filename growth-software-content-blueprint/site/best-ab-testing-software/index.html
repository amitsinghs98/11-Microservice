Best A/B Testing Software for SaaS and Ecommerce

Choosing A/B (or AB) testing software comes down to where your changes live: storefront themes and marketing pages suit visual, client-side tools, while pricing logic, onboarding and app features suit server-side platforms with feature flags. Traffic decides whether you should be testing at all.

Which A/B testing platform should you choose?

Pick by who will own experiments: marketers need a visual editor, engineers need SDKs and flags, and data teams want analysis on their own warehouse. Shopify brands testing prices and shipping have a specialist option of their own.

  • Marketer-led web testing at mid-market scale: VWO or Convert.
  • Experimentation plus personalization for larger brands: AB Tasty or Optimizely.
  • Product teams that want flags and experiments together: Statsig or PostHog.
  • Results computed in your own data warehouse: GrowthBook.
  • Shopify stores testing price, shipping and offers: Intelligems.

Do you have enough traffic to run A/B tests?

Probably not for small lifts on low-volume conversions, and that is the most important thing to check before you buy. The sample you need depends on your baseline conversion rate and the smallest lift worth detecting, and halving that lift roughly quadruples the sample.

As illustrative arithmetic with the standard two-proportion formula at 95% confidence and 80% power: at a 3% baseline, detecting a 20% relative lift (3.0% to 3.6%) needs about 13,900 visitors per variant, while a 10% lift (3.0% to 3.3%) needs about 53,200. These are calculations, not vendor data. A SaaS pricing page with 5,000 visitors a month can't realistically detect small changes in paid conversion.

Low-traffic teams have options: test bolder changes, use a metric closer to the change (clicks to sign-up rather than paid plans), run fewer variants, or skip live tests and use research. Our landing-page CRO toolkit covers that research-first route.

Two more sample-size traps. Stopping a test the moment it looks significant inflates false positives unless the platform uses sequential or Bayesian methods built for continuous monitoring. And slicing results across many metrics or segments makes a spurious winner more likely, so ask each vendor how its statistics handle both.

Should you test client-side or server-side?

Test client-side when marketers own the roadmap and the changes are visual; test server-side when the change lives in pricing, checkout logic, onboarding or a mobile app. Many platforms now offer both, so the real question is which mode your team will use every week.

Client-side tools alter the page in the browser with a JavaScript snippet, usually through a visual editor. They're quick to launch, but they can flicker (the original showing briefly before the variant), they're affected by ad blockers and consent choices, and they struggle with server logic. Server-side testing assigns variants in your code through an SDK or feature flags, avoids flicker, and costs developer time for every experiment. For how flags and experiments divide the work, see our explainer on feature flags versus A/B testing.

Which guardrail metrics protect a test?

A guardrail is a metric you don't try to improve but refuse to harm, such as order value, refund rate or trial-to-paid conversion. Set guardrails before launch, because a variant that lifts clicks while hurting revenue isn't a winner.

  • Ecommerce: average order value, return or refund rate, page load time, checkout errors.
  • SaaS: trial-to-paid rate, activation, support tickets, first-cycle churn.
  • Lead generation: lead-to-opportunity rate and unsubscribe rates on follow-up email.

Guardrails often live outside the testing tool, in product analytics, the CRM or billing. That's why integration with your performance analytics stack, and a sober view of what attribution can and can't prove, matter as much as the editor.

What is each platform best and worst at?

The web-testing tools win on marketer speed, and the product platforms win on statistical depth and flags. None of them is right for every team, so read the "consider another option" line for each.

VWO: best for marketers who want testing and insights together

Best for: marketing and ecommerce teams that want testing plus behavior research from one vendor. Consider another option if: most experiments are in-app product changes.

VWO sells web testing, behavior analytics, personalization and server-side experimentation as separate products, and its documentation describes a Bayesian-based statistics engine. Costs scale with traffic and modules, so a full bundle becomes a significant line item. See the VWO pricing page.

Convert: best focused client-side tool

Best for: agencies and mid-sized teams that want a dedicated web-experimentation tool. Consider another option if: server-side SDKs and native flags are your main workflow.

Convert concentrates on web experiments with audience targeting, analytics integrations and a privacy-focused position. Check its documentation for which statistical methods it offers and how it handles flicker. Plans are on the Convert pricing page.

AB Tasty: best for retail personalization alongside tests

Best for: consumer and retail brands combining experiments with campaign-style personalization. Consider another option if: you need transparent self-serve pricing.

AB Tasty covers web experimentation, personalization and feature management. Its strength is letting marketers run targeted campaigns beside formal tests. It's sold through sales conversations; start at the AB Tasty website.

Optimizely: best for enterprise experimentation programs

Best for: enterprises running many concurrent experiments across web and product, with analysts on staff. Consider another option if: one marketer runs a few tests a quarter.

Optimizely offers web and feature experimentation, and its Stats Engine is designed for sequential monitoring, so teams can check results without the classic peeking problem. Governance and program management are strengths, and contract size matches the enterprise focus. See the Optimizely website.

Statsig: best for engineering-led SaaS experimentation

Best for: SaaS product and engineering teams that want gates, experiments and product metrics in one place. Consider another option if: marketers need a visual editor for landing pages.

Statsig's documentation covers feature gates, experiments, variance-reduction techniques and a warehouse-native deployment option. It's developer-oriented by design. Ask about roadmap and support commitments during evaluation, and check plans on the Statsig pricing page.

PostHog: best for startups that want one product stack

Best for: early-stage teams wanting product analytics, replay, flags and experiments together. Consider another option if: non-developers own marketing-page copy and layout tests.

PostHog puts experiments next to analytics, so guardrail metrics sit beside results. Pricing is usage-based per product with a monthly free allowance, which suits young companies but needs watching as event volume grows. See the PostHog pricing page.

GrowthBook: best for warehouse-native analysis

Best for: data teams that want experiment analysis on their own warehouse data. Consider another option if: you have no warehouse or nobody to write metric definitions in SQL.

GrowthBook is open source and runs self-hosted or as a cloud service, with feature flags, a visual editor, and Bayesian and frequentist analysis according to its documentation. Because metrics come from your warehouse, revenue and retention guardrails are easier to include. See the GrowthBook pricing page.

Intelligems: best for Shopify price and shipping tests

Best for: Shopify brands testing prices, shipping thresholds, discounts and offers. Consider another option if: you aren't on Shopify or mainly want layout tests.

Intelligems focuses on profit-related experiments that generic web tools handle poorly. Price testing raises customer-trust and legal questions, so agree a policy before you run one. See the Intelligems website, and for wider store tooling our list of checkout optimization software.

Can you trust the winner a testing tool declares?

Only as far as the setup allows: a winner label is unreliable if the test was stopped early, under-powered, or showed a mismatch between planned and actual traffic split. Vendor documentation describes statistical safeguards, but no tool can correct a test that should never have been run.

What should you ask before implementation?

Ask about speed impact, bucketing, statistics, guardrails and usage limits, ideally during a trial on a real page. The answers reveal more about day-to-day fit than any feature grid.

  • How does the snippet or SDK affect page speed, and which anti-flicker method is used?
  • How are visitors bucketed across devices and sessions, and how does consent affect it?
  • Which statistical method is used, and is it safe to monitor results continuously?
  • Can guardrail metrics come from our analytics, CRM or warehouse?
  • How are sample ratio mismatches detected and flagged?
  • What counts toward usage limits, and what happens on overage?
  • Who on our team builds variants, checks them and signs off results?

Pair results with qualitative evidence: session replay and heatmap research often explains why a variant won or lost.

What else do teams ask about A/B testing software?

Most follow-up questions are about traffic thresholds, statistics and what replaced Google Optimize. The answers below are deliberately platform-neutral.

What is the minimum traffic for A/B testing?

There is no universal threshold, because it depends on your baseline conversion rate and the lift you need to detect. Run the numbers through a sample-size calculator before choosing a platform, and if a test would take many months, invest in research first.

Should we choose Bayesian or frequentist statistics?

Either works when applied correctly. What matters more is that the method matches how your team monitors and stops tests, and that results are reported with their uncertainty rather than just a winner label.

Do feature flags replace an A/B testing tool?

Not on their own. Flags control who sees a feature, and they only become an experimentation tool when paired with random assignment and metric analysis, which some flag platforms include and others leave to you.

What replaced Google Optimize?

Google retired Optimize in 2023 and pointed users toward third-party testing tools that integrate with GA4. Any platform on this list can fill the gap, depending on your traffic, team and budget.
