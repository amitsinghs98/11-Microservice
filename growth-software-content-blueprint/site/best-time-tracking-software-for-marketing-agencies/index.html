Which time tracking software should a marketing agency choose?

Choose the tool your team will actually use daily, then check it reports time by client, project and billable status. For most agencies that means Harvest, Toggl Track or Everhour; agencies that want time tied to budgets and profitability should look at Productive.

  • Simple timers, budgets and invoicing for small agencies: Harvest.
  • Fast, low-friction tracking with strong reports: Toggl Track.
  • Budget-conscious teams that want to start free: Clockify.
  • Teams that want time inside Asana, ClickUp or similar PM tools: Everhour.
  • Teams that forget to start timers: Timely.
  • Agencies wanting time, budgets and profitability in one platform: Productive.
  • Distributed field or contractor teams that need attendance features: Hubstaff, with care.

What should agency time tracking actually tell you?

Good time data answers three questions: is each retainer within its hours, did each fixed-fee project make money, and how much time goes on internal investment such as pitching and training. It isn't a productivity score, and treating it as one tends to make the data worse.

Retainers need hours burned against an allowance each month. Fixed-fee work needs actual time compared with the estimate behind the quote. Internal investment needs its own projects, so new-business effort doesn't disappear into "admin". Pull those together and you can see true margins per client, which our guide to agency profitability reporting tools covers in depth.

What does each time tracker do well, and where does it fall short?

The simplest trackers get the most consistent data, while the broader platforms give the richest analysis. Weigh adoption against depth.

Harvest: best for simple time, budgets and invoicing

Harvest combines timers, timesheets, project budgets and invoicing, which covers the core agency loop for small teams. It's easy to adopt, but capacity planning sits outside it. Skip it if you need resource scheduling in the same product. Check plans on the Harvest pricing page.

Toggl Track: best for low-friction tracking and reports

Toggl Track is known for fast timers, browser extensions and clear reports, which helps adoption. Billable rates, approvals and team controls depend on the plan. Skip it if you want invoicing and budgets handled in the same place. See Toggl Track's pricing page.

Clockify: best for starting free

Clockify offers a free tier, which makes it easy to trial with the whole team. Check which admin controls, approvals and reports need a paid plan. Skip it if the free plan's limits would force an early upgrade anyway. Confirm what the free plan includes on the Clockify pricing page.

Everhour: best for tracking inside your project tool

Everhour adds timers and budgets inside popular project management tools, so people track time where they already work. It depends on the quality of that integration. Skip it if your PM tool isn't supported. See the Everhour pricing page.

Timely: best for teams that forget timers

Timely captures activity automatically and suggests time entries for people to confirm. That reduces forgotten hours, but the team must be comfortable with activity capture, and the data stays only as good as the confirmations. Skip it if your team will see it as monitoring. Check the Timely pricing page.

Productive: best for joining time to profitability

Productive records time against budgets, so you can see retainer burn and project margin in one system. It's a full agency platform, so you get the most value if you also run projects and billing in it. Skip it if you just want a timer. See the Productive pricing page. Our agency project management roundup covers its delivery side.

Hubstaff: best for distributed teams that need attendance data

Hubstaff markets workforce features such as activity levels and attendance alongside time tracking. Those suit some contractor and field teams, but they're a poor fit for most creative and strategic agency work. Skip it if you don't need attendance records. Check which features are optional on the Hubstaff pricing page.

Does tracking more closely make a team more productive?

There's no basis in the product information reviewed for claiming that closer monitoring improves agency output. What time data does reliably is show where scopes, estimates and retainers are wrong, provided people log it honestly.

What is a proportionate time tracking policy for an agency?

Track enough to answer profitability questions and no more: client, project, work type and billable status. Agree the policy openly, review it quarterly and drop any category nobody uses.

  1. Purpose: state that time data is for pricing, scoping and capacity, not individual performance rankings.
  2. Granularity: log to project and work type, with short notes for anything unusual.
  3. Timing: daily entries, submitted weekly, approved by the account lead.
  4. Internal work: separate projects for new business, training and operations.
  5. Monitoring: no screenshots or activity scoring unless a client contract requires it, and then only with consent.
  6. Review: a monthly look at retainer burn and fixed-fee variance, shared with the team.

Use the data to feed capacity decisions in agency resource planning software and invoices through retainer billing software for service businesses.

Which integrations matter for agency time tracking?

The two integrations that matter most are your project management tool and your invoicing or accounting system. Without them, time gets re-typed and errors creep into client bills.

Check calendar integration for meetings, browser extensions for tools your team uses all day, and an export or API for profitability reporting. If you'll glue tools together yourself, plan the connectors with our guide to workflow automation for agencies.

What mistakes do agencies make with time tracking?

The biggest mistake is introducing tracking without explaining why, which produces guesswork logged on Friday afternoon. The second is building so many categories that nobody picks the right one.

  • Tracking fixed-fee work but never comparing it with the original estimate.
  • Leaving internal work untracked, which hides the true cost of new business.
  • Buying monitoring features you don't need.
  • Not linking time to invoices, so billable hours slip through.

What else do agencies ask about time tracking?

The usual follow-ups are about tracking fixed-fee work, monitoring features and how precise entries need to be. Answers are general guidance; confirm plan features on each vendor's pricing page.

Should an agency track time on fixed-fee projects?

Yes, if you want to know whether the fee was right. Tracking fixed-fee work isn't for billing the client; it shows which scopes you underprice so the next proposal is better.

Do we need screenshots or activity monitoring?

For most marketing agencies, no. Project-level time with honest notes answers the profitability question, and monitoring features can damage trust without making creative or strategic work faster.

How precise do time entries need to be?

Precise enough to answer your business questions, which is usually the client, project and type of work. Rounding to sensible increments is fine for profitability analysis; billing hourly clients may need more care.

Should time tracking live in our project management tool?

If your PM tool's time tracking is good enough for reports and budgets, one tool means fewer missed entries. Use a dedicated tracker if you need stronger reporting, approvals or invoicing than the PM tool offers.

How do we get the team to track time consistently?

Explain what the data is for, keep categories few, and review it with the team rather than about them. Weekly reminders and timesheet approvals help, but shared purpose does more.

What's the verdict?

For most marketing agencies, a simple tracker such as Harvest or Toggl Track, or Everhour inside your PM tool, gets consistent data fastest. Move to Productive when you want time, budgets and profitability in one place.
