Which creative proofing software should an agency choose?

Choose by the asset type your clients review most, then confirm guest reviewers, version comparison and an exportable audit trail. Mixed-asset agencies should start with Ziflow, Filestage, PageProof or ReviewStudio; video and web teams should look at the specialists.

  • Multi-stage workflows across many clients and asset types: Ziflow.
  • Simple, client-friendly review steps with due dates: Filestage.
  • Detail-heavy or sensitive proofing with strict version control: PageProof.
  • Creative studios reviewing designs, video and web in one place: ReviewStudio.
  • Video production and motion work: Frame.io.
  • Feedback on live websites and landing pages: Markup.io.
  • Proofing inside an existing work management tool: Wrike.

Why do agencies need proofing instead of email feedback?

Email feedback splits comments across threads, attaches them to the wrong version and leaves no clear record of final sign-off. Proofing software pins each comment to a spot on the asset, ties it to a version and records the approval decision.

For agencies the stakes are commercial. When a client disputes a change or asks for "one more round", the record of what was approved, by whom and on which version decides whether the work is in scope. That's why version clarity and approval traceability matter more here than in an in-house team.

What does each proofing tool do well, and where does it fall short?

General proofing platforms handle the widest range of client assets; specialists are better at one format. Match the tool to what fills your review queue.

Ziflow: best for multi-stage workflows across clients

Ziflow offers online proofing with staged workflows, reviewer roles, version comparison and integrations, which suits agencies running many clients at once. The depth means more setup. Skip it if you only send a handful of proofs a month. Check plan contents on the Ziflow pricing page.

Filestage: best for simple client review steps

Filestage organises reviews into steps with due dates and lets external reviewers comment on documents, images and video. It's approachable for clients who don't want another tool. Skip it if you need complex conditional routing. See the Filestage pricing page for project and storage limits.

PageProof: best for detail-heavy and sensitive proofing

PageProof emphasises precise annotations, version comparison and security, which suits packaging, print and regulated clients. Casual reviewers may need a short guide. Skip it if your clients mainly review quick social assets. See the PageProof pricing page.

ReviewStudio: best for mixed creative review in one place

ReviewStudio covers review of designs, documents, video and websites with markup and approvals in one workspace. It's a sensible all-rounder for creative studios. Check how it connects to your project tool before committing. Skip it if you already rely on proofing inside your PM platform. See the ReviewStudio pricing page.

Frame.io: best for video review

Frame.io focuses on frame-accurate video review and comments, with close ties to video editing workflows. It's less suited to print proofs and documents. Skip it if video is a small part of your output. See the Frame.io pricing page.

Markup.io: best for feedback on live web pages

Markup.io lets reviewers pin comments to live websites, images and PDFs, which suits web and landing-page work. Check how far its staged approval controls go compared with the general platforms. Skip it if you need formal multi-stage sign-off. See the Markup.io pricing page.

Wrike: best for proofing inside project management

Wrike includes proofing and approvals within its work management platform, so tasks and proofs stay linked. It's less specialised than a dedicated proofing tool. Skip it if you're not already using or considering Wrike for projects; our agency project management roundup compares it with alternatives. See the Wrike pricing page.

What revision policy should sit behind your proofing tool?

Write the policy into the statement of work, then configure the proofing tool to enforce it. Software records the rounds; the contract decides what each round costs.

  1. Included rounds: state how many revision rounds each deliverable includes, and that consolidated feedback per round is expected.
  2. Who approves: name the client reviewers and the final approver for each asset type.
  3. Turnaround: agree how long reviewers have per round, and set due dates in the tool.
  4. What counts as approval: an approve decision recorded in the proofing tool, on a named version.
  5. Extra rounds: priced or estimated before work starts, logged as a change request.
  6. Archive: export approved versions and the audit trail at project close.

Link the policy to your contracts with e-signature software for SOWs, and pass approved files to digital asset management software for storage and reuse.

Do proofing tools really cut revision rounds?

We found no evidence in the product information reviewed that a tool alone reduces rounds; vendor claims of faster approvals weren't verified and aren't repeated here. What the tools do reliably is make each round visible and attributable, which gives you a basis for enforcing scope.

Should proofing live in your client portal?

Proofs can live in a client portal if it offers on-asset annotation and version control; otherwise link from the portal to the proofing tool. Clients should have one obvious place to find what needs their sign-off.

What mistakes do agencies make with creative proofing?

The commonest mistake is letting clients send feedback outside the tool, which breaks the audit trail. Another is inviting every stakeholder to every round, which multiplies conflicting comments.

  • Uploading a new file instead of a new version, so comparison is lost.
  • No named final approver, so sign-off is ambiguous.
  • Ignoring storage limits for large video files.
  • Not exporting the approval record before archiving a project.

What else do agencies ask about creative proofing?

Most questions are about client logins, revision limits and what counts as a valid approval. The answers below are general; confirm guest and audit features on each vendor's official pages.

Do clients need an account to approve a proof?

Some proofing tools let external reviewers comment and approve through a shared link without a full account. Rules and limits differ by vendor and plan, so confirm guest reviewer access before you send the first proof.

How many revision rounds should an agency include?

Set the number in the statement of work and tie it to the fee, rather than picking an industry figure. What matters is that both sides know when a round ends and what happens after the last included round.

Is an approval in a proofing tool legally binding?

A proofing tool records who approved which version and when, which is strong evidence of sign-off. Whether it is binding depends on your contract, so reference the approval process in the agreement.

Can proofing replace a digital asset management system?

No. Proofing manages review and approval of work in progress; a DAM stores and distributes approved, final assets. Many agencies use both, passing approved files from one to the other.

What is the difference between creative proofing and content approval software?

The terms overlap. Creative proofing usually means on-asset review of designs, video and web pages with version control, while content approval can also cover copy, social posts and editorial workflow.
