Which project management software should a marketing agency choose?

Choose an agency-specific platform such as Teamwork.com or Productive if you bill retainers and need time, budgets and client access in one tool. Choose a general work management tool such as Asana, ClickUp, monday.com or Wrike if delivery is your main problem and billing already lives elsewhere.

  • Retainer and client-work agencies that want budgets beside tasks: Teamwork.com.
  • Agencies that want projects, time, budgets and profitability in one system: Productive.
  • Teams that value clean templates and cross-team visibility: Asana.
  • Agencies willing to configure a flexible, feature-dense tool: ClickUp.
  • Teams that think in boards and dashboards and want quick setup: monday.com.
  • Larger creative teams that need proofing and request forms inside PM: Wrike.
  • Small studios that want calm client communication over detailed tracking: Basecamp.

What does an agency need that a generic task board misses?

An agency runs many clients in parallel, so it needs hard separation between client spaces, repeatable templates for recurring deliverables, and a link between hours and what the client pays. A generic board handles tasks well but rarely answers "is this retainer still profitable?" without extra tools.

Think about the five kinds of work your team juggles: client projects with an end date, monthly retainers that repeat, internal tasks such as sales and hiring, creative jobs that need approvals, and reporting that has to go out on time. Software that treats all of them as identical tasks will work, but you'll rebuild the agency layer yourself with tags, custom fields and spreadsheets.

What does each tool do well, and where does it fall short?

The two agency-specific platforms win on retainers and profitability; the general tools win on flexibility and familiarity. None is right for every agency, so read the skip-it-if lines as carefully as the strengths.

Teamwork.com: best for retainer-led client-work agencies

Teamwork.com positions itself around client work rather than generic tasks, so projects, time logging, budgets and client users sit in one product. Strengths are client-facing permissions and a project model that matches how agencies sell. Check which tier includes budgets and profitability reporting, because that decides the real cost for a ten-person team. Skip it if you only need a task list. Check plans on the official Teamwork.com pricing page.

Productive: best for one system from deal to invoice

Productive goes beyond project management into budgets, time, resourcing and agency financial reporting. That breadth is the strength and the risk: you'll get more value if you also move time and billing, and less if you only want boards. Skip it if your finance team is happy with its current invoicing and only delivery needs fixing. See the Productive pricing page for current tiers.

Asana: best for clean templates and visibility across teams

Asana is strong on project templates, timelines, portfolios and rules-based automation, which suits repeatable monthly deliverables. It isn't built around agency billing, so retainer hours and profitability need a separate time or finance tool. Skip it if you want budgets and timesheets in the same screen as tasks. Plan details are on Asana's pricing page.

ClickUp: best for agencies that want one heavily configurable tool

ClickUp combines tasks, docs, goals, time tracking and many views in one workspace, so it can stand in for several apps. The trade-off is governance: without a clear structure for client spaces, it sprawls quickly. Skip it if nobody on your team will own the setup. Check ClickUp's official pricing for plan-gated features.

monday.com: best for board-first teams who want quick wins

monday.com's work management product is approachable, visual and quick to set up with dashboards and automations. Agencies should check how seats are bundled and which features, such as time tracking or guest access, depend on the plan. Skip it if you need deep agency financials. See the monday.com pricing page.

Wrike: best for creative teams that need proofing inside PM

Wrike offers request forms, workload views and proofing that suit agencies with steady creative output. It can feel heavy for clients and occasional users. Skip it if you're under ten people and want something light. Confirm plan contents on Wrike's pricing page.

Basecamp: best for small studios that value calm communication

Basecamp keeps messages, to-dos, files and schedules per project, and clients can be invited into projects. It's lighter on budgets and workload planning than the agency platforms here, so a growing agency may outgrow it. Skip it if you bill hourly against retainers. The current plan structure is on the Basecamp pricing page.

What would a 10-person, 15-client agency actually pay for?

Cost comes down to paid internal seats, how clients are counted and which tier unlocks time, budgets and client permissions. For a ten-person agency with fifteen clients, the client-access rules can matter more than the headline seat price.

Here's a hypothetical model built from stated assumptions, not fetched prices. Assume ten internal users who all need full access, fifteen clients who each need one or two reviewers, and a plan tier that includes time tracking and client permissions.

  1. Internal seats: ten multiplied by the per-seat price on the tier you need, not the entry tier. Check for minimum seat bundles that force you to buy more than ten.
  2. Client users: fifteen to thirty people. If guests are free with limited rights, this adds nothing; if they're paid, it can double the bill. This is the line to check first.
  3. Add-ons: time tracking, resource planning, extra automation runs or storage may be add-ons on some plans.
  4. Billing term: compare monthly and annual billing, and note when the annual price renews.

Run that formula against each shortlisted vendor's official pricing page before a trial. If two tools land close together, pick the one that removes a separate time or billing tool, because that's where real savings appear.

Will a new project tool actually fix missed deadlines?

Software makes missed work visible, but it doesn't fix unclear scopes, too many clients per account manager or a team at full capacity. Based on the product information reviewed, the tools above help most when templates, ownership and weekly review habits are agreed first.

What mistakes do agencies make when buying project management software?

The commonest mistake is buying for features instead of for the delivery process you already run. The second is inviting clients before permissions and naming conventions are settled.

  • Building every client from scratch instead of from a tested template.
  • Letting each account manager invent their own statuses and fields.
  • Ignoring guest-user pricing until the first invoice arrives.
  • Tracking time in a different tool without a reliable link to projects.
  • Skipping a pilot with one or two clients before migrating everyone.

What else do agencies ask about project management software?

Most remaining questions are about client access, seats and when to move off spreadsheets. The short answers follow; confirm plan details on each vendor's official pricing page.

Should clients have logins to our project management tool?

Give clients access only to the views they need, such as approvals, milestones and shared files. Full access to internal boards exposes estimates, internal notes and other clients' names if permissions are set up carelessly.

Do client guests count as paid seats?

It depends on the vendor and the plan. Some tools offer free guest or client users with limited rights, others charge for them, so check the guest rules before you invite fifteen clients.

Is a general work management tool enough for a small agency?

Often, yes, if you mainly need tasks, templates and a calendar. Once you need retainer budgets, billable time and profitability per client in one place, an agency-specific platform usually saves duplicate admin.

When should an agency replace spreadsheets with project management software?

Move when recurring work is missed, when nobody can see who is overloaded, or when you can't tell how many hours a retainer has used. Those are delivery risks, not tidiness problems.

How long does it take to move an agency onto a new project tool?

Plan for templates first, then one or two pilot clients, then the rest of the book. Rushing every client across in one week tends to recreate the old mess in the new tool.
