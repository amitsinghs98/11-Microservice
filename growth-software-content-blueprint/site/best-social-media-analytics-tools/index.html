Which social analytics tool should you choose?

If you already need publishing and an inbox, use the analytics in your management suite; buy a standalone analytics tool only when competitor benchmarks or cross-brand reporting are the main job. Enterprise platforms make sense when many brands, regions and stakeholders need governed reporting.

  • Reporting inside a full management suite: Sprout Social.
  • Enterprise multi-brand, multi-region programs: Emplifi.
  • Competitor benchmarks on a mid-market budget: Rival IQ or Socialinsider.
  • Visual-first brands judging creative performance: Dash Hudson.
  • Small teams and agencies on a tight budget: Metricool.
  • Analytics alongside large-scale listening: Brandwatch.

When are native analytics enough?

Native analytics are enough when one person reports on one brand and a few networks, and nobody asks for competitor context. They stop being enough when you need consistent definitions, longer history, benchmarks or one report across brands.

Each network's own dashboard is the closest thing to a source of truth for that network, and it costs nothing. The problems are practical: definitions differ between networks, exports come in different shapes, history windows vary, and none of them show your competitors. Third-party tools pull data through each network's API and normalize it, which saves hours but introduces its own definitions. Keep native exports as your audit trail.

Which metrics can you compare across networks?

Compare reach-based or view-based rates, link clicks and follower change, and avoid raw totals across networks with different definitions. Write your definitions down once, and apply them to every brand.

  • Reach: unique accounts that saw content. Not every network reports it for every format.
  • Views or impressions: total times content was displayed. Networks count these differently, so compare trends within a network rather than totals across them.
  • Engagement rate: state the formula. Engagements divided by reach, by views or by followers give very different numbers, and tools default to different ones.
  • Link clicks and sessions: clicks reported by the network, reconciled with sessions in web analytics using consistent UTM tags.
  • Follower change: net growth over the period, shown as a rate so large and small brands compare fairly.
  • Share of voice: your mentions or engagement as a share of a defined competitor set, which only benchmarking tools provide.

Campaign tagging is what makes these comparable across brands: tag posts by campaign, content pillar and format inside the tool, so a report can answer "which pillar works best for each brand" rather than listing posts.

What is each tool best and worst at?

Suites win on convenience, benchmarking specialists win on competitor context, and enterprise platforms win on governance across many brands. Each block below names who should skip the tool.

Sprout Social: best when analytics sits inside your management suite

Best for: teams that already publish and engage through Sprout and want cross-profile reports without another login. Skip it if: you only need analytics, because per-seat pricing pays for publishing and inbox features you won't use.

Sprout Social offers cross-network reports, tagging for campaign analysis and presentation-ready exports. Competitor reporting depends on what each network's API allows. If management is the bigger job, start with our social media management software for agencies guide.

Emplifi: best for enterprise multi-brand programs

Best for: large organizations running many brands and regions with central reporting standards. Skip it if: you're a small team, because implementation and contracts reflect its enterprise focus.

Emplifi combines social analytics, benchmarking and content and care modules. Its value is governance: consistent definitions and dashboards across many accounts and markets. Pricing is quote-based.

Rival IQ: best for competitive benchmarking

Best for: marketers who need to show performance against a defined competitor set. Skip it if: you mainly need publishing or inbox features.

Rival IQ is built around comparing your brands with competitors across social channels, with landscapes, alerts and custom reports. Plans are tiered by the number of companies you track, so define the competitor set before you price it.

Socialinsider: best for agency benchmarking and audits

Best for: agencies producing competitor audits and pitch research as well as client reports. Skip it if: you want analytics bundled with scheduling.

Socialinsider focuses on social analytics and benchmarking, with competitor comparisons and exportable reports. Check how profiles are counted across clients and competitors, because tracked competitor profiles usually consume the same allowance.

Dash Hudson: best for visual-first creative analysis

Best for: fashion, beauty, media and consumer brands whose question is which creative works. Skip it if: your reporting is mostly B2B LinkedIn and link clicks.

Dash Hudson emphasizes visual content performance, including creative insights and planning for image- and video-led networks. It is sold through sales conversations.

Metricool: best budget option for small teams

Best for: freelancers, small agencies and small brands that want clear reports across networks at low cost. Skip it if: you need deep benchmarking or enterprise governance.

Metricool combines analytics, scheduling and report exports, with plans tiered by the number of brands. Benchmarking is lighter than in the specialists, so pair it with a dedicated tool if competitor context matters.

Brandwatch: best when analytics and listening must live together

Best for: large teams that need owned-channel analytics beside large-scale conversation data. Skip it if: you only report on your own accounts.

Brandwatch pairs social management and analytics with consumer intelligence and listening. If mentions and reputation are the real question, compare options in our guide to social listening platforms for brand and competitor research.

Do third-party numbers match the native dashboards?

Not always: third-party tools receive data through each network's API, which may expose different metrics, update on a delay or apply different definitions from the native dashboard. Small gaps are normal, but unexplained gaps should be raised with the vendor before they reach a client report.

What should a monthly multi-brand report contain?

A monthly report should open with one summary per brand, then show the same small set of comparable metrics for every brand and network, then explain what changed and what you'll do next. Keeping the structure fixed is what makes month-on-month and brand-to-brand comparison possible.

  1. Header: brand, reporting period, networks included and the metric definitions used.
  2. Summary: three sentences on performance against the brand's goal.
  3. Core metrics by network: reach, views, engagement rate (with formula), link clicks and follower change, each against last month.
  4. Campaign and pillar breakdown: results grouped by your tags, not by individual post.
  5. Top and bottom content: three of each, with a reason for the difference.
  6. Benchmarks: share of voice or engagement against the agreed competitor set.
  7. Web outcomes: sessions and conversions from social, from web analytics using UTM tags.
  8. Data notes: API gaps, metric changes or reconnection issues this month.
  9. Next month: two or three actions, each tied to a finding.

The web-outcomes row depends on disciplined tagging, which our guide to UTM governance tools covers. For connecting social to pipeline, see our small-team attribution guide, and for blending social with other channels, our marketing analytics software shortlist.

How do account and seat limits change the cost?

Your cost is driven by whichever unit the vendor charges for: seats, brands, tracked profiles or tracked companies. Multi-brand teams should count all four before comparing quotes, because competitor profiles often consume the same allowance as your own.

As a hypothetical model, take 6 brands, each with 4 networks and 3 tracked competitors, reported on by 4 people.

  • Owned profiles: 6 × 4 = 24.
  • Competitor profiles: 6 × 3 × 4 = 72, if competitors are tracked on every network.
  • Total tracked profiles: 96, against a plan that may have been sized for 24.
  • Seats: 4 internal users, plus any stakeholders who need logins rather than exports.

Under per-seat pricing, the 72 competitor profiles may cost nothing extra if benchmarks are included. Under per-profile pricing, they can quadruple the plan size. Ask each vendor how competitors, historical backfill and view-only users are counted.

How do platform API changes affect your reports?

Networks change what their APIs expose, so metrics can be renamed, merged or withdrawn and historical comparisons can break mid-year. Treat every report as dependent on API access you don't control.

Recent changes have included moves toward unified view metrics on some networks and tighter rules on which account types third-party tools can connect. Practical safeguards: record metric definitions in the report header, keep monthly native exports as a backup, note any change in a data-notes section, and ask vendors how they communicate API changes and backfill history after reconnection.

What else do teams ask about social media analytics?

Teams usually ask about engagement rate formulas, history and whether a free tool will do. These answers assume a multi-brand setup.

Which engagement rate formula should we use?

Use engagements divided by reach where every network you report on provides reach, and state the formula in every report. Whatever you choose, apply it to every brand and never mix formulas in one comparison.

Can a free tool handle multi-brand reporting?

Native dashboards and free plans work for one or two brands. Beyond that, the time spent merging exports usually costs more than a paid tool.

How far back can social analytics tools report?

It varies by vendor, plan and network, and some tools only collect data from the day you connect an account. Ask about historical backfill before you migrate, and keep your own native exports.

Should social analytics live in our main BI dashboard instead?

If you already have a warehouse and an analyst, piping social data into BI gives the most control. Most teams start with a dedicated tool and export its data later.

What's the verdict?
