Which RFP or proposal tool should a B2B agency choose?

If you respond to formal RFPs, security questionnaires and due-diligence forms every month, a dedicated response platform such as Loopio or Responsive is more likely to pay back in reused, approved answers. If most of your bids are agency-written proposals with an occasional RFP, a proposal tool like PandaDoc, Proposify or Qwilr plus a well-kept answer library is the leaner choice.

  • A searchable, reviewed answer library for frequent RFPs and questionnaires: Loopio.
  • High RFP volume with project management and AI-assisted drafting: Responsive.
  • Large, document-heavy enterprise bids: Upland Qvidian.
  • Proposals, quotes and e-signature in one workflow: PandaDoc.
  • Agency-style proposals with templates and approval controls: Proposify.
  • Interactive, web-based proposals buyers can accept online: Qwilr.

What's the difference between proposal software and an RFP response platform?

Proposal software helps you write and send a document you designed; RFP response software helps you answer someone else's questions, fast and consistently, from a library of approved content. Agencies often need the first and only sometimes need the second.

An agency proposal follows your structure: the problem, your approach, team, timeline, pricing and terms. A formal RFP arrives as the buyer's spreadsheet or portal with dozens or hundreds of questions about capabilities, security, insurance, accessibility and references. The work there is finding the current, approved answer, routing unknowns to the right expert and tracking who's done what before the deadline.

Response platforms are built around that answer library and its governance: owners, review dates, and search across past submissions. If your agency mainly sends its own proposals, our comparison of proposal software for marketing and SEO agencies is the better starting point.

What does each tool do well, and where does it fall short?

Loopio is the most natural first response platform for an agency growing into regular RFPs, with Responsive the alternative when volume and process complexity are higher. For most agencies under a few formal bids a month, a proposal tool is enough.

Loopio: best for a reviewed answer library

Loopio centres on a content library with owners and review cycles, plus project workflows for assigning questions and tracking progress, and it markets AI-assisted answer suggestions. It suits agencies where the same security, capability and team questions recur. Limitations: the library only stays useful if someone curates it, and you'll need to check the official pricing page for how it's sold. Skip it if you answer only a few RFPs a year. See Loopio's pricing page.

Responsive: best for high-volume response management

Responsive (formerly RFPIO) offers response project management, a shared content library and AI-assisted drafting for RFPs, security questionnaires and due-diligence requests. Limitations: its breadth suits dedicated bid teams, so a small agency may spend more time configuring it than using it. Skip it if nobody owns bids full-time. See Responsive's pricing page.

Upland Qvidian: best for large enterprise bids

Qvidian is Upland's proposal and RFP automation product, aimed at organisations producing large, document-heavy responses. Limitations: it's sized for enterprise teams and is likely heavier than an agency needs. Skip it if your bids are under a few dozen pages. See Upland's Qvidian page.

PandaDoc: best for proposals with quotes and e-signature

PandaDoc combines document templates, a content library, pricing tables, approvals and e-signature, and integrates with common CRMs. Limitations: its library is built for proposal sections, not for searching hundreds of past RFP answers. Skip it if most bids arrive as buyer spreadsheets or portals. See PandaDoc pricing.

Proposify: best for agency proposals with approval controls

Proposify focuses on proposal templates, a content library, approval workflows and engagement tracking for sales teams that send their own documents. Limitations: it's not designed for questionnaire-style RFPs. Skip it if you need a governed answer library. See Proposify pricing.

Qwilr: best for interactive web proposals

Qwilr produces web-based proposals with embedded media, interactive pricing and online acceptance. Limitations: formal procurement often insists on its own format, so Qwilr helps with the cover proposal more than the questionnaire. Skip it if procurement dictates the response format. See Qwilr pricing.

How should an agency decide whether to bid?

Score every RFP against a short, fixed set of criteria before anyone writes an answer, and decline anything below your threshold. A consistent matrix protects senior time and makes the decision easy to explain to the team.

Score each criterion from 0 to 2 and set your own cut-off; the scale and threshold below are illustrative assumptions, not a benchmark.

  • Relationship: 0 if you've never spoken to the buyer, 1 if you've had contact, 2 if you helped shape the brief.
  • Fit: 0 if the scope is mostly outside your services, 2 if it's your core offer.
  • Evidence: 0 if you lack relevant case studies or references, 2 if you have close matches you're allowed to share.
  • Capacity: 0 if winning would overload delivery, 2 if the team is available; check your capacity forecast.
  • Commercials: 0 if the budget or terms are unlikely to be profitable, 2 if they clearly are.
  • Effort: 0 if the response is huge relative to contract value, 2 if it's proportionate.
  • Competition: 0 if there's an entrenched incumbent, 2 if it's a genuinely open process.

Record the score and outcome for each bid in your CRM; our guide to CRM software for marketing agencies covers tracking opportunities.

What does a good RFP response workflow look like?

Qualify, plan, draft from the library, review, approve and submit, with a named owner and an internal deadline a few days before the buyer's. Then feed new answers back into the library so the next response is faster.

  1. Qualify: run the bid/no-bid matrix and get a yes from the account lead.
  2. Plan: list every question, assign owners and set internal deadlines.
  3. Draft: pull approved answers, then tailor them to the buyer's context.
  4. Review: subject experts check accuracy; a senior lead checks the win themes.
  5. Approve: pricing and legal sign off before anything leaves the building.
  6. Submit and learn: archive the response, log the result and update stale library answers.

What mistakes do agencies make with RFPs?

The biggest mistake is bidding on everything because the tool makes it feel cheap. The next is letting the answer library go stale, so reused answers describe services, people or policies that have changed.

  • Copying generic answers without tailoring them to the buyer's stated goals.
  • Missing mandatory format or compliance requirements and being disqualified.
  • Leaving pricing to the final evening.
  • Never recording why bids were won or lost.

What's the verdict?

Choose Loopio or Responsive once formal RFPs and questionnaires are a regular, recurring workload, and Qvidian only for enterprise-scale bid teams. For most agencies, PandaDoc, Proposify or Qwilr plus a disciplined answer library and a bid/no-bid matrix is the better-value setup.

Start by scoring your last few RFPs against the matrix, then decide whether volume justifies a dedicated platform. What else do agencies ask about RFP software?

Most follow-ups are about volume thresholds, AI drafting and libraries. These answers are general, so confirm current features with each vendor.

How many RFPs a year justify a dedicated platform?

There's no fixed number. Compare the senior hours you spend finding and rewriting answers with the platform's quoted cost, and include the time needed to build and maintain the library.

Can AI write our RFP answers?

AI features in these tools can draft from your approved library, which speeds up first drafts. A person who knows the account still needs to check accuracy, tailor the answer and approve it.

What should go in an RFP answer library?

Start with company facts, service descriptions, team bios, security and data-handling answers, accessibility statements, insurance details and approved case-study summaries. Give each entry an owner and a review date.

Should we answer security questionnaires with the same tool?

If you receive them regularly, yes, because the same approved security answers recur. Make sure your technical or security lead owns those entries.

Can a shared drive work instead of RFP software?

For a small agency, a well-organised folder of approved answers can work. It breaks down when several people edit answers, nobody knows which version is current, or deadlines overlap.
