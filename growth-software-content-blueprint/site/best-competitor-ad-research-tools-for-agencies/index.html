Best Competitor Ad Research Tools for Agencies

For most agencies, the right competitor ad research tools are a pair: the free platform ad libraries for evidence, plus one paid tool chosen for the job the libraries can't do, whether that's search history (SpyFu, Semrush, iSpionage), shareable swipe files (Foreplay) or market context (Similarweb). Below, we separate libraries from intelligence platforms, compare what matters for client work, and give you a 60-minute review routine.

Which competitor ad research tools should an agency use?

Use the Meta Ad Library and Google Ads Transparency Center on every client, because they're free and come straight from the platforms. Then pick one paid tool to match your service mix: search-heavy agencies get most from SpyFu or Semrush, social and creative agencies from Foreplay, and strategy-led agencies from Similarweb.

  • Live social creative for any client's competitors: Meta Ad Library.
  • Google ads by advertiser, region and format: Google Ads Transparency Center.
  • Saving, tagging and sharing ads with clients and creatives: Foreplay.
  • Paid keywords and ad copy history for search clients: SpyFu.
  • Agencies already running SEO in the same platform: Semrush.
  • Tracking competitors' search ads over time: iSpionage.
  • Pitch decks that need channel-mix and market context: Similarweb.

What's the difference between an ad library and an intelligence platform?

An ad library is the ad platform's own public record of ads, so it's accurate but limited to that platform and usually to creative only. An intelligence platform collects observations across sources and adds history, estimates and analysis, so it's broader but sampled and modelled.

The difference matters most when you present findings. A screenshot from the Meta Ad Library showing an ad has run since a given date is evidence. A third-party chart of a rival's estimated monthly spend is a model, and a client may make budget decisions on it if you don't label it. A third category, swipe-file tools like Foreplay, sits between the two: they don't collect market data themselves so much as help your team save, organise and annotate ads found in the libraries.

What is each tool best for, and when should you skip it?

Every agency should use the two free libraries; the paid choice depends on whether your clients buy search, social or strategy from you. Buying all of them duplicates data you'll rarely look at.

Meta Ad Library: best for live social creative

What it does: Meta's public archive of active ads across its apps, filterable by country, platform and media type. Open it at the Meta Ad Library.

Strengths: real ads with run dates and the variants a brand is running. Limitations: there's no built-in way to organise or annotate findings for a client, and most commercial ads carry no performance data. Skip it if you need history after an ad stops running; a swipe file keeps what the library drops.

Google Ads Transparency Center: best for Google ads by advertiser

What it does: shows ads from verified advertisers across Search, YouTube and Display, by region and date. Search it at the Google Ads Transparency Center.

Strengths: authoritative, free, and good for checking offers and claims before a pitch. Limitations: it won't reveal the search terms an ad appears for. Skip it if your question is which keywords a competitor buys.

Foreplay: best for saving and sharing creative across a team

What it does: a swipe-file tool for saving ads you find in the platform libraries into shared collections for creative teams. Check current features on Foreplay's site.

Strengths: it addresses the agency problem the libraries ignore: keeping saved ads organised and shared across a team. Limitations: it's creative-focused, with no paid search keyword data. Skip it if your clients are mostly search advertisers. To act on the boards, pair it with our list of paid social advertising management platforms.

SpyFu: best for paid keyword and ad copy history

What it does: estimates the Google search keywords a domain buys and keeps historical ad copy, so you can see which headlines and offers survived. See SpyFu's site.

Strengths: quick answers to "what does this rival bid on, and what do they say?" for search clients. Limitations: coverage depends on sampled searches, so local and niche advertisers can be thin. Skip it if you don't sell paid search.

Semrush: best when SEO and PPC research share a platform

What it does: advertising research with estimated paid keywords, ad copy and positions next to the organic data agencies use for SEO. See Semrush's site.

Strengths: one place to compare a rival's paid and organic footprint, useful for "pay or rank?" conversations. Limitations: ad data varies by database and country, and broader ad intelligence may be priced separately. Skip it if you don't also use it for SEO. Our PPC management software guide for agencies covers the execution side.

iSpionage: best for tracking competitor search ads

What it does: tracks competitors' paid keywords and ad copy. Check iSpionage's site for its current monitoring and alert options.

Strengths: a search-focused tool for agencies that report competitor moves regularly. Limitations: coverage varies by market, so check yours before buying. Skip it if you only need occasional one-off lookups.

Similarweb: best for pitch and strategy context

What it does: estimates traffic by channel, including paid search and display, with top paid keywords and referring publishers. See Similarweb's site.

Strengths: it frames ads within the whole acquisition mix, which helps new-business pitches. Limitations: modelled data that tends to be weaker for small sites. Skip it if you need creative, not context.

How do you run a competitor ad review in 60 minutes?

Pick three competitors and one question, spend about 40 minutes collecting dated evidence, and spend the last 20 turning it into two or three test ideas. The time box stops research from becoming a gallery of screenshots with no decision attached.

  1. Minutes 0–5, frame it: agree the question with the account lead, for example "How are rivals positioning price this quarter?", and confirm the three competitors and markets.
  2. Minutes 5–20, social: in the Meta Ad Library (and LinkedIn's or TikTok's library where relevant), filter each competitor to your markets. Save ads that have run longest and ads with many variants, noting start dates.
  3. Minutes 20–30, search: check each competitor in the Google Ads Transparency Center, then use your paid tool for the keywords and copy history behind those ads.
  4. Minutes 30–40, landing pages: open the destination of each saved ad and record offer, headline, proof, form length and pricing visibility. Capture the page, because it may change next week. Our list of landing page builders for paid acquisition helps you test your own versions.
  5. Minutes 40–50, patterns: group what you saw into themes: offers, angles, formats and proof types. Mark which are new since your last review.
  6. Minutes 50–60, hypotheses: write two or three tests in your client's own terms, each with a metric and a stop date, and log them in your monthly review.

Feed the output into the client's regular cycle; our guide on running a monthly paid media account review shows where it fits.

Do clients actually value competitor ad research?

Clients tend to value it when it ends in a decision or test, and to ignore it when it's a folder of competitors' ads. Whether it justifies a paid tool depends on how often you'll run it and how many clients share the cost.

What mistakes do agencies make with competitor ad research?

The common failures are presenting estimates as facts and recommending copies of rivals' ads. Both damage trust when a client acts on them and results disappoint.

  • Unlabelled estimates: mark every third-party figure as an estimate, with tool and date.
  • Copying instead of testing: a rival's ad suits their audience and offer, so translate the idea, don't clone it.
  • One tool per strategist: centralise boards and logins so research survives staff changes.
  • Ignoring licence terms: check what a tool's terms allow you to share in client reports.
  • No baseline: without dated records, you can't tell clients what changed since last month.

What else do agencies ask about competitor ad research?

Agencies mostly ask about client billing, competitor selection and tool overlap. Here are brief answers.

Should we bill clients for competitor ad research?

One common approach is to include a light version in the retainer and scope deeper reviews as a separate project. Either way, state the deliverable, such as a dated findings log and test plan, rather than hours in a tool.

How many competitors should we track per client?

Three to five is a practical range for a regular review. Include at least one indirect competitor that targets the same customers, because they often shape expectations more than the obvious rival.

Do we need both SpyFu and Semrush?

Rarely. Their search ad data overlaps, so pick the one that fits the rest of your work, usually Semrush if you also sell SEO from it.

Can we show competitor ads in client presentations?

Showing public ads as examples in a client-facing analysis is common practice. Check your tool's terms on sharing exports, and avoid republishing competitors' creative publicly.

What's the verdict?

Build every review on the Meta and Google libraries, then add one paid tool that matches your services: SpyFu, Semrush or iSpionage for search, Foreplay for creative, Similarweb for strategy. Run the research on a fixed timebox and end every session with tests, not screenshots.
