Which heatmap and replay tool should you choose?

Start with Microsoft Clarity unless you need a specific paid capability, such as on-page surveys, form analytics or replays filtered by product events. Move to an enterprise platform only when governance, data export and many stakeholders justify a sales-led contract.

  • No budget, solid basics: Microsoft Clarity.
  • Watching behavior and asking visitors why: Hotjar.
  • Funnels and form analytics beside replay: Mouseflow.
  • Simple visual reports for small sites: Crazy Egg.
  • Replay, surveys and live chat in one bundle: Lucky Orange.
  • Replay tied to product events: PostHog.
  • Enterprise governance and journeys: FullStory or Contentsquare.

When do qualitative tools help, and when don't they?

They help once analytics has pointed to a specific page or step with a problem, such as a pricing page with high exits or a mobile checkout step losing people. They don't tell you how big a problem is across all traffic, or whether a fix worked.

The signals they surface are concrete:

  • Click maps: clicks on elements that aren't links, and ignored calls to action.
  • Scroll maps: how far most visitors get, and whether key content sits below that point.
  • Rage and dead clicks: repeated clicks out of frustration, or clicks that do nothing.
  • Funnels: the step where sessions drop, with a path to example recordings.
  • Replays: hesitation, back-and-forth navigation, errors and form struggles in context.

A heatmap of a few hundred visits isn't a measurement of your whole audience. Sizing the problem is the job of your web analytics tool, and proving the fix is the job of a controlled experiment.

What is each tool best for?

Each tool suits a specific team and research habit, and several overlap heavily at the low end. The "skip it if" lines are where the real differences show.

Microsoft Clarity: best free option

Best for: small teams and agencies that want heatmaps and recordings on every site without new subscriptions. Skip it if: you need surveys, collaborative research workflows or vendor support commitments.

According to Microsoft's product information, Clarity provides heatmaps, recordings and automatic insights such as rage clicks, dead clicks, excessive scrolling and quick backs, and it integrates with GA4. Filtering is capable for the price, but research features are thinner than in paid tools. Review masking settings before rollout.

Hotjar: best for marketing and UX teams that want feedback too

Best for: teams that want to watch behavior and ask visitors why in the same tool. Skip it if: you mainly need product analytics or high-volume capture.

Hotjar, part of Contentsquare, pairs heatmaps and recordings with surveys and feedback widgets. Plans are limited by sessions captured, so high-traffic sites see a sample unless they upgrade. Teams outgrowing it can compare options in our guide to Hotjar replacements.

Mouseflow: best for funnels and forms

Best for: conversion specialists who want funnels and form analytics beside replay. Skip it if: you only need occasional heatmaps.

Mouseflow offers heatmaps, recordings, funnels, form analytics and frustration detection. Funnels built from recorded sessions let you jump from a drop-off step straight to examples of it. Agencies should check how plans count websites as well as recordings.

Crazy Egg: best for simple visual reports

Best for: site owners who want clear click and scroll reports plus occasional simple tests. Skip it if: you need deep segmentation or product analytics.

Crazy Egg is known for heatmaps, scroll maps and click-breakdown reports, and also offers recordings, surveys and basic A/B testing. Its built-in testing helps small sites but doesn't replace a dedicated platform.

Lucky Orange: best small-business bundle

Best for: small ecommerce and service businesses that want replay, surveys and live chat together. Skip it if: you already run separate chat and survey tools.

Lucky Orange combines dynamic heatmaps, recordings, surveys, form analytics and live chat. The bundle can replace several small tools, but overlap with existing software reduces its value.

PostHog: best for event-linked replay in SaaS

Best for: SaaS teams that want to filter replays by product events, such as users who started onboarding but never invited a teammate. Skip it if: your users are marketers who want point-and-click heatmap reports.

PostHog includes session replay alongside product analytics, feature flags and experiments, with usage-based pricing and a monthly free allowance per product. Monitor recording volume as usage grows. Product teams should also see our shortlist of session replay tools for SaaS product analytics.

FullStory: best for autocaptured data and export

Best for: larger digital teams that want searchable journeys and interaction data they can export. Skip it if: you need transparent self-serve pricing.

FullStory captures interactions automatically, so you can define events after the fact, and it surfaces frustration signals across journeys. Its documentation describes privacy controls aimed at regulated industries. Pricing is typically contract-based.

Contentsquare: best for enterprise ecommerce and media

Best for: enterprise teams that need zone-level analysis across many templates and markets. Skip it if: you're a small team, because Hotjar, from the same company, is the lighter option.

Contentsquare measures engagement by page zone, maps customer journeys and adds experience analytics for large sites. Implementation effort and contract size reflect that focus.

How do sample limits and privacy controls differ?

Vendors cap usage in different units, including sessions per day, recordings per month, tracked pageviews or events, so a plan that looks generous for one site can be tight across ten client sites. Privacy defaults differ just as much, and you're responsible for configuring them.

Ask whether the tool samples once the cap is reached, stops recording, or bills an overage, because sampling changes what your heatmaps represent. Then check that:

  • Form inputs are masked by default, and you can mask other elements such as account details.
  • Recording starts only after consent where your consent rules require it.
  • Data residency, retention periods and a data processing agreement meet your obligations.
  • Sensitive pages, such as account areas and payment steps, can be excluded entirely.

None of this is legal advice, so confirm requirements with your adviser.

Do teams actually watch the recordings they collect?

Often not, which is why several tools on this list flag rage clicks and dead clicks automatically to shorten the search. Recordings nobody watches are a cost and a privacy liability with no return.

What does a weekly review routine look like?

A focused 60-minute weekly session on one page or funnel step works for most small teams. Rotate the page each week, and revisit a page after any change ships.

  1. Pick one page or step (5 minutes). Choose it from analytics: high traffic with weak conversion, or a recent drop.
  2. Check frustration signals (10 minutes). Review rage clicks, dead clicks and error sessions on that page.
  3. Watch ten to fifteen filtered sessions (25 minutes). Split converters from non-converters, and mobile from desktop.
  4. Compare heatmaps by segment (10 minutes). Look for differences between traffic sources or devices.
  5. Log observations (10 minutes). Record each pattern, how often you saw it and a draft hypothesis in a shared backlog.

How do you turn findings into tested changes?

Size each pattern in analytics, then decide whether to fix it directly or run a controlled test. Judge the outcome on leads or revenue, not clicks.

Check how many visitors reach the affected step before you prioritize it. What else do buyers ask about heatmaps and replay?

The recurring questions concern viewing effort, overlap with product analytics, page speed and personal data. Short answers follow.

How many recordings should we watch to find a problem?

There is no fixed number. A focused batch of well-filtered sessions on one page often reveals repeated patterns, so stop when new sessions stop showing new issues, then size the issue in analytics.

Is session replay the same as product analytics?

No. Replay shows individual visits, while product analytics counts events across all users. Some tools, such as PostHog and FullStory, combine both, which helps you move between the count and the example.

Will a replay script slow my site?

Any third-party script adds some load. Measure page speed before and after installation, and remove tools nobody reviews any more.

Can we use these tools on pages with personal data?

Only with masking, consent and contractual safeguards configured. Excluding sensitive pages entirely is often the simplest option.
