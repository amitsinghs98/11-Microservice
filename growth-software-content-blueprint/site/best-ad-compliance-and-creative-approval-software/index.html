Which ad compliance and approval tool should you choose?

Match the tool to how regulated your claims are. Life-sciences and other heavily regulated brands need formal review workflows with claims and references; financial services teams benefit from AI pre-screening against their rule sets; most other advertisers need disciplined proofing with version history and an audit trail.

  • Pharma, biotech and medical device marketing: Veeva PromoMats.
  • Enterprise brands wanting claims management inside a content operations platform: Aprimo.
  • US financial services and insurance marketers: Saifr.
  • Brands that want AI rule checks next to a brand and asset hub: IntelligenceBank with Red Marker.
  • Creative and agency teams that need fast, auditable proofing: Ziflow or PageProof.
  • Smaller marketing teams that want simple external reviews: Filestage.

What can compliance software do, and what can't it do?

It enforces your internal controls: routing, required reviewers, evidence attached to claims and a record of who approved what. It can't make an ad legal or guarantee that Google, Meta or another platform will approve it, because those decisions belong to your legal advisers, regulators and each platform's policy team.

That distinction matters when you buy. A tool that flags risky phrases is a screening aid, not legal advice, and AI flags miss things. Ad platforms also apply their own policies independently, so an internally approved ad can still be disapproved or limited. Treat the software as a way to make your review process consistent and provable, then keep a qualified human reviewer responsible for the final call.

Most teams also need adjacent controls. Asset licensing and talent usage windows usually sit in a rights tool (see our guide to content rights and usage management), and approved files live in a digital asset management system.

What is each tool best for, and when should you skip it?

The first four tools handle regulated claims and rules; the last three handle structured review of creative. Vendor capabilities below come from official materials, and fit judgments are our editorial assessment.

Veeva PromoMats: best for life-sciences promotional review

What it does: Veeva Vault PromoMats manages promotional content through medical, legal and regulatory (MLR) review, with claims linked to their references and version control.

Strengths: Purpose-built for the review model pharma and medtech marketers already follow.

Limitations: Enterprise procurement and configuration. Skip it if you're outside life sciences.

Aprimo: best for claims management within content operations

What it does: Aprimo combines digital asset management, workflow and review, and positions claims and compliance review for regulated industries within that platform.

Strengths: Good fit when claims, assets and campaign workflows should live in one platform.

Limitations: Broad platform with matching implementation effort. Skip it if you only need ad proofing.

Saifr: best for AI pre-screening in financial services

What it does: SaifrReview is an AI tool that flags potential regulatory risk in marketing content and suggests alternative wording for a reviewer to consider.

Strengths: In our assessment, useful for catching routine issues before content reaches a compliance officer.

Limitations: Oriented to US financial and insurance rules, and it screens rather than approves. Skip it if you're not in financial services.

IntelligenceBank with Red Marker: best for rules plus a brand hub

What it does: IntelligenceBank offers brand, asset and approval tools, and Red Marker's AI compliance checks are now part of the IntelligenceBank business.

Strengths: In our assessment, a fit when you want approved assets, review workflows and compliance checks from one vendor.

Limitations: Confirm how the products are packaged today. Skip it if you already run another DAM you won't replace.

Ziflow: best for enterprise creative proofing

What it does: Ziflow provides online proofing for creative files, with staged reviewer workflows and version comparison.

Strengths: In our assessment, a good fit when you need a clear record of who approved each version.

Limitations: It isn't built around a claims library, so evidence travels as attachments or comments. Skip it if you need regulatory reference management.

PageProof: best for secure, strict review workflows

What it does: PageProof offers proofing with staged review workflows and version comparison, and emphasizes security.

Strengths: Good for agencies whose clients require controlled sign-off.

Limitations: Proofing-focused rather than built for regulatory claims management. Skip it if claims substantiation is the core need.

Filestage: best for simple external review rounds

What it does: Filestage lets internal and external reviewers comment on files and approve versions.

Strengths: Low friction for clients and outside reviewers.

What should a preflight check cover before an ad goes to review?

Every ad should arrive at review with its claims listed, evidence attached, rights confirmed and channel rules checked. That turns review into a check of documented items rather than a hunt for problems.

  1. Claims: list each factual, comparative, price or performance claim, and link it to an approved claim or new evidence.
  2. Evidence: attach the source, its date and the owner who confirmed it.
  3. Expiry: set an end date for time-limited offers, prices and claims backed by dated data.
  4. Disclosures: include required disclaimers and confirm they're legible on the smallest placement.
  5. Rights: confirm licenses for images, music, fonts and talent, including the channels, regions and dates covered.
  6. Channel rules: check each platform's policies for restricted categories such as health, finance, alcohol or political content.
  7. Landing page match: the page repeats the ad's claims and disclosures accurately.
  8. Version and audit: the approved version is the one trafficked, and the approval record is stored.

Approved creative should then flow into your ad creative management platform with its approval status attached, so nobody uploads an older draft.

What mistakes do teams make with ad approvals?

The biggest mistake is approving an ad once and never revisiting it, so claims and offers outlive their evidence. The second is approving creative in email threads, which leaves no reliable record of which version was signed off.

  • Letting variants and resized versions skip review because the "master" was approved.
  • Treating a platform's approval as proof the ad complies with law or industry codes.
  • Keeping the claims library in a spreadsheet nobody updates.
  • Leaving agency and in-house reviewers on different systems with no shared audit trail.

What's the verdict on advertising compliance software?

Regulated brands should start with a tool built for their regime: Veeva PromoMats in life sciences, Saifr in US financial services, and Aprimo or IntelligenceBank where claims sit within wider content operations. Everyone else is usually better served by a proofing tool such as Ziflow, PageProof or Filestage plus a disciplined preflight checklist.

What else do marketers ask about ad compliance tools?

Questions usually concern legal responsibility, platform approvals and whether small teams need this at all. Short answers follow.

Does advertising compliance software guarantee my ads are legal?

No. It structures and records your review process and may flag risks, but legal responsibility stays with your business and its advisers.

Will an internally approved ad pass Google or Meta review?

Not necessarily. Each platform applies its own advertising policies, so an ad your team approved can still be disapproved or restricted.

What is a claims library?

It's a managed list of approved statements, each linked to its supporting evidence, owner and expiry date, which writers reuse instead of drafting new claims.

Do small teams need dedicated compliance software?

Often not. A proofing tool with version history plus a written checklist covers most unregulated advertisers; dedicated compliance platforms make sense when regulators expect documented review.
