Best Ecommerce Merchandising Analytics Tools

Good ecommerce merchandising analytics software tells you which products, categories and promotions make money and which ones stall, not just which pages got traffic. Most brands should combine platform reports with one dedicated merchandising or BI layer that brings in margin and stock, then add search analytics if onsite search drives a large share of revenue.

Which merchandising analytics tool should you choose?

Start with Shopify Analytics or your platform's reports, then add Glew or Daasity if you need margin and inventory across channels, Peel or Lifetimely if repeat purchase drives the business, and Algolia or Searchspring analytics if search behaviour shapes merchandising. Use Inventory Planner when availability, not demand, is what limits sales.

  • Shopify stores that need product and sales reports without another tool: Shopify Analytics.
  • Traffic and funnel context alongside merchandising data: Google Analytics 4.
  • Multichannel brands that want ready-made ecommerce BI: Glew.
  • Brands with a data team building on a warehouse: Daasity.
  • Retention-led brands analysing product cohorts: Peel.
  • Shopify brands that want lifetime value and profit by product: Lifetimely.
  • Catalogs where search terms reveal demand: Algolia.
  • Merchandisers who tune search and category pages together: Searchspring.
  • Brands losing sales to stockouts or tied up in overstock: Inventory Planner.

How is merchandising analytics different from web analytics?

Web analytics explains how visitors arrive and move through the site; merchandising analytics explains how products, categories, prices, promotions and stock perform as a business. You need both, but they answer different questions and usually come from different tools.

A web analytics report can tell you a category page had a high exit rate. A merchandising report tells you whether that's because the top sellers were out of stock, the promoted product has a thin margin, or shoppers searched for a term that returned nothing useful. That second view needs product-level sales, cost of goods, inventory and search data joined together, which most web analytics tools don't hold.

What does each tool do well, and when should you skip it?

The native and BI tools answer "what sold and what did it earn", the cohort tools answer "what brings customers back", and the search and inventory tools answer "what did shoppers want that we failed to show or stock". Pick the layer for your biggest blind spot.

Shopify Analytics: best for a first merchandising view on Shopify

Shopify's built-in reports cover sales by product and variant, inventory and customer reports, and the depth of reporting rises with the plan. It's the right starting point because it uses your order data directly. Skip relying on it alone if you sell through marketplaces or wholesale too, or need margin after discounts, shipping and returns.

Google Analytics 4: best for traffic and funnel context

GA4's ecommerce events show item views, add-to-carts and purchases by item, list and promotion, which helps explain why a product underperforms. Its revenue figures depend on tagging and consent, so don't treat it as the finance source. Skip it as a merchandising tool if you can't maintain item-level tagging.

Glew: best for ready-made multichannel ecommerce BI

Glew pulls store, marketplace, ad and other data into prebuilt product, customer and profit dashboards. It suits a team that wants cross-channel product reporting without building a warehouse model. Skip it if you already have a data team and a warehouse, where a tool like Daasity or custom models give more control.

Daasity: best for brands building on a data warehouse

Daasity provides commerce data pipelines and models on top of a warehouse, with dashboards for merchandising, retention and marketing. Its strength is that the data stays in your warehouse and can be extended. It needs analytics skills. Skip it if nobody on the team writes SQL or owns the warehouse; our list of data warehouse platforms for small analytics teams covers that groundwork.

Peel: best for product and cohort retention analysis

Peel focuses on cohorts, repeat purchase and which first products lead to repeat orders. That makes it useful for deciding which products to feature for acquisition. Skip it if repeat purchase isn't central to your model or you need inventory and margin reporting first.

Lifetimely: best for profit and lifetime value by product

Lifetimely combines lifetime value, cohorts and a profit view that accounts for costs like product cost, shipping and ad spend. It's a fit for Shopify brands that want contribution margin by product without spreadsheets. Skip it if you're not on Shopify or need deep cross-channel inventory reporting.

Algolia: best for search-term and zero-result analysis

Algolia includes analytics on search terms, click-through, conversions and searches with no results, which is direct evidence of unmet demand. It only applies if Algolia runs your search; pricing is usage-based on the official pricing page. Skip it if search is a small share of sessions. Searchspring: best for merchandisers tuning search and categories together

Searchspring pairs search, category merchandising rules and reporting, so merchandisers can see the effect of boosting or burying products. Its analytics are tied to its own search platform. Skip it if you only want reports and don't plan to change search providers.

Inventory Planner: best when availability limits sales

Inventory Planner, part of Sage, forecasts demand and recommends reorders, flagging stockouts and overstock. It answers a different question from the others: whether you'll have the product to sell. Skip it if stock is rarely a constraint for you.

Which metrics belong in a weekly merchandising review?

A weekly review should cover revenue and margin by category, top and bottom sellers, stock cover on best sellers, promotion performance, and search terms with no or poor results. Keep it to one page and assign an owner to every action.

  • Category performance: revenue, units, gross margin after discounts, and change against last week and last year.
  • Product movers: top 20 by revenue and margin, and products with views but few add-to-carts.
  • Availability: weeks of cover on best sellers, stockouts in the past week, and sizes or variants out of stock.
  • Promotions: discounted revenue share, margin given away, and whether promoted items sold through.
  • Search: top terms, zero-result terms, and terms with low click-through.
  • Returns: return rate by product, to catch sizing or quality issues early.
  • New products: sell-through in the first weeks against plan.

Search findings often lead to better ranking rules, and repeated cross-sell patterns can feed product recommendation engines.

Can you trust the numbers these tools show?

The numbers are only as good as the cost, discount and returns data you feed in, and each tool's documentation sets its own refresh schedule. Reconcile one week of revenue and margin against your finance records before anyone acts on the dashboards.

What mistakes do teams make with merchandising analytics?

The common mistakes are treating GA4 revenue as finance-grade, ignoring margin in favour of revenue, and buying a dashboard tool before agreeing on definitions. A second analytics tool doesn't help if nobody acts on the first.

  • Ranking products by revenue alone, so heavily discounted lines look like winners.
  • Leaving cost of goods blank, which makes every profit report wrong.
  • Reviewing category pages without checking stock, then blaming the page layout.
  • Buying several overlapping dashboards; our guide to building a lean ecommerce growth stack covers how to audit overlap.
  • Ignoring personalization effects; if personalization software changes what each shopper sees, product views aren't comparable across segments.

What else do buyers ask about merchandising analytics tools?

The recurring questions are about data sources, freshness and whether GA4 is enough. The answers are short.

Is GA4 enough for merchandising analytics?

Usually not on its own. GA4 shows item-level behaviour, but it doesn't hold cost of goods or inventory, and its revenue depends on tagging and consent.

How fresh does merchandising data need to be?

Daily refreshes are enough for weekly reviews and most buying decisions. Promotions and flash sales benefit from more frequent updates, so check each tool's refresh schedule.

Do I need a data warehouse for merchandising analytics?

Not to start. Prebuilt tools work without one; a warehouse becomes worth it when you need custom models or join many sources.

What data do I need before buying a tool?

Accurate cost of goods per SKU, consistent product categories, and returns linked to orders. Without these, margin and product reports won't be reliable.

Should merchandising and marketing use the same dashboards?

They can share a data source, but merchandisers need product, stock and margin views that marketing dashboards rarely include.

What's the verdict on ecommerce merchandising analytics software?

Use platform reports as the base, add one layer that brings margin and stock into the same view, and add search analytics only if search is a major path to purchase. Agree on metric definitions before you buy.
