How to Audit Your Marketing Stack and Remove Duplicate Subscriptions

A marketing stack audit lists every tool, owner, cost and data flow before anyone cancels anything. This guide gives you the steps, a spreadsheet schema and the safeguards that stop a cut from breaking a workflow.

Why do marketing stacks end up paying twice for the same job?

Stacks grow one urgent purchase at a time, and most modern tools now bundle features that overlap with tools you already own: your CRM sends email, your email platform builds landing pages, and your SEO suite writes content briefs. Nobody owns the whole list, so duplicates survive every renewal.

A marketing stack audit fixes that by putting every tool, owner, cost, workflow and data flow in one sheet before anyone cancels anything. The order matters. Cancelling first and mapping later is how teams lose a form that fed the CRM, or a tracking script that fed the monthly report.

What are the steps in a marketing stack audit?

There are six steps: inventory every subscription, assign an owner to each, map tools to the workflows they serve, measure real usage and overlap, score each tool keep, consolidate or replace, and only then export data and cancel. Budget two to four weeks for a small team, most of it waiting on owners to answer.

1. Build the inventory from money, not memory

Pull twelve months of card statements, expense reports and invoices, then add anything on single sign-on or in your browser's saved logins. Free tools belong on the list too, because they often hold data or run a workflow. Record the renewal date and notice period for each paid line; annual contracts often require notice weeks before renewal.

2. Name one owner per tool

The owner is the person who would notice if it disappeared tomorrow, not the person whose card pays for it. Tools with no owner are your first candidates for review, but not for immediate cancellation: an ownerless tool can still be running a live automation.

3. Map each tool to the workflows it serves

List your core workflows (lead capture, nurture, outbound, content production, reporting, client delivery) and mark which tool does each step. Overlap becomes visible when two tools sit on the same step. This is where an SEO tool stack review often finds two platforms tracking the same keywords.

4. Measure use and overlap

Compare seats paid with seats active in the last 90 days, using each tool's admin or billing screen. For automation platforms, check task or credit usage against the allowance: Zapier and Make both meter usage on their Zapier and Make pricing pages (checked 4 November 2026), so the admin screen shows whether you are paying for headroom you never use.

5. Score keep, consolidate or replace

Score each tool from 1 to 3 on three questions: is it used weekly, is it the only tool doing its job, and would replacing it break a data flow? A tool scoring low on all three is a cancellation candidate. A tool that overlaps but scores high on data flow is a consolidation project, not a quick cut.

6. Export, then cancel

Export data, rebuild dependent workflows, remove scripts and DNS records, and only then cancel. The safeguards section below is the checklist.

What should the audit spreadsheet record?

One row per tool and one column per decision input: owner, cost, renewal, workflow, usage, overlap, data flows and a score. If a field can't be filled, that gap is itself a finding.

What does an audit look like in practice?

In a hypothetical five-person B2B team, the audit typically surfaces one or two clear cancellations and one consolidation project that takes longer than expected. The example below is illustrative, not drawn from a real client.

The team pays for an email platform, a CRM whose paid tier includes marketing email, two connector platforms (one set up by a former contractor), an SEO suite plus a standalone rank tracker, and an AI writing tool alongside a general AI assistant.

  • Rank tracker: duplicates the SEO suite's tracking, has one active user and no data flows out. Cancel at renewal after exporting history.
  • Second connector platform: no owner, but it still runs a webinar-to-CRM sync. Rebuild that one workflow in the main connector, monitor both for two weeks, then cancel.
  • Email platform vs CRM email: genuine overlap, but the email platform holds three years of engagement history and the consent records. A consolidation project with a migration plan, not a quick cut.
  • AI writing tools: overlap depends on whether brand-voice controls are used. Our AI marketing tool stack guide helps decide whether one general assistant is enough.

Where do stack audits go wrong?

They go wrong when a tool is cancelled before its hidden dependencies are found, or when the audit stops at cost and ignores adoption. The visible saving is easy; the broken form or lost history shows up weeks later.

  • Treating unused seats as proof a tool is unused, when one admin runs a critical automation.
  • Cancelling during the notice period and paying for another full year anyway.
  • Forgetting tracking scripts and pixels, which keep firing (and collecting data) after the account closes.
  • Consolidating onto a suite whose higher tier costs more than the two tools it replaced. Check the marketing automation pricing units before you commit.

What safeguards should you run before cancelling a tool?

Export the data, rebuild every dependent workflow, remove scripts and records, revoke access, and confirm the cancellation in writing. Run the checklist below per tool, and keep the evidence in the audit sheet.

  • Data export: contacts with consent status and source, engagement history, assets, reports and any custom fields.
  • Dependencies: search your connector platforms for workflows that reference the tool, and test each replacement.
  • Site and DNS: remove tracking scripts, embedded forms and sending-domain records so nothing points at a dead account.
  • Access: revoke API keys and integrations, and update shared credentials; agencies can use the password management tools for digital agencies shortlist for this.
  • Confirmation: get written confirmation of the cancellation date and of data deletion, and diarise it.

What else do teams ask about marketing stack audits?

These are the practical questions that come up mid-audit. Short answers follow.

How often should you audit a marketing stack?

A full audit once a year and a lighter review every six months is a sensible cadence for a small team. Review an individual tool a month before its renewal date.

Who should run a marketing stack audit?

One person should own the sheet, usually marketing operations or the head of marketing, with finance supplying the spend data. Each tool owner confirms usage and dependencies for their own rows.

Should you cancel a tool nobody seems to use?

Not until you have checked its dependencies. An unused-looking tool can still run a live automation, host a form or hold the only copy of consent records.

Is consolidating onto one suite always cheaper?

No. A suite's higher tier can cost more than the separate tools it replaces, and migration takes time. Compare the full annual cost, including seats and onboarding, before consolidating.

What should you do after the audit?

Turn the decisions into a dated action list tied to renewal dates, and repeat a lighter audit every six months. The second audit takes a fraction of the time because the sheet already exists.

Add a rule that every new tool gets a row, an owner and a renewal date before the first payment. That single habit prevents most of the duplicate spend an audit finds.
