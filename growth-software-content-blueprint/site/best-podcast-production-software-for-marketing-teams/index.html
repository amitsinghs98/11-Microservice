Which podcast production software should a marketing team choose?

For most lean marketing teams, the shortest workable stack is Riverside or Zencastr for remote recording plus Descript for text-based editing, with Auphonic or Adobe Podcast to clean up audio. If one person does everything and wants guidance rather than control, Alitu is simpler; if you want an open-source editor and accept a learning curve, Audacity still works.

Podcast production software splits into recording, editing, cleanup and publishing, and the most common buying mistake is paying for overlapping tools in all four. Decide which step is your bottleneck, buy for that, and only then consider an all-in-one.

  • Remote interviews with video: Riverside
  • Editing by transcript: Descript
  • Browser-based remote recording: Zencastr
  • Quick speech cleanup: Adobe Podcast
  • Automated levelling and loudness: Auphonic
  • Radio-style audio editing: Hindenburg
  • Open-source desktop editing: Audacity
  • Guided, all-in-one for beginners: Alitu

Who is this shortlist for, and what problem does it solve?

It's for marketing teams producing a business podcast without a dedicated audio engineer, typically one or two people handling recording, editing and publishing alongside other work. The problem it solves is turning remote conversations into publishable episodes, transcripts and clips without a studio.

Can AI cleanup really replace a decent microphone?

No: AI speech enhancement can rescue a poor recording, but it can also introduce artefacts, and it doesn't fix crosstalk or a guest who's far from the microphone. The product information we reviewed describes what enhancement does, not how it compares with better source audio, so test it on your own recordings before relying on it.

What does each tool do well, and where does it fall short?

Riverside and Descript together cover most of a marketing team's needs, Zencastr is an alternative recording option, and Adobe Podcast and Auphonic handle cleanup. Hindenburg, Audacity and Alitu suit specific skill levels and budgets.

Riverside: best for remote video interviews

Riverside records each participant locally in the browser and uploads the files, so a weak connection affects the live call more than the final recording. It records separate audio and video tracks and includes editing, transcription and clip tools. Limitations: check usage allowances on the pricing page. Skip it if you only record in one room.

Descript: best for editing by transcript

Descript lets you edit audio and video by editing a text transcript, with filler-word removal, remote recording and clip creation. Strengths: marketers who don't know audio software can make clean cuts quickly. Limitations: fine waveform-level edits are less natural than in a traditional editor. Skip it if you already edit comfortably in a DAW.

Zencastr: best for browser-based remote recording

Zencastr is a browser-based recording studio for remote interviews with separate tracks per guest. Strengths: a straightforward browser-based option. Limitations: compare its video and editing features against Riverside before committing. Skip it if video is the main output.

Adobe Podcast: best for quick speech cleanup

Adobe Podcast offers web-based speech enhancement that reduces noise and echo in voice recordings. Strengths: one-step rescue for a rough track. Limitations: heavy processing can sound unnatural, and you have limited control. Skip it if your source audio is already clean.

Auphonic: best for consistent loudness

Auphonic automates levelling, noise reduction and loudness normalisation, and can produce transcripts. Strengths: makes every episode sound consistent in a listener's playlist. Limitations: it processes rather than edits. Skip it if your editor already handles loudness well.

Hindenburg: best for spoken-word editing habits

Hindenburg is an audio editor designed for radio and podcast producers, with automatic levelling. Strengths: tuned for voice rather than music. Limitations: audio only. Skip it if you publish video episodes.

Audacity: best open-source editor

Audacity is an open-source multitrack audio editor. Strengths: runs locally on your own machine. Limitations: no transcript editing or team collaboration, and a steeper learning curve. Skip it if several people need to edit.

Alitu: best guided option for beginners

Alitu combines recording, automatic cleanup, simple editing and publishing to a host in one guided web app. Strengths: few decisions for a first-time producer. Limitations: less control as your show grows. Skip it if you need multicam video.

What does a minimal production workflow look like?

A minimal workflow has five steps: record each speaker on a separate track, edit by transcript, run loudness and cleanup, export audio plus a transcript, and publish to your host with show notes. One person can run it weekly if each step has one tool and one owner.

  1. Prepare: send guests a checklist covering headset, quiet room and browser.
  2. Record: use a platform that captures locally and separately per speaker.
  3. Edit: cut by transcript; keep a list of standard edits such as intro, outro and sponsor slot.
  4. Clean and level: apply enhancement and loudness normalisation once, at the end.
  5. Publish and reuse: upload to your host, attach the transcript, and cut clips for social and email.

What mistakes do marketing teams make when buying production tools?

The usual mistakes are buying overlapping subscriptions, recording everyone on one mixed track, and over-processing audio until voices sound artificial. Each is avoidable with a short checklist before you subscribe.

  • Overlap: recording platforms increasingly include editing and transcription, so check what you'd duplicate.
  • Single-track recording: makes it hard to fix one speaker without affecting the other.
  • Usage surprises: recording hours, transcription minutes and processing credits run out fastest in busy months.
  • No transcript plan: transcripts feed show notes, blog posts and accessibility; decide who reviews them.

What else do marketing teams ask about podcast production software?

Questions usually concern tool overlap, video and whether free tools are enough. The answers below assume a small team with no dedicated audio engineer.

Do we need separate recording and editing tools?

Not always. Some recording platforms now include editing and transcription, which may be enough for a simple interview show, while a dedicated editor gives you more control for complex episodes.

Should we record video for a B2B podcast?

Record it if you'll use it for clips, YouTube or sales enablement, since it's hard to add later. If you won't publish video, audio-only recording keeps files and editing simpler.

Is free podcast production software good enough?

An open-source editor such as Audacity can produce a professional result if someone has the skills and time. Paid tools mainly save time through transcript editing, automation and collaboration.

Who should own podcast production in a small team?

Name one owner for the workflow, even if editing is outsourced. That person keeps the checklist, file naming and publishing schedule consistent from episode to episode.

What's the verdict on podcast production software?

Riverside plus Descript is the most efficient pairing for a lean marketing team producing interviews, with Auphonic or Adobe Podcast for cleanup; Zencastr is an alternative recorder, Alitu the guided option and Audacity the open-source one. Buy for your bottleneck step first and avoid paying twice for the same feature.
